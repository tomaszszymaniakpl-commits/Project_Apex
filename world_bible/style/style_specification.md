# Style Specification - PROJECT APEX

*Based on analysis of Take A Stand & Out of the Woods + advanced thriller techniques*

---

## NARRATION
- **POV:** Third Person Limited, Deep POV
- **Primary POVs:** Nick Wilde, Judy Hopps (majority of chapters)
- **Secondary POVs (allowed):** Skye Winters, Jack Savage (when Nick/Judy are absent or for dramatic irony)
- **Antagonist POV (rare):** Magnus Volt interludes for tension/dramatic irony. Keep brief, mysterious. Never reveal his full plan to the reader before the protagonists discover it
- **Deep POV technique:** Narrator merges with the character. No filter words ("he saw", "she felt", "he thought"). Instead, render sensory experience directly: "The alley reeked of wet concrete and old fear" not "Nick smelled the alley"
- **Tense:** Past tense
- **POV switching:** Between chapters or clear section breaks (***) only. Never mid-scene
- **Information asymmetry:** Controlled release for mystery, dramatic irony for emotional impact
- **Language:** English (target: older teens/adults, mature themes acceptable)

---

## SYNTAX & RHYTHM

### Sentence Structure
- **Average length:** 20-21 words per sentence
- **Distribution:** 
  - Short (1-10 words): 15-18% - use for impact, emotional beats
  - Medium (11-20 words): 40-45% - standard narrative flow  
  - Long (21+ words): 40-45% - complex scenes, atmosphere building

### Complexity Guidelines
- **Simple:complex ratio:** ~35:65 (favor complex for sophistication)
- **Punctuation:** 
  - Semicolons (;) frequently for compound thoughts
  - Dashes (—) rarely - prefer semicolons
  - Fragments acceptable in dialogue ("My bad.", "Deal.")
  - Rhetorical questions in internal monologue

### Dynamic Pacing
- **Action scenes:** Shorter sentences, fragments OK, present tense verbs for momentum
- **Emotional scenes:** Longer, complex sentences with introspection
- **Dialogue scenes:** Mix of short ripostes and occasional monologs

## VOCABULARY

### Language Levels
- **Casual:** 35-45% (dialogue, internal thoughts)
- **Neutral:** 45-50% (standard narrative)  
- **Literary:** 10-15% (atmospheric moments, key scenes)

### ZOOTOPIA-SPECIFIC TERMS
**Must Use:**
- **"Inters"** - inter-species couples (not "interspecies relationships")
- **"gentlemammal"** - gentleman equivalent
- **Brand names:** "Snarlbucks" (coffee), "Furbook" (social media)

### ANIMAL ANATOMY (Critical)
- **Ratio:** 85% animal terms, 15% human terms
- **Always use:** "paws" not "hands", "muzzle" not "mouth/face"  
- **Consistent:** "ears", "tail", "fur" throughout
- **Only use "hands":** For specific medical/technical contexts

### ENHANCED SENSORY LANGUAGE
- **Emotional/relational scenes:** Scent > Sound > Visual > Touch (biology reveals hidden feelings)
- **Action/location scenes:** Visual > Sound > Scent > Touch (situational awareness first)
- **Scent examples:** "fox musk", "predator scent", "fear-smell", "ozone/musk of cold terror"
- **Sound examples:** Enhanced hearing details, species-specific vocalizations
- **Visual:** Species-appropriate (fox night vision, rabbit wide-angle peripheral)

## DIALOGUE & NARRATOR BALANCE

### Core Principle: Dialogue Leads, Narrator Supports
- **Default ratio: ~50% dialogue, 50% narrator** (Balanced style)
- Dialogue is the PRIMARY vehicle for: revealing plot, showing relationships, exposing character, building tension
- Narrator handles ONLY what dialogue cannot: location setup, physical action, sensory detail, time transitions
- **The reader should learn the story from what characters SAY and HOW they say it**

### When Narrator Takes More Space (exceptions):
- **Scene openings:** Brief establishing shot (location, atmosphere, time) before dialogue begins — max 2-3 sentences
- **Pure action sequences:** No one talks during a fistfight — narrator drives (see ACTION scene type)
- **Emotional peaks without words:** Silence, a look, a gesture that dialogue would cheapen (use sparingly)
- **Time montages:** "Three months later" transitions, multi-day passages (M1.3)
- **Sensory immersion moments:** A specific smell, a shift in light — one beat, then back to dialogue

### When Dialogue Dominates (push to 60-70% dialogue):
- Interrogations, arguments, confrontations
- Planning scenes (M3.1 rooftop)
- Character reveals and confessions (M2.1 Bogo, M2.9 Jack)
- Banter and partnership chemistry (M1.0, M1.1)

### Anti-Patterns (AVOID):
- Narrator explaining what a character just said ("He was angry, she could tell")
- Narrator summarizing a conversation instead of showing it
- Long narrator paragraphs between dialogue lines — kills rhythm
- Characters saying nothing while narrator describes their feelings for half a page

### Format Distribution (within dialogue)
- **Said tags:** 60-65%
- **Action tags:** 25-30%
- **Bare dialogue:** 10-15%

### Eyes as Emotional Anchors (Nick & Judy)
- **Nick's emerald green eyes:** Vivid, expressive — the feature Judy loves most. His voice lies; his eyes don't. Fear reads as cold ozone-green, warmth as deep forest-green. When he's masking, his eyes go flat. Judy is the only one who can read the difference. Use eye descriptions at key emotional beats (M1.1 almost-kiss, M1.6 "Predators don't change," Nick Interlude II, M3.1 reunion)
- **Judy's violet/lavender eyes:** Large, impossible to hide emotion. Nick calls them his compass. When she's furious they darken to deep purple; when hopeful, they lighten to pale lavender. These are the last thing Nick sees before the explosion — and the first thing he looks for when they reunite
- **The Pattern:** In intimate/emotionally charged scenes, characters should reference each other's eyes as a shorthand for trust, love, or loss. "Those eyes" becomes a recurring motif — what they see in each other vs. what the world sees

## NARRATIVE TECHNIQUES

### Scene Architecture
- **In Medias Res:** Start scenes mid-action, not from waking up. Open with tension, explain context through flashback or dialogue after the hook
- **Scene/Sequel pattern:** Every action scene must be followed by a processing scene (characters assess losses, plan next steps, build relationships). This is where Nick/Judy intimacy develops
- **Cliffhangers:** End every scene at a decision point or revelation. Never end a scene with characters going to sleep (unless someone is breaking in)

### Information Management
- **Flashbacks:** Clear time markers or smooth italics transition
- **Internal monologue:** Italics for direct thoughts, single quotes occasionally
- **Foreshadowing (Chekhov's Gun):** Every planted detail must pay off. If Nick has a broken lighter in Chapter 1, it saves his life in Chapter 20. Track plants/payoffs explicitly
- **Mystery elements:** Clues through enhanced senses, species advantages
- **Gap Manipulation:** Reader should know less than the antagonist, roughly same as protagonist (or slightly more for dramatic irony/dread)

### Twist Engineering
- **The Inevitable Surprise:** After a twist, the reader should be able to re-read Chapter 1 and see clues they missed. Twists must be surprising but retroactively logical
- **Recontextualization:** The most powerful twist type. Don't change facts—change the interpretation of facts. (Example: Nick pushes Judy away not because he doesn't love her, but because his presence makes her a target. Aggression = ultimate protection)
- **Red Herrings:** Introduce high-weight clues that suggest a wrong direction. The reader assumes X, but the truth is Y. Must be internally consistent—never cheat

### Action Scene Choreography
- **Spatial clarity first:** Establish the arena (room layout, exits, obstacles) in 2-3 sentences before combat begins
- **Size-based tactics:** Small characters (Judy, Jack) use agility, environment, momentum. Large characters (McHorn, Ragnar) use force, reach, endurance. Never have a rabbit win a straight power contest against a bear
- **Sentence rhythm:** Short bursts during exchanges, slightly longer between beats. "She rolled left. Glass shattered where she'd been. Up—knee to the gut—pivot—"
- **Injuries matter:** Track wounds across scenes. A gunshot in Chapter 8 still hurts in Chapter 10. No miraculous recoveries
- **Clarity over poetry:** During fast action, prioritize reader understanding of who-does-what-to-whom over beautiful prose. Save lyrical language for the aftermath

### Flashback Rules
- **Entry:** Trigger with a sensory detail (a scent, a sound) that connects present to past. Smooth transition, not jarring
- **Format:** Italics for short flashbacks (under 500 words). Section break (***) + past tense header for longer ones
- **Exit:** Return to present triggered by interruption (someone calling the character's name, a loud noise, physical contact)
- **Frequency:** Max 1-2 per chapter. Overuse kills forward momentum
- **Purpose:** Every flashback must reveal information that changes the reader's understanding of the present scene. Never use flashbacks for decoration

### Scene Transitions
- **Prefer:** Dramatic cuts between scenes
- **Occasional:** Smooth flow for emotional continuity
- **Chapter breaks:** Natural POV switches, tension peaks

## EMOTIONAL ENGINEERING

### Show, Don't Tell 2.0
- **Never label emotions.** Don't write "Judy was terrified." Write the features: "Judy's ears flattened against her skull, her nose twitching at a rate she couldn't control. She felt the blood drain from her paws, leaving behind a tingling numbness"
- **Let the reader infer.** The reader decoding emotion from behavior = stronger immersion than being told
- **Trust-the-reader default.** In emotional beats, prefer evidence (gesture/scent/pause/action choice) over interpretation. If an image is clear, do not explain it again.
- **One interpretation max.** If needed, allow at most one short interpretive sentence after 2-4 concrete signals; then move on to action/dialogue.
- **Conflict scenes rule.** During arguments/confrontations, cut "what she felt" summaries first; keep body language and spoken lines.
- **Revision check:** For each emotional paragraph ask: "Can a reader infer this without narrator diagnosis?" If yes, delete the diagnosis.

### Subtext (Hemingway's Iceberg)
- **Characters never say what they feel directly.** That's "on-the-nose dialogue" and it kills tension
- Bad: Nick: "Judy, I'm afraid of losing you"
- Good: Nick (checking her bulletproof vest for the second time): "If you get this uniform dirty, Carrots, don't expect me to help you wash it. Be careful"
- The reader must decode the care hidden inside the gruff behavior

### Vulnerability Through Biology
- **Ears and tail are involuntary emotion displays.** If Nick says "I'm fine" but his tail is tucked and trembling—the reader's heart breaks from the contradiction between declaration and biology
- **Instinct override:** When Judy (strong cop) freezes in tonic immobility under extreme fear—her helplessness hits harder than if she simply cried
- **Scent betrayal:** Characters can't hide their emotional scent from other animals. Fear smells different from anger. Use this as an involuntary truth detector

### Hurt/Comfort Balance
- **Hurt phase:** Push the character to their breaking point (physical or psychological)
- **Comfort phase:** The partner becomes the safe harbor
- **Critical rule:** If you only hurt without comfort, the reader desensitizes (torture porn). The higher the happiness peak you build, the more devastating the fall into the thriller abyss
- **The Cost of Choice:** Build dilemmas with no good outcome (zero-sum). Judy must choose: save Nick and let the killer escape, or sacrifice Nick for the "greater good." This generates maximum tension

### Pacing as Heartbeat
- **High stress:** Short sentences. No commas. Action verbs. "He ran. Breath burning. Wall. Dead end."
- **Emotional deep dive:** Long, flowing compound sentences. More sensory descriptions (smell of wet fur, cold of rain). Let the reader settle into the character's pain
- **Match sentence rhythm to the character's pulse.** If they're panicking, the prose panics too

### Motifs and Symbols
- **Rain/water:** Cleansing but also evidence destruction. Ambiguous symbol
- **Species size contrast:** Judy looking UP at a suspect is not just physical—it's a power imbalance symbol
- **The Analog Chip:** John Wilde's legacy, justice delayed, father-son connection
- **Predator/prey dynamics:** Use biological tension as metaphor for social power structures

## SCENE TYPE CALIBRATION

Every scene must be classified before writing. The type determines pacing, density, and prose style.

### Type: EMOTIONAL (prolog, almost-kiss, reunion, death, loss)
- **Ratio description:action = 70:30**
- Slow tempo. Rich sensory detail. Metaphors allowed (max 1 per paragraph)
- Longer sentences, breathing rhythm. Space for silence
- Internal experience rendered through body/scent/eyes, not narrator explanation
- Eye motif (emerald/lavender) used freely at key beats

### Type: PLOT (interrogations, planning, dialogue scenes, discoveries)
- **Ratio description:action = 40:60**
- Dialogue dominates. Descriptions are functional only (room, body positions, what we see)
- Narrator does NOT explain emotions — characters show through body language
- Medium tempo. Each scene must have an arc (emotion at start ≠ emotion at end)

### Type: ACTION (raids, chases, fights, escapes, explosions)
- **Ratio description:action = 20:80**
- Short sentences. No metaphors. Pure cinematography
- Every sentence = one camera movement
- Zero internal monologue DURING action. Emotions only BEFORE and AFTER the sequence
- Dialogue: max 1-2 words during combat ("Go!" / "Cover me!" / "DOWN!")
- Describe what happens, not how it feels. Let the reader supply the adrenaline

### Scenes with TYPE TRANSITIONS
Some milestones shift type mid-scene. Mark the transition explicitly:
- M1.0: ACTION (raid) → PLOT (banter + component discovery)
- M1.6: ACTION (docks confrontation) → EMOTIONAL ("Predators don't change") → ACTION (explosion) → EMOTIONAL (Judy in the fire)
- M2.5: ACTION (motorcycle chase) → EMOTIONAL (Judy alone in the tunnels)
- M3.6: ACTION (three simultaneous fights) → EMOTIONAL (Skye crawling to console)

### Universal Rules (ALL scene types)
- **NEVER explain emotions via narrator** — show through body, eyes, scent, silence
- **NEVER describe absence of action** ("She didn't scream. She didn't run.") — describe what she DID
- **Max 1 simile/metaphor per paragraph.** Zero in ACTION scenes
- **Trust the images.** The reader is an adult. If the image is strong, the narrator adds nothing by explaining it
- **No "on-the-nose" dialogue.** Characters never say what they feel directly. Subtext always

## WORKFLOW: MILESTONE → SCENES → PROSE

### Step 1: Scene Breakdown (per milestone)
For each scene, define:
- **Number & title**
- **Scene type:** EMOTIONAL / PLOT / ACTION (+ transitions if mixed)
- **POV** (whose perspective)
- **Location & time**
- **What happens** (3-5 bullets)
- **Reader emotion** (what should they feel at the end)
- **Foreshadowing** (what we seed for later)
- **Tempo** (fast/medium/slow — estimated page count)

### Step 2: Beat Sheet (per scene)
For each beat within the scene:
- **Action** (what happens)
- **Key dialogue** (2-3 lines in English, with action tags)
- **Sensory details** (sight/sound/smell/touch/taste — species-specific)
- **Character emotion & reader emotion**
- **Eye motif** (where applicable)

Adapt beat density to scene type:
- EMOTIONAL: fewer beats, more space between them
- PLOT: moderate beats, dialogue-driven
- ACTION: rapid beats, one per sentence

### Step 3: Prose (per scene)
Convert beat sheet to final prose. Rules:
- Follow the scene type calibration above
- Maintain voice patterns from character profiles
- Check: does every paragraph earn its place? If you can cut it without losing story, cut it
- Check: is the narrator explaining something the image already shows? Delete the explanation
- Final pass: read aloud. If you stumble, the sentence is too long or awkward

### Step 4: Milestone Review (after all scenes written)
Verify:
1. Emotional arc of the full milestone (start emotion ≠ end emotion)
2. What the reader knows vs. doesn't know after this milestone
3. Foreshadowing planted (list)
4. Connections to other milestones
5. Page/word count estimate
6. Any needed changes to the master story plan
