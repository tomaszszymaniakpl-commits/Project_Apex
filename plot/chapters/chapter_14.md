# Chapter 14: Collateral Damage

The ride back to Precinct One was suffocatingly quiet. 

Nobody spoke. There was nothing left to say. They had played their best hand, broken every rule in the book, and still ended up chasing dust in a storm drain while the real prize slipped through their fingers. Apex wasn't just a step ahead of them; they were playing an entirely different game. 

By the time they pulled into the underground garage, the adrenaline had completely burned out of Judy’s system, leaving behind a deep, aching exhaustion and a throbbing pain in her throat where Skye had pinned her. 

"Wolford, McHorn, go log the vehicles," Fangmeyer ordered as they stepped out of the cruiser. She didn't wait for their acknowledgment. She put a heavy, gentle paw on Judy’s shoulder. "Come on, Judes. Ambulatory."

Judy didn't have the energy to argue. 

The precinct infirmary was quiet at this hour, smelling sharply of antiseptic and rubbing alcohol. A bored EMT cleaned the scrapes on Judy’s face and applied a soothing gel to the dark, purple bruising blooming across her windpipe before leaving them alone in the small examination room. 

Judy sat on the edge of the padded table, staring at her paws. 

Fangmeyer leaned against the doorframe, her arms crossed, watching her. "You look like hell."

"I feel like it," Judy rasped. Her voice sounded like it was being dragged over gravel. 

"She had military CQC training, Judy. You held your own."

"She put me on the ground in three seconds, Nadine. She wasn't even trying." Judy looked up, the frustration finally leaking out. "How do we beat them? They have the money, they have the tech, and they have the muscle. We're just... local cops."

Fangmeyer sighed, her tail flicking slowly. She walked over and sat on the rolling stool next to the table. "We beat them by not breaking. We go home, we sleep, and tomorrow we start pulling the thread on those serial numbers Wolford grabbed." 

The tiger offered a small, tired smile. "Besides, I promised my sister I'd come over for Sunday dinner. If I get myself killed by a terrorist fox before then, she'll resurrect me just to yell at me for ruining her roast."

Judy managed a weak smile. "I didn't know you had a sister."

"Older sister. Three cubs. They treat me like a jungle gym every time I walk through the door." Fangmeyer’s eyes softened, a rare look of genuine warmth crossing her usually stoic face. "She worries. Thinks I work too much. I told her this case was almost wrapped up."

"You lied to her."

"I'm a cop. Lying to family about how dangerous the job is practically in the job description."

Before Judy could reply, the heavy double doors at the end of the hallway swung open. 

Both officers looked out of the infirmary. 

A tall, impeccably dressed elk with a rack of polished antlers was marching down the corridor, flanked by two nervous-looking aides. His face was set in a furious scowl. He didn't look left or right as he stormed past the infirmary and headed straight for the private elevators. 

"Commissioner Antler," Judy murmured, her brow furrowing. "What's he doing here at this hour?"

"When the Police Commissioner walks out of the Chief's office looking like he wants to murder someone, it never means anything good for us," Fangmeyer said grimly. 

A moment later, Chief Bogo appeared in the hallway. He watched the elevator doors close behind the Commissioner, his massive shoulders slumped. He looked older than Judy had ever seen him. 

Bogo turned and walked slowly toward the infirmary. He stopped in the doorway, looking at Judy’s bruised neck, then at Fangmeyer. 

He didn't ask what happened. He didn't ask about the clash with ZIA. 

"Take the night off, Hopps," Bogo said. His voice was heavy, drained of its usual thunder. 

"Chief, I can still—"

"I said take the night off," Bogo interrupted, though there was no anger in it. Just a profound weariness. He shifted his gaze to the tiger. "Nadine. Drive her home. Make sure she gets inside."

The weight of what he wasn't saying hung heavily in the sterile air of the room. The politicians were circling. The ZIA was closing the net. The ZPD was being cut out of the equation. 

"Yes, Chief," Fangmeyer said quietly. 

Bogo nodded once, turned, and walked back down the empty hallway. 

Fangmeyer stood up and tossed Judy her jacket. "Come on. Let's get you out of here."

***

Fangmeyer’s personal car was a ten-year-old sedan that smelled faintly of vanilla air freshener and shedding fur. It was a stark contrast to the sterile, ozone-scented cruisers of the precinct. 

The drive to Judy’s apartment was quiet, the streets mostly empty in the late hours of the night. 

"So, Sunday dinner," Judy said, leaning her head against the cool glass of the passenger window. She felt completely drained, but the conversation was keeping her grounded. "What's the occasion? Just family time?"

"My oldest nephew is turning six," Fangmeyer said, keeping her eyes on the road. A genuine smile touched the corners of her mouth. "He wants a superhero-themed party. My sister expects me to show up in full uniform so he can show off his 'real cop' aunt to his friends."

"Are you going to do it?"

"Are you kidding? I bought a cheap plastic badge and a cape just for him. If I show up in my actual gear, I'll be answering questions about arrest quotas all afternoon." 

Judy let out a small, tired laugh. It felt good. It felt normal. "He sounds sweet."

"He's a terror," Fangmeyer corrected affectionately. "But yeah. He's a good kid."

Fangmeyer pulled the sedan up to the curb in front of Judy’s apartment building. The streetlights cast long, yellow pools of light across the pavement. The neighborhood was dead silent. 

Fangmeyer killed the engine and popped the trunk lock. "Go get some sleep, Judes. We'll figure out the next move tomorrow."

"Thanks, Nadine," Judy said, opening her door. She stepped out onto the sidewalk, the cool night air biting at her bruised throat. "And... thanks for having my back today. In the tunnel."

Fangmeyer stepped out of the driver's side, resting her arms on the roof of the car. "Always."

Judy turned to walk toward the glass doors of her building. 

She didn't see it. But Fangmeyer did. 

The tiger’s ears snapped forward, swiveling toward the roof of the four-story brownstone across the street. Her pupils dilated instantly. She caught the faintest, momentary glint of ambient streetlight reflecting off a glass optic lens near the chimney stack, followed immediately by the sharp, compressed *thwip* of a pneumatic firing mechanism. 

"Judy, down!" Fangmeyer roared. 

The tiger didn't draw her weapon. She didn't seek cover. She launched her massive body over the hood of the car, tackling Judy to the pavement just as the projectile crossed the street. 

Judy hit the concrete hard, Fangmeyer’s heavy frame landing directly on top of her, crushing the breath from her lungs. 

"Nadine!" Judy gasped, struggling under the weight. "What—"

Fangmeyer didn't answer. The tiger let out a sharp, choked gasp. 

Judy managed to push herself up onto her elbows. Fangmeyer rolled off her, clutching at the back of her neck. 

There was no blood. No gunshot wound. Just a tiny, metallic dart with a clear plastic fletching, buried deep in the thick fur just below the collar of Fangmeyer’s shirt. 

"Sniper," Fangmeyer managed to say, her voice suddenly slurring. She reached up with a trembling paw to pull the dart out, but her claws wouldn't close. They were locked open, rigid and shaking. 

"Nadine?" Judy scrambled to her knees, her heart slamming into overdrive. She looked at the roof across the street, but there was nothing there. Just empty shadows. 

She turned back to her friend. 

Fangmeyer’s eyes were wide, the pupils blown completely out. She tried to speak, but only a wet, gagging sound came out. Her jaw locked shut with a terrifying *crack*. 

"Nadine! Hey, look at me!" Judy grabbed the tiger’s shoulders. The muscles beneath the fur felt like solid stone. 

Suddenly, Fangmeyer’s back arched violently. Her entire body seized in a brutal, full-body convulsion. Her heels drummed against the pavement, and white foam began to bubble at the corners of her locked jaw. 

It wasn't a bullet. It was a neurotoxin. 

"No, no, no, no," Judy chanted, pure, unfiltered panic shredding her police training. She fumbled wildly for the radio on her belt, her paws slipping on the plastic. She ripped it free and mashed the transmit button. "Officer down! Officer down! 10-33 at my residence! I need a bus, forthwith! Suspect used a chemical agent! She's seizing!"

*“Copy, Car 27, rolling EMS and backup to your location,”* dispatch responded instantly. 

Judy dropped the radio and grabbed Fangmeyer, trying to roll the massive tiger onto her side to keep her from choking on the foam. It was like trying to move a marble statue. Fangmeyer’s claws scraped uselessly against the concrete, her eyes rolling back in her head. 

"Stay with me, Nadine!" Judy screamed, tears blurring her vision as she desperately tried to force the tiger's jaw open to clear her airway. "Stay with me! Think about Sunday! Think about the party!"

The street remained dead silent, save for the horrifying sound of Fangmeyer suffocating on dry land. The sniper was gone. The white fox's promise had been kept. 

*Someone you love is going to die.*

Judy knelt in the street, her hands slick with her friend's saliva, screaming into the empty night.

***

The ambulance was gone, but the flashing red and blue lights of the patrol cars still painted the street in harsh, rhythmic strobes. 

Judy sat on the curb next to Fangmeyer’s sedan. She was staring at a dark, wet patch on the asphalt. It was a mixture of saliva and the chemical foam that had bubbled from her friend's mouth. Judy’s paws were still stained with it. She hadn't tried to wipe them clean. 

Fifty feet away, Chief Bogo was a terrifying force of nature. He was pacing the perimeter line, his massive frame vibrating with barely contained rage, barking orders at the forensics teams and patrol officers with a voice that rattled the windows. When one of his own was hit, the politics and the ZIA didn't matter. He was tearing the street apart looking for answers. 

A pair of crime scene technicians walked out of the brownstone across the street, shaking their heads as they approached the Chief. 

"Nothing," one of them said, loud enough for Judy to hear. "No brass, no footprints, no fibers. The roof access door was picked clean, and the sniper didn't leave so much as a scuff mark on the chimney. They were a ghost."

Bogo let out a roar of frustration, dismissing them with a wave of his hand. 

Judy didn't react. She just kept staring at the wet patch on the road. 

The screech of tires announced the arrival of an unmarked ZPD cruiser. Wolford and McHorn practically tore the doors off their hinges as they piled out, with Leni pulling up on her motorcycle a second later. 

Wolford took one look at the crime scene tape, the empty street, and Judy sitting on the curb, and his ears pinned flat against his skull. 

"Hopps," Wolford said, dropping to one knee in front of her. His voice was thick with dread. "Where is she? How bad is it?"

"They took her to Zootopia General," Judy said. Her voice didn't shake. It was completely flat. "She's in a medically induced coma. The EMTs didn't know what the toxin was. They said her nervous system was shutting down."

McHorn let out a low, rumbling curse, kicking the tire of the cruiser. Leni covered her mouth with her paws, her eyes wide with shock. 

"Did you see the shooter?" Wolford asked, his yellow eyes scanning the rooftops. 

"No."

"It was the white fox," Wolford growled, his claws extending. "Or Drakov. It had to be. They warned you in the tunnel, Hopps. This is retaliation."

"I know."

"We're going to find them, Judy," Wolford said, leaning closer, trying to offer some kind of anchor. "I don't care what ZIA says. I don't care about the Black Warrant. We're going to find the fox who pulled that trigger, and we're going to make them pay."

"It doesn't matter who pulled the trigger, James," Judy said softly. 

Wolford frowned. "What?"

Judy slowly lifted her head. The streetlights caught her eyes, but they weren't the bright, hopeful violet they used to be. They were dark. Deep purple. Almost black. 

"It doesn't matter if it was the white fox, or Drakov, or the caracal," Judy said, her voice dropping to a chilling, absolute zero. "Nick is with them."

Wolford hesitated. "Judy, you don't know that Nick had anything to do with—"

"He is with them," Judy repeated, cutting him off. The words were sharp as broken glass. She stood up, looking down at her stained paws. 

For three months, she had built a shrine to his memory. For the last two days, she had desperately clung to the hope that he was deep undercover, that he was a victim of circumstance, that he needed to be saved. 

She looked at the blood and foam on the asphalt. The last piece of her idealism shattered, leaving behind nothing but cold, hard edges. 

"He stood next to Drakov on that screen," Judy said, looking at her team. "He watched them steal the hardware. He watched the white fox nearly break my neck in the tunnel. And he let them send a sniper to my front door."

McHorn stopped pacing. Leni lowered her paws. They all stared at her, recognizing the fundamental shift in the rabbit standing before them. 

"I'm done making excuses for him," Judy said, her jaw locking. "I'm done trying to save him. He's not a cop in trouble. He's a terrorist."

Wolford stood up slowly, matching her cold intensity. "So what's the new objective, Commander?"

Judy didn't flinch at the title. She embraced it. 

"We stop looking for a way to clear his name," Judy ordered, her gaze sweeping over Wolford, McHorn, and Leni. "We find the Apex network. We find their hardware. And we burn it all to the ground. If Nick Wilde gets in the way..."

She paused, looking back at the dark roof across the street. 

"...we treat him like any other target."

***

The Intensive Care Unit at Zootopia General Hospital was a symphony of rhythmic, mechanical beeping. 

Room 412 was bathed in the cold, blue light of life-support monitors. Nadine lay perfectly still on the bed, a massive ventilator tube taped to her muzzle, forcing air into lungs that had forgotten how to expand. 

Dr. Aris, an older badger with deep bags under his eyes, stood at the foot of the bed, reviewing a digital chart. A young sheep nurse stood beside him, adjusting the flow rate on one of the five IV bags hanging from the metal stand. 

"Her core temperature is still dropping," the nurse whispered, her ears pinned back. "And the neuromuscular blockade is spreading to the cardiac tissue. The adrenaline push only bought us a few hours."

Dr. Aris rubbed his face, his expression grim. "I've never seen a synthetic neurotoxin act like this. It's rewriting her nervous system in real-time. The broad-spectrum antivenoms aren't even slowing it down."

"What do we do?"

"We keep her comfortable," the doctor said, his voice heavy with defeat. He looked at the tiger's motionless form. "Call the precinct. Tell them to contact her family. She won't make it to sunrise."

The nurse nodded, her eyes shining with unshed tears. She followed the doctor out of the room, the heavy glass door sliding shut behind them with a soft click. 

For two minutes, the room was empty, save for the mechanical breathing of the ventilator. 

Then, the fluorescent lights in the hallway flickered. It was a microscopic power fluctuation, lasting less than a second. But inside Room 412, the digital monitors blinked, their screens freezing as they temporarily lost connection with the hospital's central diagnostic server. 

The glass door slid open. 

A figure stepped into the room. They wore standard hospital scrubs, a surgical cap, and a mask that obscured everything but their eyes. They moved with absolute silence, their steps making no sound against the linoleum floor. 

The figure didn't look at the monitors. They didn't look at Nadine's face. They moved directly to the IV stand. 

A gloved paw reached out, manipulating the primary intravenous port connected to the tiger's arm. There was a faint click of plastic against plastic. The figure stood there for exactly ten seconds, their body completely still, shielding the IV line from the doorway. 

Footsteps echoed down the hall. Someone was coming back. 

The figure withdrew their paw, turned, and slipped out the door, melting into the shadows of the stairwell just as the lights in the corridor stabilized. 

Inside Room 412, the monitors reconnected to the network. 

Ten minutes later, the rhythmic, sluggish beep of the heart monitor suddenly hitched. The pitch changed. The tempo accelerated. 

The door flew open. Dr. Aris rushed in, holding a defibrillator paddle, expecting to see the tiger crashing into cardiac arrest. 

He stopped dead in his tracks. 

He stared at the central monitor. The red line tracking her heart rate wasn't flatlining. It was climbing. The oxygen saturation numbers were ticking upward, flashing from critical yellow back to stable green. 

"Doctor?" the nurse asked, running in behind him. "What's happening? Did she code?"

Dr. Aris slowly lowered the paddles. He stepped up to the bed and placed a hand on Nadine's arm. The muscles, which had been rigid as stone just fifteen minutes ago, were softening. 

"Her vitals..." Dr. Aris breathed, staring at the screen in absolute disbelief. "They're stabilizing. The blockade is reversing itself."

"How?" the nurse asked, her eyes wide. "We didn't give her anything new."

"I don't know," the doctor whispered, checking the IV lines, finding nothing out of the ordinary. He looked down at the tiger, shaking his head at the impossible data scrolling across the screen. "I don't know."

***

The heavy steel door of their assigned quarters in the bunker clicked shut, sealing them inside. 

Nick leaned against the concrete wall, his chest heaving as he stripped off the stolen hospital scrubs and the surgical mask. He threw them onto the floor with a disgusted noise, running trembling paws over his face. He smelled like antiseptic and cold sweat. 

Skye was sitting on the edge of her cot, fully dressed in her tactical gear, a disassembled pistol resting in her lap. She looked up, her pale blue eyes searching his face. 

"Did you make it in time?" she asked quietly. 

"I don't know," Nick rasped, his voice cracking. He walked over to the small sink in the corner and splashed cold water on his face. "I pushed the prototype into her IV. But the neuromuscular blockade was already hitting her cardiac tissue. It was just a theory, Skye. A stolen formula mixed in a dirty med-bay. I don't know if it's going to save her."

"You did everything you could, Nick."

"It wasn't meant for her!" Nick spun around, slamming his fist into the concrete wall so hard his knuckles bled. The dull *thud* echoed in the small room. "Vesper didn't go to that apartment to shoot a tiger. She went there for Judy. Nadine just got in the way."

Skye didn't flinch at the outburst. She set the slide of her pistol down on the cot. "Vesper is cleaning up loose ends. Judy got too close."

"I'm done," Nick snarled, pacing the narrow space between the cots like a caged animal. "I'm pulling the plug on this right now. We have the tranq-darts. We go out there, we put Vesper and Ragnar to sleep, we zip-tie them to a pipe, and we call the ZIA. We publish the v1 data package and we end this tonight."

"Nick, stop and think—"

"I am thinking!" Nick yelled, pointing a shaking finger at the door. "I'm thinking about Judy kneeling in the street while her best friend suffocates on dry land! I'm thinking about the fact that if we stay in this bunker one more day, Vesper is going to try again, and next time she won't miss!"

Skye stood up. She crossed the room in two strides and grabbed Nick by the shoulders, her grip surprisingly strong. 

"If we take down Ragnar and Vesper tonight, we lose the Architect," Skye said, her voice a harsh, urgent whisper. "Magnus will cut them loose. He'll deny everything, burn the shell companies, and find another crew to finish the job."

"We have the data!"

"We have *logistics* data!" Skye countered, shaking him slightly. "It's enough to cause a scandal, but it's not enough to put a billionaire CEO in a federal prison. He'll tie it up in court for years. And while the city is distracted by the trial, he'll execute the final phase."

Nick stopped fighting her grip, his breathing ragged. 

"You saw the schematics, Nick," Skye said, her eyes boring into his. "You know what happens to this city if he realizes his plan. You know what happens when the grid goes dark and the water stops flowing. Taking down Ragnar doesn't stop that. It just delays it."

Nick looked away, his jaw clenching so hard his teeth ached. He knew she was right. If they walked away now, they would save Judy today, only to watch her burn with the rest of the city tomorrow. 

"The only way to stop Magnus is to let him start the final sequence," Skye said softly, releasing his shoulders. "We have to be inside the network when he makes the connection. We have to cut the wire from the inside. It's the only way to destroy him completely."

Nick looked down at his bleeding knuckles. The blood looked black in the dim light of the bunker. 

If they stayed, they had to pretend they didn't care. They had to stand next to Vesper and pretend they didn't know she had just tried to murder Judy. They had to become the monsters Judy already thought they were. 

"She thinks I did it," Nick whispered, the realization settling over him like a suffocating blanket. "She thinks I'm part of this."

"I know," Skye said. There was no pity in her voice, only a shared, terrible understanding. 

Nick closed his eyes. He thought about the violet eyes he hadn't seen in three months. He thought about the promise he had made to keep her safe. 

He opened his eyes. The panic was gone, replaced by a cold, dead resolve. 

"We stay," Nick said.
