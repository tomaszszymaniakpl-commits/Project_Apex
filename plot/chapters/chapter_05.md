# CHAPTER 5: The Weight of What She Knew

The morning briefing was gray.

Not just the weather—though that too was a flat, overcast ceiling pressing down on the city like a lid—but the quality of the air inside the bullpen. The fluorescent hum seemed louder than usual. The burnt coffee smell from the break room had been there since yesterday and nobody had bothered to replace it.

Judy arrived seven minutes early, as she always did.

She hung her jacket on the hook. Straightened her badge. Sat down at her desk, opened her laptop, and pulled up the Dune Runners evidence file she'd been ignoring for three days.

The stress ball was already in the air.

Catch. Toss. Catch.

"Morning, Carrots," Nick said, without looking up.

Judy sat down at her desk. "Morning."

Catch. Toss. Catch.

She stared at the evidence file. The Dune Runners evidence file. The one that had started everything—the circuit board, the freeze, the beginning of whatever this was. She closed it.

"You finish the asset inventory?" she asked, keeping her voice level.

"Working on it."

"Bogo wanted it by ten."

"I'm aware, Carrots."

She typed three words. Deleted them. Typed them again. Deleted them again.

She could do this. She had trained her face not to crack under pressure. She could look across at this desk, at this fox, and feel absolutely nothing.

The stress ball hit the edge of the desk and bounced onto the floor, rolling under the filing cabinet.

"Great," Nick muttered, pushing back his chair.

"I'll get it." She was already half-standing.

"I've got it."

"It's right—"

"Judy." His voice had a flatness to it that she hadn't heard before. Not the practiced flat of his public mask, the smooth impenetrability he wore in interrogation rooms or in front of witnesses. Something genuinely tired underneath. Something that didn't bother performing. "I've got it."

She sat back down.

He retrieved the ball from under the cabinet and dropped back into his chair and didn't start tossing it again. He just set it on the desk and looked at his screen.

The silence was different now. It had weight.

For three years of partnership, she had never minded silence with Nick. Their silences were comfortable, full of shared context. They'd eaten lunch without speaking a dozen times and it had felt easy. But this one pressed against her ears. She could hear the clock above Bogo's office door. She could hear Delgato laughing about something across the room. She could hear herself breathing, which she never noticed.

"Did you submit the transfer confirmation?" he asked, without turning.

"This morning."

"Good." A beat. His claws moved across the keyboard. "You're going to do great things over there, Carrots."

Something in her chest clenched tight enough to hurt.

"Thanks," she said.

It came out smaller than she intended.

---

The briefing ran from nine to nine forty. Bogo covered three active cases, two personnel updates, and a parking enforcement memo that made the entire room simultaneously lose the will to live. Judy sat in the front row, as she always did. Nick sat down next to her a moment later, as he always did. They didn't acknowledge each other. Bogo started talking; she kept her eyes on the whiteboard for the entire forty minutes, and so did he. They sat close enough that she could feel the warmth radiating off his jacket, and she did not look at him once.

Afterward, in the break room hallway, it was the stupidest thing that did it.

She was standing at the coffee station, adding creamer to her mug, when Nick came in behind her. She heard his footsteps—she always recognized them—and stepped slightly to the side to make room.

He reached past her.

Not around her, not with an excuse-the-reach or the usual careful awareness he had of the physical space between them. He just reached directly past her head to get the sugar from the shelf above, his arm crossing within centimeters of her ears, his jacket sleeve brushing her shoulder, and then he was gone again, footsteps heading back to his desk, coffee in paw.

Like she was part of the room.

It wasn't dramatic. It wasn't even cruel. It was absence—the absence of the attention he'd always paid to her, the small constant awareness that had been the background radiation of their partnership for three years.

She stood there with her mug for a moment.

Then she set it down carefully on the counter and walked back to her desk and sat down and opened the evidence file and stared at it without reading a word of it for four minutes.

Then she stood back up.

"Nick."

He stopped mid-reach for his keyboard. Turned.

"Is there something you want to say to me?" She kept her voice quiet. Controlled. That was worse, she realized—controlled was worse than angry, it sounded like the end of something. "Because I've been waiting. For days, I've been watching you disappear. You leave early, you're vague about where, you give me these—" She stopped. Pressed her paws flat against her thighs, nails digging in slightly. "You look at me like you're already somewhere else. And I know when you're lying, Nick. I've watched you lie to criminals for three years. I know what your face looks like when you're telling the truth. And for days it has not looked like that."

She pointed at his face. She immediately wished she hadn't.

He looked at her. His eyes gave her nothing back.

"I think the stress of the transfer is getting to you," he said. His voice was careful and kind and perfectly pitched, the voice of someone genuinely concerned about a colleague, and she wanted to throw something. "You should get some sleep before you go. Zoo-York is no joke—the feds there are going to—"

"Don't do that," she said.

"Do what?"

"Counsel me. Be reasonable at me. I'm not a witness, Nick. I'm your partner." She caught herself. Was. Past tense now. She was his partner until yesterday's paperwork cleared. "I am asking you, directly, as someone who has had your back every day for three years—is there anything you want to tell me? Anything. Before I leave."

He held her gaze.

The silence stretched out between them.

Something flickered in his jaw, then disappeared. His face reset to careful concern.

She took a breath that hurt going in.
"At this point I can only see two versions," she said. "Either I did something and you won't tell me what, or you decided shutting me out was easier than trusting me."
Her ears dipped. "So which one is it, Nick?"

He didn't move.

"Neither," he said. "You didn't do anything."

"Then *why.*"

The keyboard started up again. Slowly. Deliberate.

Something snapped.

"Don't." She was around his desk before she'd decided to move, putting herself between him and the screen, forcing him to look at her. "Don't you dare type right now. Don't give me the keyboard. I'm standing here talking to you."

Nick leaned back in his chair. His ears went slightly flat—not anger, something tighter. Cornered. "Judy—"

"I'm not going anywhere. I have been *patient* with you for days. I've watched you disappear and I've covered for you and I've smiled at your jokes and I have been so—" Her voice cracked. She pushed through it. "I have been so careful with you. And you give me *nothing.* You sit there and you look right through me and you tell me to have a safe trip like I'm a colleague you barely know."

"I don't know what you want me to say." His voice came out sharper than he'd intended. She saw it register on his face. He tried to dial it back. "I'm not—I'm not doing anything, Judy. I'm just trying to—"

"You're lying. Right now. To my face."

"I'm not lying."

"You are. You've been lying for days and I don't know about what, and the worst part is—" She stopped. Pressed a paw against her sternum, like she was trying to hold something in. "The worst part is you're so *good* at it. I keep second-guessing myself. I keep thinking maybe I'm imagining it. Because you've never done it to me before. You've never—" Her voice dropped. "You were the one person who never did this to me."

Something shifted behind his eyes. Not guilt, not quite. Something more complex, more guarded, that she couldn't name and couldn't read.

"Have a safe trip, Carrots." Quiet. Final.

She stared at him for a long moment.

"Right," she said.

She picked up her jacket from the back of the chair. She walked to the elevator and pressed the button, standing very straight while she waited. When the doors opened, she stepped inside and turned to face the bullpen. The last thing she saw before the doors closed was Nick, still at his desk, still facing his screen, one paw flat against the surface, not moving.

Not watching her go.

The doors closed.

She pressed her back against the far wall and did not make a sound.

---

The evidence archive on the fourth floor connected to the stairwell through a door nobody used anymore. The room was cool and dim and smelled of aged cardboard and plastic evidence sleeves and the specific institutional quiet that settled into spaces no one visited.

Judy sat on the floor between two rows of shelving and pressed her forehead to her knees.

She locked the archive door, sat on the floor between the shelves, and fixed her eyes on the opposite wall.

A sound escaped her. Small. Involuntary. The kind of sound she hadn't made since she was very small and very new to disappointment.

She pressed both paws over her muzzle, hard.

The archive door opened.

Nadine Fangmeyer was built like a piece of architecture—broad shoulders, a slow deliberate way of moving through space, eyes that took everything in and gave very little back. She was the kind of officer you wanted next to you in a dark warehouse and the kind of friend who could sit in complete silence without making it strange.

She sat down on the floor across from Judy. Leaned her back against the opposite shelving. Said nothing.

Judy lifted her head. Straightened.

"I'm fine," she said.

"You're on the floor of the dead archive at nine forty-seven in the morning."

"I needed a minute."

"You've needed a lot of minutes this week." Fangmeyer looked at her steadily. "What happened?"

"Nothing. I'm fine. It's the transfer, it's a lot to—"

"Judy."

"I said I'm fine, Fang."

The tiger said nothing. She simply waited, the way only very patient mammals can wait, without pressure, without filling the silence, just present.

"It's nothing," Judy said again. Smaller this time.

"You were crying."

"I wasn't."

"Jude."

"I wasn't—" Her voice cracked on the second word. She stopped. Pressed her paws against her knees. Tried again. "It doesn't matter. It's fine. Everything is fine. I'm going to Zoo-York and I'm going to do the rotation and come back and it will all be—"

She stopped because she couldn't finish the sentence. She didn't know what it would all be.

Fangmeyer waited.

"He told me to have a safe trip," Judy said. Her voice came out very flat. "I told him I knew he was lying to me, and that was all I got."

The tiger's ears moved back slightly.

"Jude... what happened?" she asked.

The question broke something open. Judy's ears flattened against her skull. She pressed both paws over her eyes, and the breath that came out of her was a shaking thing, undignified and uncontrolled. Then another followed it. Then she stopped trying to hold it and put her forehead back down on her knees and let it go—quietly, contained, not the way she'd cried on the tram platform, not that gasping thing in public—but fully, completely, without performance.

Fangmeyer didn't move. Didn't speak. Just stayed.

After a while the shaking stopped.

"I saw him," Judy said. Muffled, into her knees. "Yesterday. I followed him to the Rainforest District. Lower canals."

Fangmeyer's expression didn't change. She waited.

"He met someone. A vixen. White fur, blue eyes." Judy's voice was flat, factual, the voice she used for incident reports. "She pulled back her hood and he—" She stopped. Swallowed. "He leaned into her. Like she was the only thing keeping him standing. Like he'd been carrying something for weeks and she was the only one he'd let put it down."

She lifted her head. Her eyes were dry now. Past the crying part.

"She was beautiful, Fang. The kind of beautiful that felt inevitable. Like—looking at them, standing there in the rain—I understood it. I wasn't even angry, for a second. I just thought: of course. Of course it's her. Of course it's someone like that." She exhaled, a thin, careful sound. "I can't even hate him for it. That's the worst part. I look at what I am—" A short pause. "And I completely understand why he chose her instead."

Fangmeyer's jaw tightened. "Judy—"

"I'm not saying it for sympathy." Judy's voice was flat. "I'm saying it because it's the only version that makes sense. She was there, he was there, and he's been trying to get me out of the city for days." She looked up at the shelf. 2019. 2020. 2021. "I even have the bags packed."

A silence.

"I'm in love with him," she said. Just like that. Flat and factual, the way you stated something you'd known for a long time but never written down. "I've been in love with him for—I don't know how long. A while." She let out a breath. "The jokes, the deflections, the way he looks at me when he thinks I'm not watching. The way he remembers stupid things—how I take my coffee, which step on the precinct stairs creaks, that I hate the smell of pine air freshener. All of it. I love all of it." A short pause. "I love that dumb fox."

She pressed her paw over her eyes. "First time I've said it out loud."

She looked at Fangmeyer.

Fangmeyer hadn't moved. Her expression was careful, steady — but not surprised. Not even slightly.

"You're not surprised," Judy said.

"Anyone paying attention could see it, Jude."

Judy stared at her for a moment. Then looked away.

"Does it bother you?" she asked quietly. "That it's—" She stopped. Tried again. "He's a fox, Fang. I'm a rabbit. I know what that looks like. I know what people say about that."

Fangmeyer looked at her steadily. "Is that a real question?"

"Yes."

"Then no." Her voice was even, without ceremony. "I've watched you take down mammals twice your size without blinking. I'm not going to have opinions about who you fall in love with." A pause. "Anyone who does can come talk to me about it."

Judy almost laughed. It came out close enough.

Something loosened slightly in her chest. Not better, not fixed. Just less compressed.

"That thing you said," Fangmeyer continued, after a moment. "About understanding why he'd choose someone else. About looking at what you are." She waited until Judy met her eyes. "Don't do that. That's not grief. That's something nastier and you're better than it."

Judy didn't answer.

"Are you going?" Fangmeyer asked. "To Zoo-York."

"Yeah," Judy said. "I'm going."

The tiger nodded. She didn't look convinced — she never did when she thought someone was running rather than choosing — but she didn't push it.

"Call me," she said. "Any time."

"I know."

"Get up." She stood, extending a paw. "Four hours. Make them count."

Judy took the paw. Let herself be pulled up.

"How did you even know I was here?" she asked.

Fangmeyer looked at her with the specific expression of someone who had been keeping an eye on someone for several days and was choosing not to make a big deal of it. "Lucky guess," she said.

Judy almost smiled.

Almost.

---

She left at two in the afternoon.

She didn't announce it. She packed her desk—three years of accumulated small things, a photo, the lucky carrot pen Janet had given her, a spare battery pack—into a box that she carried to her locker and then to her car.

She didn't walk past Nick's desk.

She drove to her apartment and put the box on the kitchen table and sat on the couch.

Her bags were already packed. Two of them, standing by the door, exactly where she'd staged them four days ago when she'd first started building the performance of departure. She'd checked in online. Digital footprint, clean: Officer Judy Hopps, ZPD rotation transfer, departure confirmed.

She looked at the bags.

The shuttle left at seven-fifteen tomorrow morning. Three months. Advanced Tactics and Infiltration. The kind of posting that fast-tracked lieutenancies. She should be excited. Eighteen months ago she would have been incandescent.

She picked up her phone, looked at the confirmed ticket, and set it face down on the table.

She went to bed at ten and lay awake until past two.

---

The shuttle left at seven-fifteen.

She didn't make it.

She was awake when the alarm went off. She turned it off, and she lay there, and the ceiling was the same ceiling it had been at two in the morning, and at three, and at five, and she told herself she was going to get up in one minute, and then the minute passed, and another followed it, and somewhere in there the light shifted from gray to pale gold and she heard the distant sound of the first trams starting their routes, and she knew.

She'd missed it.

She lay there for a while longer, not moving, taking stock of how she felt about that. The answer was: not much. Not the expected guilt of someone who had just derailed a career opportunity. Not relief, either. Just a flat, empty acknowledgment, like closing a file.

She made coffee. She sat at her kitchen table. She looked at the bags still standing by the door.

There was an evening connection—she'd checked the schedule weeks ago, the way she always checked backup routes. It left at six-forty and arrived in Zoo-York just before midnight. Tight, but the program orientation wasn't until tomorrow morning. She could still make it.

She had time.

The question was what to do with it.

She sat with that for a long time, her coffee going cold, watching the morning light move across the kitchen table. And gradually, methodically, the way she worked through difficult cases, she began to arrange what was in her head into something she could hold.

Nick wasn't hers. He'd made a choice, and she had no authority over it.
What they had—partnership, trust, routine—was still real even if it hurt now.
She didn't have to like it. She just had to handle it.

And she still wanted him in her life. Even reduced, even at arm's length. Half of him was still better than none.

So.

She was going to go to his apartment. She was going to say she was sorry for the fight—for cornering him, for the raised voice, for demanding answers he clearly wasn't ready to give. She owed him that. She'd put him in an impossible position and she'd done it in the middle of the bullpen and she wasn't proud of it.

And she was going to tell him she was leaving tonight. Properly. Not the way it had happened yesterday—no box, no silence, no walking past him without a word. She was going to stand in front of him and say: *I'm sorry. I'm going. I hope you're okay.* And she was going to mean every word.

She couldn't leave it the way it was. That wasn't who she was.

Then she'd catch the six-forty. She'd be in Zoo-York before midnight. The plan would hold.

She rehearsed on the tram.

*I'm sorry about yesterday. I was out of line. I just—I care about you. That's all it was. I wanted to say it properly before I go.*

Short. Clean. No confession, no demand. Just the truth, the small digestible version of it.

She stopped rehearsing when the tram crossed into Sahara Square.

The heat hit her as she stepped off the tram—Sahara Square at midday was a different planet from the gray drizzle of the rest of the city. The air was dry and sharp, smelling of hot sand and sun-warmed stone, and the low thud of workout music rose from street level even before she turned the corner.

Nick's place sat below Ellie's Gym. The bass from upstairs still made the pavement hum under her paws.

She knew those stairs by heart. The chipped second step. The rust line on the railing. The low retaining wall halfway down where delivery boxes were sometimes stacked.

She stopped at the top of the stairwell.

The basement door at the bottom was not fully closed.

Just fractionally open—not broken, not ajar in the way of a struggle, but that specific unclosed quality where the latch hadn't caught, a half-inch gap of shadow between door and frame. The kind of thing that happened when someone left in a hurry and didn't check behind them.

She went down three steps, one paw already lifting toward the half-open door.

"Nick—"

She stopped.
From inside, his voice carried through the gap.

Not the words yet. Just the cadence—that flat, measured baritone he used when he was being professionally calm. Not his story voice, not his banter voice. His careful voice. The one she'd heard him use exactly three times in three years: once with a victim who needed to be handled, once with a suspect who was more frightened than dangerous, and once, briefly, with his mother over the phone last Christmas when he hadn't known Judy could hear him.

Then the words snapped into focus.

"Confirmed," Nick was saying. Low. Even. "No. She's gone. She won't be a problem."

The sentence hit like a slap.

Judy moved on instinct then, dropping into a crouch behind the low retaining wall halfway down the stairs, angling herself so the door stayed in her sightline while her body stayed out of view.

She listened.

A pause. A step. Another.

"I cleared the timeline. Tomorrow, during shift change." A longer pause. His voice dropped another register, harder now—not careful, resolved. "I told you. I got rid of the rabbit. She won't interfere."

The concrete wall behind her was very cold.

"Yes." A pause. "I understand what's at stake. No complications. No mistakes. I'll have it done."

She heard the call end.

Then the sounds of him moving—fast, purposeful. A zip. A buckle. A drawer opening, then closing, then a second drawer. The specific sounds of someone preparing.

She flattened herself behind the wall.

The door opened.

Nick stepped out of the basement apartment and pulled the door shut behind him. The deadbolt clicked with finality.

Judy held her breath.

He didn't check the stairwell shadows. He was wearing dark civilian clothes—fitted jacket, no reflective detail, gloves already on. He carried a bag close to his body, the kind of positioning that meant it was heavy and he was conscious of the weight. His head was down, focused somewhere interior.

He took the stairs up to the street without looking back.

Judy gave him a five-second lead, then slipped out of the shadows and followed him up.

---

She lost him at the 7th Avenue interchange.

Her own fault—she'd stayed too far back. The right call professionally, but it meant when he turned into the interchange and the evening crowd hit, she couldn't close the gap without risk of being seen. She reached the corner thirty seconds behind him and he was simply gone. Three exits, two bus lines, a tram platform.

She stood in the flow of commuters and breathed through it.

He'd said *tomorrow, during shift change.*

She had a timeline. She didn't have a location yet.

She turned and caught the tram back. Not home. Back to Sahara Square.

When she reached Nick's building again, the street was quiet. She went down the basement stairs, moving silently, but the landing was empty. The door was locked tight.

She crouched in front of it, pulling her phone out and turning on the flashlight. If this was an operation, there were always traces.

She scanned the doorframe, the handle, the threshold.

And on the doormat—she'd nearly missed it in the low stairwell light—a fine grit of pale material. Grayish-white. Industrial.

She knew that material. She'd spent three days logging a shipment of it in the Dune Runners evidence lockup not six weeks ago.

Acoustic insulation compound. Used in climate-control infrastructure.

She looked at it without touching it.

Then she stood, backed up the stairwell, and climbed back to the street.

She walked home with her paws in her pockets, and she walked the long way, and while she walked she played it back very carefully.

*She's gone. She won't be a problem.*
*I got rid of the rabbit.*
*No complications. No mistakes.*

She had written it off as romance. The secrecy, the distance, the careful management of her schedule, the push toward Zoo-York. A relationship he couldn't tell her about. An affair, or something that had outgrown being called that.

That was what she'd seen in the Rainforest District and that was the story she'd built and she had cried on a train platform over it.

But *no complications, no mistakes* wasn't how you talked about a relationship. That was how you talked about an operation. That was language she'd used herself—briefing her team, confirming a timeline, ensuring all variables were managed. It was the language of someone who was treating risk professionally.

She wasn't a variable in his personal life.

She was a variable in a plan.

She made tea and sat at her kitchen table and opened her notebook to a fresh page.

*Acoustic insulation compound.* She wrote it down.
*Dark jacket, gloves—counter-surveillance kit.*
*Timeline: tomorrow, shift change (18:30).*
*Language: operational, not personal.*

She looked at what she'd written.

She had worked three years in criminal investigation and she knew exactly what this looked like. She also knew that knowing what something looked like and knowing what it was were two different things. She needed more. She needed something she could hold and not explain away.

She had one night to find it.

---

She couldn't use official channels. She'd been transferred out—her credentials were technically inactive, and any access through ZPD systems would flag immediately. But she had a phone, and she had two favors available, and she needed to be precise about what she asked for.

She called Clawhauser first. His personal cell, not the ZPD line.

He picked up on the second ring.

"Judy!" His voice pitched up immediately. "You made it! How's Zoo-York? Is it everything? Is it more than everything? Did you find Prancy's Pastries yet, because—"

"Ben," she said. "Hi. I made it, yes." She paused to let that land. "I'm just missing something—my good charging cable, I think I left it in my desk. Before you send it, I just wanted to check—I left a case note with Nick for the Dune Runners filing, we were going to coordinate before I left and I'm not sure he has everything he needs. Has he been around? Normal schedule?"

A small pause. She could hear him weighing whether this was gossip.

"Well," Clawhauser said, "he's been here. But he asked me to cover his reception check-ins last Tuesday. And he left early on Thursday without logging out properly—he was in the system as remote but he definitely wasn't at his desk, because I walked by three times. He does that sometimes now. Uses the remote flag but I can tell he's not there."

"He's been stressed," Judy said. "With me leaving. I think."

"That's probably it," Clawhauser agreed, warmly. "He's been a bit—you know. Nick-ish. But more so."

"Don't worry about the cable," Judy said. "I'll buy one here. How's Bogo?"

She listened to Clawhauser for five minutes—budget complaints, a memo about the break room coffee machine, an update on a minor vandalism case—and made the right sounds, and when she hung up she had three items confirmed on her notepad.

*Tuesday: missing from reception. Thursday: false remote log. This week: pattern.*

Tuesday was the night she'd been in the Rainforest District.

She looked at the ceiling for a moment.

Then she wrote: *He's been planning this longer than a few days.*

---

The second call needed to be Leni, and it needed to be from somewhere untraceable.

She found the payphone near the Meadowlands—one of the last in the city, used mostly by elderly mammals who didn't trust digital contracts, overlooked by everyone else. She called from there the next morning.

Leni picked up after four rings.

"Hopps?"

"I need something small."

A pause. The specific quality of pause that meant an ocelot was reconsidering her life choices.

"You're supposed to be in Zoo-York."

"I need something small, Leni."

Another pause. Longer. "Define small."

"Maintenance schedules. Public infrastructure—Civic Hub district, anything with a service window in the next forty-eight hours. Climate systems or municipal data routing. Specifically somewhere that has reduced monitoring during the maintenance period."

Silence.

"Hopps. That sounds like you're trying to find a building with a blind spot."

"I'm trying to find a building someone else is planning to use a blind spot in."

The silence stretched out long enough that she started wondering if Leni had put the phone down. Then: the sound of keyboard clicks.

"I'm not pulling restricted files," Leni said. "I'm looking at public maintenance postings and cross-referencing with infrastructure logs I already have authorized access to. And I'm doing this exactly once."

"Once is all I need."

More keys. Judy stood in the payphone box and watched a family of otters navigate a stroller through the morning foot traffic.

"Okay," Leni said finally. "There's a maintenance window tomorrow evening—19:00 to 23:00. Central Civic Hub. They're running diagnostics on the municipal climate routing array. During that window, secondary camera feeds route to a backup server with a twenty-second lag, and the auth-verification layer for the vault access routes through a legacy system instead of the main server."

Judy wrote it down.

"What that means," Leni continued, and the edge in her voice was unmistakable now, "is that during that specific window, the secondary access vault in that building can be entered with an authorization level that would normally be insufficient. Something like a senior officer's credentials might get you past the secondary door when the main verification layer is offline."

A pause.

"Which isn't relevant to you," Leni added. "Because you're in Zoo-York."

"I'm in Zoo-York," Judy confirmed.

"I'm sending you a PDF of public maintenance postings. Freely available information."

"Thanks, Leni."

"Two bags of chips," Leni said, and the line went dead.

Judy stood in the payphone box and looked at her notes.

The window was tomorrow evening. 19:00 to 23:00.

Nick had said: *tomorrow, during shift change.*

The evening shift change at ZPD was 18:30.

She circled the window on her notepad and underlined it twice.

---

She spent the morning of the next day watching ZPD from the driver's seat of a rented sedan parked across the street.

It was a risk—any officer coming off shift might recognize her silhouette through the windshield—but she wasn't going to risk losing him again. If he was moving at shift change, she needed to be on his tail from the moment he walked out the doors. She was wearing a cap and a dark jacket, sitting low in the seat, a pair of tactical binoculars resting on her lap.

At 11:14, the glass doors across the street swung open.

Bogo came outside.

This didn't happen often—Bogo was a desk animal most days, his working rhythm built around not leaving the building before noon—but today he came through the main doors with his jacket draped over one arm and something printed in the other paw, and he stopped on the front steps and pulled out his phone.

Nick came through the doors twenty seconds behind him.

Judy brought the binoculars up to her eyes.

Nick moved at a pace that was professional but unhurried—the natural pace of someone following up on something routine—and he had a manila folder in one paw, and he went directly to Bogo and held the folder out.

She couldn't hear anything across four lanes of traffic.

But the lenses brought him right into her lap.

Bogo took the folder. Nick leaned in—not aggressively, just the standard lean of someone pointing something out on a document. Look here, is this the right figure, should I adjust the timeline. It was a completely unremarkable interaction. She'd seen Nick brief Bogo in this exact posture dozens of times.

Bogo's access credential hung from a belt clip on his left hip. Standard department carrier, clear front pocket.

Nick stepped slightly closer.

She recognized the body language instantly. She'd watched him demonstrate the hustle with a suspect's wallet once, purely for show, pulling the prize and replacing it with a deck of cards before the perp even blinked. It was all sleight of paw and misdirection.

Nick shifted his weight, using the manila folder to completely obscure Bogo's belt line. A slight bump of the shoulder.

The card vanished.

Judy's breath caught.

Under the cover of the folder, Nick slid the plastic rectangle into his jacket pocket. He held his position, keeping Bogo engaged, counting the seconds. One. Two. Three.

He pulled his paw out.

As Bogo handed the folder back, Nick adjusted his stance, bumping the buffalo's side again. The card slipped back into the plastic carrier, perfectly seated.

Bogo never even noticed. It was a masterpiece of pickpocketing, executed flawlessly in broad daylight on the front steps of a police precinct against the Chief of Police.

Nick stepped back. Bogo said something short—she saw Nick nod—and Bogo went back inside. Nick turned and walked back toward the entrance.

Judy lowered the binoculars.

The front steps of the precinct were empty.

She sat in the rented sedan, gripping the steering wheel, and let the pieces snap together.

Why take the Chief's card only to return it three seconds later?

Leni's words played back in her mind. *Something like a senior officer's credentials might get you past the secondary door when the main verification layer is offline.* Three seconds were enough.

Nick hadn't just picked Bogo's pocket. He'd cloned Bogo's credentials in those three seconds.

He had the location. He had the window. And now he had the keys.

She tried to think of an innocent explanation.

She tried for a long time.

She couldn't find one.

---

She was in position at 18:45.

She'd taken the maintenance access road on the north side of the Civic Hub, the one used by service vehicles that bypassed the main entrance. She knew the building from a theft case two years ago—she'd walked every access route. She found her position on a service walkway above the rear loading bay: metal grating, cold under her paws, a clear sightline through the reinforced window to the infrastructure vault antechamber below.

She had her tranq pistol. She had her notebook. She had Fangmeyer's number ready.

She had not called Fangmeyer yet.

She needed to see it first. She needed it to be real—concrete, undeniable, documented—because there was still a part of her, a small persistent part that had spent seventy-two hours building alternative explanations, and she needed that part to finally go quiet.

The lights in the antechamber below were on maintenance mode: half-power, amber-orange, turning everything to underwater shadows.

She waited.

At 19:23, a figure came through the access door.

Nick.

Dark jacket. Efficient movement. He scanned the room once, twice—professional sweep—and crossed to the authorization terminal at the far wall.

Judy's paw tightened on the railing.

He pulled out a blank access card and a small, matte-black cloner—the device he'd used to strip the key from Bogo's physical badge. He connected the cloner to the terminal's secondary input port, waiting for the handshake to clear. Then he slid the blank card through the reader.

The light went green.

The terminal authorized.

He pocketed both items and moved to the secondary vault. A heavy steel cabinet, combination lock, the kind of physical redundancy that existed specifically because whoever had designed this system understood that digital locks needed a backup you couldn't hack remotely. He entered the code—she watched his paw move across the dial but couldn't see the digits—and the door opened.

Inside: a row of containers. Small, labeled, numbered. He moved along them without hesitating or searching, which told her he already knew exactly which one he was looking for. He'd planned this down to the container number.

He lifted the seventh container from its housing. Turned it over, examined it briefly—a metallic edge catching the amber light—and slid it into his bag.

He closed the vault.

He re-engaged the authorization terminal.

He smoothed everything back into place the way a practiced professional does—not erasing the evidence, just delaying discovery. Then he picked up his bag and walked to the exit door.

He didn't look up.

He never looked up.

The door closed behind him with a soft, decisive click.

---

Judy stood on the metal grating in the amber half-light and didn't move.

The vault remained secure. The heavy steel door remained locked. But the mammal she trusted more than anyone else in the world was gone into the dark.

She was entirely alone.
