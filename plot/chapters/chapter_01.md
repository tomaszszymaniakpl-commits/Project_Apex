# CHAPTER 1: Business as Usual

## Sahara Square — 02:00 ZST

Sahara Square didn't cool down at night; it just changed colors.

The heat radiating off the gravel roof was a physical weight, heavy and suffocating, pressing Judy Hopps flat against the tar paper. Forty degrees Celsius at two in the morning. Even the air conditioning vents humming from the Palm Hotel across the street seemed to exhale furnace heat.

Judy adjusted the focus on her tactical binoculars. Sweat trickled down her neck, matting the gray fur beneath her kevlar vest, but her paws remained steady. Below, nestled in the shadows of an abandoned desalination plant, the target sat like a rusty metal box in the sand.

Sector 4. Known drop point for the "Dune Runners" smuggling ring.

She tapped her earpiece, cutting through the static of the city.

"Dispatch, this is Hopps. Eyes on target. Suspects are stationary. Requesting status on Alpaca-One."

A beat of silence. Then, a familiar, lazy baritone crackled directly into her ear, bypassing dispatch entirely.

"Alpaca-One... seriously, Carrots? I feel like I should be knitting a sweater, not infiltrating a smuggling den."

Judy rolled her eyes, keeping her gaze fixed on the warehouse door. A hyena with a submachine gun was pacing out front.

"Stick to the protocol, Nick. What's your twenty?"

"My twenty is... approachable. Standing by for the green light, Fluff."

"It's Officer Hopps," she corrected automatically, though her nose twitched with suppressed amusement. "And hold position. Don't engage until I give the signal."

"Relax. You know I love a grand entrance."

Down on the street level, a sleek, rented convertible—paid for with a seized asset budget that Bogo had grumbled about for twenty minutes—rolled around the corner. It moved with the silent purr of an electric engine, the midnight-blue paint gleaming under the neon palms.

It stopped right in front of the hyena.

The door opened. Nick stepped out. He wasn't wearing his ZPD blues. Tonight, he was wearing a silk shirt that cost more than Judy's first car, gold aviator sunglasses (at night), and a smile that could sell sand to a camel.

"Showtime," he whispered over the comms, his voice shifting from partner to performer.

Judy watched through the lenses. Her heart kicked a rhythm against her ribs—part adrenaline, part the sharp anxiety that always came when he stepped into danger without a vest.

Nick strolled up to the hyena. The guard bristled, raising the barrel of his weapon.

"Whoa, easy there, Cuddles," Nick said, his voice loud enough for her directional mic to pick up clearly. He held up his paws, palms open. "I'm here for the auction. Unless the invitation got lost in the mail? The service in this district is terrible."

The hyena snarled. "Boss said no visitors. Get lost, fox."

Nick lowered his sunglasses, peering over the rim. "No visitors? That's funny. Because my employer—let's call him Mr. Big, though he hates the nickname—was under the impression that you boys were looking to liquidate some... sensitive inventory. And he sent me with a briefcase."

Nick patted the leather case in the passenger seat.

"Heavy thing. Full of unmarked bills. But if you're not interested in money, I can just drive over to the Tundratown crew. I hear they're more... hospitable."

He turned back to the car. The oldest con in the city still worked.

The hyena hesitated. Greed battled with orders. Greed won.

"Wait." The guard lowered the gun. "Let me check with the boss."

"Hook, line, and sinker," Nick murmured. "I'm going inside."

"Copy that," Judy breathed, her finger hovering over her tranquilizer pistol. "We're holding. One wrong move and we breach."

Nick vanished into the rusty maw of the warehouse.

The silence that followed stretched thin. Judy counted the seconds, listening intently through the earpiece. The audio was slightly muffled, but clear enough.

"So," Nick's voice came through, casual and bored. "This is the merchandise? A bit dusty, isn't it?"

A deep, rumbling voice answered—likely a Rhino. "Top quality military surplus. Straight from the convoy. You want it or not?"

"I want it," Nick replied. "But not at this price. My employer authorized me to offer fifty thousand. You're asking for eighty."

"Eighty is the price," the Rhino grunted. "Take it or leave it."

"Fifty-five," Nick countered. "And I don't mention the scratch on that generator casing."

"Seventy," the Rhino growled. "And you walk out of here with your tail attached."

Judy tensed on the roof. The negotiation was stalling. Nick needed three more seconds.

"Fine, fine," Nick sighed theatrically. "You drive a hard bargain. Seventy it is." A briefcase latch clicked. "But I have to ask... how do you guys split a score like this? Evenly?"

"What's it to you?" a high-pitched voice—another Hyena—snapped.

"Nothing, nothing," Nick said, his tone dripping with innocence. "It's just... that's a very nice watch you're wearing, big guy. A Cerberus Mark IV, isn't it? Solid gold bezel?"

"Yeah," the Rhino sounded proud. "Got it last week."

"Beautiful," Nick admired. "My boss wears one. Costs about twenty grand. Must be nice, being able to drop a third of the take on jewelry while your boys here are wearing..." Nick paused. "Are those knock-off sneakers? Ouch."

The atmosphere in the room shifted instantly. Judy felt the room turn on itself.

"Wait a minute," the Hyena's voice was sharp now. "You said the whole shipment was only worth forty."

"I... I meant net profit!" the Rhino stammered, his confidence cracking.

"Net profit?" Nick laughed softly. "Math isn't my strong suit, gentlemen, but if he's getting eighty thousand from me, and he told you the shipment was worth forty..."

"He's playing you!" the Hyena screamed. "You said we were partners!"

"He's lying!" the Rhino roared. "Shut up!"

The sound of a table flipping violently echoed through the comms. A gun cocked.

"Whoops," Nick whispered amidst the shouting. "Cue the cavalry, Fluff."

Judy didn't hesitate. She vaulted over the parapet, sliding down the drainpipe with practiced ease, hitting the sand running.

"All units, GO! GO! GO! Code 3!"

From the alleyways, the ZPD tactical team swarmed. Blue and red lights flooded the darkness, washing out the neon palms. The warehouse doors were kicked in with a deafening crash.

"ZPD! DROP YOUR WEAPONS! GET DOWN!"

Judy burst through the entrance, tranquilizer gun raised. The scene inside was absolute chaos. The Hyenas were grappling with the Rhino, money was flying everywhere, and crates were overturned.

Nick stood near a stack of crates, trying to look inconspicuous.

"ZPD!" Judy shouted again.

The Hyenas froze, throwing their paws up instantly. But the Rhino—a massive tank of a mammal—wasn't done. His eyes, red with rage, locked onto the fox in the white suit. He realized exactly who had turned his crew against him.

"YOU!" the Rhino bellowed.

He ignored the police. He lowered his horn and charged.

Nick's eyes went wide. He scrambled back, but his path was blocked by a forklift. There was nowhere to go. The floor shook under the Rhino's tonnage.

"Nick!" Judy screamed.

She didn't think. She tracked the target—a moving mountain of muscle—and squeezed the trigger.

*Thwip.*

The tranquilizer dart hit the Rhino squarely in the thick neck muscle, just below the ear.

The beast took one more step, his horn inches from Nick's chest, then stumbled. His momentum carried him forward, legs giving out, and he crashed into the crates beside Nick with a crash that shook the crates. Dust billowed up, coating Nick's pristine white suit.

Nick stood frozen for a second, pressed against the forklift, staring at the unconscious giant at his feet. He looked up, finding Judy through the settling dust. She stood ten meters away, her dart gun still raised, chest heaving.

He slid his sunglasses back up his nose, though his paws were trembling ever so slightly.

"Cutting it a little close there, Officer Hopps," he called out, his voice steady but breathless. "I was about to use my karate."

Judy holstered her weapon, letting out a breath she felt she'd been holding for ten minutes. She walked over to him, ignoring the other officers securing the hyenas. She checked him for injuries in one fast sweep: ears, paws, tail.

"You don't know karate, Nick," she said, her voice tight with residual adrenaline.

Nick grinned, flashing that sharp-toothed smile that always made her want to punch him and hug him at the same time.

"Details, Fluff. Details."

---

The adrenaline was fading, leaving behind the dull, throbbing ache of a job done.

The warehouse was a hive of controlled activity now. Uniformed officers were tagging crates, photographing the damage, and hauling the groggy, handcuffed Rhino toward the transport vans. The air was thick with the smell of stirred-up dust, old grease, and the metallic tang of adrenaline still hanging in the air.

Judy wiped sweat from her forehead with the back of her paw. She walked over to where Nick was leaning against a stack of wooden pallets, examining a crate the Rhino had been guarding.

His pristine white suit was ruined—coated in gray dust and dirt from the scuffle.

"Nice look," she noted, nudging his arm. "Very... 'distressed chic'. I think it suits you better than the clean version."

Nick didn't look up immediately. He was sifting through the contents of the open crate: military-grade jammer units, long-range scopes, and piles of tangled wiring.

"I'll have you know, Carrots, that this suit is a rental," he drawled, though his voice lacked its usual bite. He picked up a jammer, turning it over in his paws. "I'm going to lose my deposit. And possibly my dignity. Do you know how hard it is to get rhino dust out of silk?"

Judy chuckled, reaching into the crate to help him catalog. "I'm sure you'll talk your way out of it. You always do."

"True," Nick murmured.

He reached deeper into the crate, pushing aside a heavy battery pack. His claws scraped against something metal at the bottom.

He pulled it out.

It wasn't a weapon. It wasn't a jammer. It was a small, green circuit board, no larger than a deck of cards. It looked old—the soldering was chunky, the silicon faded, the corners chipped. It looked like trash compared to the modern military tech surrounding it.

Judy watched Nick's face. She expected a quip. A joke about antique electronics. A sarcastic comment about the smugglers' standards.

Instead, Nick froze.

It wasn't a hesitation. It was a full-body lock. His tail, which had been swaying lazily with the rhythm of their banter, stopped mid-swing and went rigid. His ears swiveled forward, locking onto the object in his paws.

"Nick?" Judy asked, her voice dropping. "What is it?"

He didn't answer. He was staring at the circuit board as if it were a live grenade.

Then, it hit her.

Judy's nose twitched involuntarily. The air in the warehouse smelled of dust and grease, but beneath that, cutting through it like a knife, was a new scent radiating from the fox beside her.

It wasn't the warm, woodsy musk of Nick Wilde, the partner she trusted.

It was sharp. Acrid. Cold.

It was the smell of ozone and wet copper.

*Cold terror.*

Judy had smelled fear on suspects a thousand times. She had smelled it on victims. But she had never, not once in two years of partnership, smelled it on Nick.

This was primal terror.

"Nick," she said again, sharper this time. She reached out, touching his arm. His muscles were rock hard, tense as wire.

The contact seemed to snap him back to reality.

Nick blinked. The emerald light in his eyes, which had gone terrifyingly flat and dark, suddenly flickered back to life. He jerked his head up, looking at her. For a split second, Judy saw a stranger looking out from behind his eyes—someone she had never met.

Then the mask came down.

Nick let out a laugh. It was loud. Too loud. It bounced off the metal walls, sounding hollow and brittle.

"Would you look at this?" he said, shoving the circuit board into the evidence bag he was holding, his movements jerky and fast. "Vintage tech! I haven't seen a layout like this since... well, since the Stone Age. These guys must be desperate if they're trading in museum pieces."

"Nick," Judy said slowly, ignoring his deflection. "What's wrong?"

Nick sealed the evidence bag and tossed it onto the pile with the others, turning his back to her to "dust off" his already ruined suit.

"It's just junk, Carrots," he said, and this time his voice was smooth, controlled, perfect. "Probably worth five bucks on eBay. Come on, let's finish up. I can hear Bogo writing up the commendations already, and I want to make sure they spell my name right."

Judy stared at him. Her nose told her he was lying. Her gut told her something was wrong. But they were surrounded by officers, the case was closed, and Nick was already walking away toward the exit, his stride loose and easy, like nothing in the world had touched him.

She looked at the evidence bag on the pile. The small green board sat there, innocent and silent.

*Just junk*, he had said.

Judy frowned, her ears drooping slightly. She grabbed the evidence bag and logged it into her tablet, marking it for priority analysis, though she didn't know why.

She mouthed it once: "Just junk."

She didn't believe him. Not for a second.

---

Chief Bogo’s office on the second floor was designed to make everyone else feel small.
Between the high ceilings, the massive buffalo-sized furniture, and the wall of commendations that looked down like judgmental ancestors, it was the architectural equivalent of a stern glare.

Nick and Judy stood on the carpet in front of the heavy oak desk. Bogo was reading their report, his reading glasses perched precariously on his broad nose. The silence was punctuated only by the rhythmic thrum-thrum-thrum of a ceiling fan struggling to circulate the coffee-scented air.

"So," Bogo rumbled, finally looking up. He took off his glasses. "You recovered the cash, secured the contraband, and dismantled the Dune Runners' leadership cell in one night."

"All in a day's work, Chief," Nick said, leaning slightly on his heels. "Though I’m still waiting for the invoice on my dry cleaning. Do you know what rhino dust does to silk?"

Bogo ignored him, turning his gaze to Judy.

"Your methods were... unorthodox, Hopps. As usual."

Judy straightened, her ears perking up. "Effective, sir. We minimized collateral damage and avoided a firefight."

Bogo stared at them for a long moment. Then, the corner of his mouth twitched. It wasn't quite a smile, but for Bogo, it was practically a standing ovation.

"Good work," he grunted. He tossed the file onto the 'Completed' stack. "The Mayor is pleased. Which means I am pleased. Which means you two are going to vanish for a few days."

He leaned back in his leather chair, the springs groaning under the weight.

"Take the long weekend. I don't want to see your faces until Tuesday. Dismissed."

"Thank you, sir!" Judy beamed, snapping a salute.

Nick offered a casual two-finger salute. "You’re too kind, Chief. Try not to miss us too much."

"Get out, Wilde," Bogo muttered, already reaching for the next file.

---

The bullpen downstairs was humming with the chaotic energy of the morning shift change. Phones were ringing, keyboards clattered like hail on a tin roof, and the smell of stale coffee and fresh donuts wafted from Clawhauser’s reception desk.

As Nick and Judy descended the stairs, the mood shifted from formal to familial.

"Nice collar last night, you guys!" Officer Wolford called out from his cubicle, raising a coffee mug in salute.

"Heard you took down a Rhino," Fangmeyer added, looking up from her paperwork. She grinned, her tiger stripes shifting as she leaned back. "Did he actually cry? Be honest."

"Like a baby," Nick lied smoothly, winking at her. "Hopps here terrified him."

Judy chuckled, feeling the warmth of the precinct wash over her. This was her team. Her precinct.

Then, the atmosphere curdled.

"Yeah, amazing," a voice drawled from the back row. "We're handing out commendations for lying now? Guess it comes naturally to some species."

Officer Delgato. The lion was leaning back in his chair, cleaning his claws with a letter opener. He didn't look up, but his tone dripped with lazy, casual venom. The bullpen went quiet.

Judy’s ears flattened. She opened her mouth to retort—to defend Nick—but the shadow fell over Delgato’s desk before she could speak.

Officer McHorn didn't say a word. The massive rhino simply stood up from his desk adjacent to Delgato’s. He loomed over the lion, blocking out the fluorescent ceiling lights. He cracked his knuckles. Once. A sound like a gunshot in the quiet room.

Delgato looked up. He saw two tons of gray muscle staring down at him with zero patience.

The lion cleared his throat, suddenly finding his letter opener very interesting. He spun his chair around, turning his back to the room. "Just saying. Paperwork isn't going to file itself."

McHorn held the stare for another second, then looked at Judy and nodded. A silent promise: not today.

Judy smiled gratefully at the tank of a mammal. She glanced at Nick. His face was a mask of bored indifference, but his thumb was rhythmically tracing the edge of an old, tarnished pocket watch he kept in his vest—a habit Judy recognized. He only did that when he was trying to hide how much something bothered him. She saw his shoulders relax, just a fraction.

"Come on," she said softly, nudging him. "Let's get out of here before Clawhauser tries to hug us."

---

Stepping out of the precinct was like walking into a new world. The morning air in Savanna Central was crisp and clean, scrubbing away the smell of the station. The sun was shining, traffic was flowing, and for the first time in weeks, no clock was chasing them.

They walked down the precinct steps in comfortable silence, shoulder to shoulder.

"So," Nick said, putting on his sunglasses. "Three whole days of freedom. What are you going to do with yourself, Officer Hopps? Bust some jaywalkers for old times' sake?"

"Very funny," Judy said, taking a deep breath of the city air. "Actually, I was thinking about going home. To Bunnyburrow."

"Ah. The quiet life." Nick nodded sagely. "Sounds thrilling. Try not to party too hard. I, on the other hand, have a date with my couch, a stack of movies, and absolutely zero movement."

Judy stopped walking. An idea, bright and sudden, pinged in her head.

"You should come."

Nick stopped a few steps ahead. He turned around, sliding his glasses down his nose. "Come where? To your couch?"

"No! To Bunnyburrow!" Judy hopped in front of him, her energy spiking. "It’s the Carrot Days Festival this weekend! My mom makes the best blueberry pie—well, technically carrot-blueberry pie—and you’ve never met the family, and the weather is going to be perfect..."

Nick’s smile faltered. He took a half-step back, his body language shifting from relaxed to guarded.

"Whoa, brake check, Carrots. Me? In Bunnyburrow? Have you thought this through?"

"What's to think about?"

"It’s a rabbit festival, Judy," Nick said, gesturing vaguely at his own red fur. "I’m a fox. A predator. In a town famous for... well, being scared of things with teeth. I’d stick out like a sore thumb. A sore, red, predatory thumb."

"Oh, come on," Judy rolled her eyes. "My parents won't care. They know we're partners. Dad even stopped buying fox repellent. Mostly."

"Mostly?" Nick raised an eyebrow. "Inspiring confidence. Look, Fluff, it’s a nice offer, but I don't think I'm cut out for country life. I have allergies. To... fresh air. And silence."

He was deflecting. Judy knew him too well. He wasn't afraid of the country; he was afraid of being the outsider. Of not being wanted.

She decided to play dirty.

She stepped into his personal space. She reached out and grabbed the lapels of his green Hawaiian shirt. Then, she looked up.

She deployed The Eyes.

She widened them until they were impossibly big, shimmering violet pools of pure, concentrated sadness and hope. Her nose twitched once. Her ears drooped just enough to look pathetic.

"Pleeeeease?" she whispered. "It won't be the same without my best friend."

Nick stared at her. He tried to look away. He looked at the sky. He looked at the traffic. He looked back at her. The Eyes were still there, relentless.

His shoulders slumped. The tension drained out of him.

"You are evil," he groaned, rubbing his face with a paw. "You know that, right? This is police brutality."

Judy beamed, dropping the act instantly. "Is that a yes?"

Nick sighed, a long, dramatic exhale of a mammal who knows he’s lost.

"Fine. Fine. But if anyone tries to pitchfork me, you're first between me and the pitchforks."

"Deal!"

In a burst of spontaneous excitement, Judy surged forward and wrapped her arms around his middle, squeezing tight.

It was fierce, impulsive, and lasted one beat too long to pass as casual. She buried her face in his shirt, smelling the familiar scents of coffee and Zootopia smog that clung to him.

Nick stiffened for a heartbeat, surprised, before his paws came up to awkwardly pat her back.

"Okay, okay," he muttered, though his voice was soft. "You're crushing the merchandise, Hopps."

Judy pulled back, grinning, her cheeks slightly flushed.

"The train leaves in an hour! Pack a bag. Don't bring the tie."

She turned and started jogging backward toward her apartment, unable to stop smiling.

"See you at the station, Slick!"

Nick watched her go, shaking his head. He adjusted his collar, fighting the smile that was threatening to break his cool facade.

"Dumb bunny," he whispered to the empty street.

But he was already mentally calculating which shirt looked least like trouble.
