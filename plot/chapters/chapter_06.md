# Chapter 06: The Line

The door had closed with a soft, decisive click.

Judy stood on the metal grating and didn't move for several long seconds.

The antechamber below was empty now, amber-lit and undisturbed. It looked exactly as it had before he’d walked in. There was nothing to see. Nothing that would immediately suggest anything had been taken, that a set of security credentials had been used without their owner's knowledge, or that three years of absolute trust had just been packed into a duffel bag and carried out a service exit.

She became aware, gradually, of her own breathing.

It was the only sound in the vast space, aside from the distant, monotonous hum of the climate control system. Her own lungs working. In. Out.

She thought about the barn in Bunnyburrow. The same amber light, the melody of the violin. The specific weight of his paw covering hers, and the way he’d said *there’s nowhere else I’d rather be* like admitting it had cost him something real. Like any of it had been real.

She stopped thinking about the barn.

This was not the time for thinking. This was the time for procedure. It was a defense mechanism drilled into them at the academy: don't fall apart in an active scene. She locked the emotion into a tight, sealed box at the back of her mind and turned the key. From this moment on, she was only an observer.

She reached for her phone.

Fangmeyer answered on the second ring.

"Jude? I thought—"

"I need you to listen." Her voice came out clean. Cold. Professional. She only used this stripped-down, sterile register when a situation was critical and there wasn't room for anything else. "I never left for the training course. I'm in the city. I'll explain everything, but not right now. Right now I need your help and I need you to not ask any unnecessary questions for the next hour."

Three seconds of silence. The tiger immediately caught the shift in her tone.

"Where are you?" Fangmeyer asked, her own voice dropping into operational mode.

"Civic Hub. North service road. I'm heading toward the harbor district, past the old Dune Runners drop point." Judy climbed down from the walkway, her paws hitting the concrete silently. In the distance, through the misting rain, she could see the back of a dark jacket moving toward the bridge.
"I'm pinging you my live location right now. I think the handoff is going to happen at one of the warehouses down there. Somewhere near the water."

"Got it. I'm forty minutes out."

"I know. That's enough." She started moving, keeping the silhouette in her sightline. The evening was gray and rapidly cooling. "Fang?"

"Yeah."

"He's not who we thought he was."

A long pause. The kind that meant Fangmeyer was processing a piece of information she wished she hadn't heard.

"I'm on my way," she finally said.

Judy pocketed the phone.

Ahead of her, across the bridge, the harbor district was a blurred smear of sodium-yellow lights and industrial dark. The rain was starting to fall harder now. The figure in the dark jacket was fifty meters ahead, moving with clear purpose toward the docks.

The rabbit followed.

---

The harbor district smelled of salt, ozone, and decaying iron. 

By the time Judy reached the perimeter of the abandoned warehouse complex, the rain had evolved from a fine mist into a heavy, relentless downpour. It drummed against the corrugated metal roofs and hissed into the dark water of the canal. There were no streetlights here—the city had stopped replacing the bulbs years ago—only the cold, intermittent sweep of a distant lighthouse beam slicing through the storm.

She moved like a shadow, slipping between rusting shipping containers and stacked pallets. She didn't use her flashlight. She didn't use her radio. She relied on hearing and instinct honed by years on the force. 

She found them near the loading docks of Warehouse 4.

Judy crouched behind a stack of rotting wooden crates, her uniform soaking through to her fur, the cold seeping into her bones. But she didn't shiver. She was numb to it.

Thirty yards away, under the overhang of a loading bay, stood two foxes.

One was Nick. The collar of his jacket was turned up against the wind, his posture tense, calculating. The other was the white vixen from the Rainforest District safehouse.

Through the curtain of rain, Judy watched them. The white vixen wasn't wearing a hood tonight. The ambient light from a nearby security bulb caught the sharp, elegant lines of her face, the pristine snow-white fur standing in stark contrast to the grime of the docks. She was beautiful, lethal-looking, and standing much too close to Nick.

They were talking, their voices lost to the sound of the storm, but their body language screamed urgency. The white vixen gestured toward the water, shaking her head. Nick replied, his paw making a sharp, cutting motion.

Then, he reached into his jacket.

Judy’s grip on her tranquilizer pistol tightened. This was it. The culmination of all the lies, all the secrecy, all the late-night disappearances. 

Nick pulled out the small, metallic case he had taken from the infrastructure vault just an hour ago. The cryptographic keys to the city. 

He held it for a second, looking down at it. Even from this distance, Judy could see a hesitation in his shoulders—a microsecond of doubt. But then the white vixen placed a paw on his arm. A reassuring, intimate gesture. 

Nick handed the case over. 

The transaction was complete. A privileged access path into the city’s security architecture—power grids, traffic routing, emergency response layers—sold out in the rain by a mammal who used to buy her carrot cake on Tuesdays.

A jagged bolt of lightning fractured the sky over the ocean, throwing the docks into brilliant, blinding white for a fraction of a second. In that flash, Judy saw the undeniable reality of what was happening. There was no deep-cover op. No hidden contingency. There was just a dirty cop, paying his debt to the underworld.

The numbness that had carried her from the Civic Hub finally cracked. Hot, blinding anger bled through the fault lines.

She didn't wait for Fangmeyer. She didn't call it in. 

Judy unholstered her weapon, stepped out from behind the crates, and walked directly into the rain.

---

The sound of her boots on the wet concrete was entirely lost in the storm, but something—some instinct or shift in the air—made Nick turn before she even spoke.

"ZPD! Keep your paws where I can see them!" 

The command tore from her throat, fighting against the roar of the rain. It sounded like a stranger’s voice. 

Nick froze. His posture went utterly rigid. Next to him, the white vixen’s hand hovered inches from her jacket pocket, her body coiled like a loaded spring. Judy kept her tranq pistol leveled perfectly at Nick's chest. Her training held her arms steady, but inside, her heart was beating against her ribs like a trapped bird.

Water ran down Nick's face, plastering the fur to his cheeks. He looked at the gun. He looked at Judy. His emerald eyes, usually so expressive, so full of warmth and cynical amusement, were wide and completely unreadable.

"Judy," he said over the sound of the downpour. His voice sounded hollow. "You shouldn't be here."

"Drop the case, Nick. Kick it away." 

He didn't move. The white vixen shifted her weight slightly, putting herself half a step behind him. Judy’s grip on her weapon tightened until her knuckles ached.

"Please," Judy said. The professional mask cracked, just a little. The cold, sterile register she had used with Fangmeyer dissolved, leaving behind only the desperate plea of a friend. "Tell me this is an operation. Tell me Bogo put you up to this. Tell me *anything*, Nick. Just give me a reason."

*Lie to me,* she thought, begging him silently. *Just give me an excuse to lower the gun. Please.*

Nick stared at her. Through the rain, Judy watched a subtle, terrifying transformation take place. 

The shock and hesitation drained out of him. The warmth vanished. His ears flattened slightly, not in fear, but in cold, calculating assessment. The fox standing in front of her stopped being her partner. He stopped being the mammal who had held her paw in the barn. The mask that fell over his face was smooth, hard, and entirely devoid of empathy. It was the face of a street hustler cornered in an alley, looking out only for himself.

He let out a short, cynical laugh that barely carried over the storm. It was a sound that made Judy's blood run cold.

"An operation?" Nick said. The lazy, mocking drawl was back, sharper and crueler than she had ever heard it. "Come on, Carrots. You're smarter than that."

"Nick, don't do this."

"You wanted to believe the best in me. That's your fatal flaw. You always see what you want to see." He looked down at the metallic case in his paw, then back at her. His eyes were flat, dead chips of green glass. "Predators don't change, Carrots. It was always about the money."

The words hit her harder than a physical blow. The air rushed out of her lungs. 

For three years, she had defended him against mammals who said exactly those words. She had fought for him, trusted him with her life, let him into the deepest, most vulnerable parts of her heart. And in three seconds, in the pouring rain, he had confirmed every warning she'd spent years fighting.

He wasn't under duress. He wasn't working an angle. He had just sold her out. 

Judy's finger rested heavily on the trigger. Her vision blurred, and this time, it wasn't just the rain. 

---

Then, three things happened in a span of less than a second. 

The white vixen moved. It wasn't a panicked flinch; it was a blur of calculated, tactical speed. Her hand whipped out of her pocket, tossing a small, cylindrical object backward into the shadows of the open warehouse doors. In the same fluid motion, she grabbed Nick by the lapels of his jacket and hauled him violently backward, pulling them both into the darkness behind a stack of industrial chemical drums. 

Judy tightened her finger on the trigger, prepared to fire into the space where they had just been.

She never got the chance.

The diversionary charge detonated. But it didn't just release a flash of light or a cloud of smoke. The spark found something in the shadows—a leaking valve, a pool of unstable industrial solvent left to rot for a decade. 

The air itself seemed to catch fire.

There was a sound like the sky tearing open, a deafening, bass-heavy roar that bypassed Judy's ears and vibrated directly in her teeth. A shockwave of solid force punched outward from the warehouse. 

Judy was thrown backward like a ragdoll. She hit the wet concrete hard, the breath driven entirely from her lungs, her tranq pistol skittering uselessly away into the dark.

For several seconds, there was only a high, persistent ringing in her ears and the taste of copper in her mouth. 

She forced her eyes open. The world was spinning, bathed in a violent, flickering orange light. The rain was still falling, but it hissed and turned to steam before it hit the ground. Where the loading bay had been, where Nick and the vixen had been standing just moments ago, there was only a towering inferno.
The chemical drums were cooking off in secondary explosions, sending pillars of green and yellow fire fifty feet into the air. 

*Nick.* 

The rational part of her brain—the detective, the officer who had just been betrayed—knew what he was. Knew he was a traitor. 

But her heart didn't care. The instinct bypassed all logic, overriding the pain in her bruised ribs. *He's in there. He's burning.*

Judy scrambled to her paws, stumbling, coughing on the toxic black smoke rolling off the water. She took one step toward the roaring flames. Then another. The heat was blistering, singeing the tips of her fur, but she couldn't stop. 

"Nick!" she screamed, the sound tearing her throat raw. It couldn't end like this. Not him. Not like this.

She lunged forward, ready to throw herself into the fire.

A massive pair of arms clamped around her waist like a vice, lifting her entirely off the ground.

"I got you! I got you, Jude!" 

Fangmeyer. The tiger's voice sounded distant, distorted by the ringing in Judy's ears. Fangmeyer hauled her backward, fighting against Judy's desperate, thrashing kicks. 

"Let me go! Fang, let me go! He's in there!" Judy clawed at the tiger's forearms, fighting with a wild, feral strength. The smoke and the tears blinded her. "Nick! Nick!"

"It's gone, Judy! The whole structure is gone! Look at it!" Fangmeyer dragged her behind the safety of a rusted shipping container, shielding the rabbit with her own body as another section of the warehouse roof collapsed in a shower of sparks. "You can't save him!"

"No! No, no, no, please!" Judy thrashed against the tiger's grip, her voice breaking into a ragged, guttural sob. "He didn't... he was right there... Fang, he was right there!"

She fought until her muscles simply gave out. The adrenaline crash hit her all at once, leaving nothing but an empty, echoing void. She collapsed against Fangmeyer, sliding down the wet metal of the shipping container until she hit the muddy concrete.

In the distance, over the roar of the fire, the first wail of emergency sirens began to cut through the storm. 

Fangmeyer knelt beside her, ignoring the mud, wrapping her large paws protectively around the trembling rabbit. The tiger looked at the burning warehouse, her own face illuminated by the destructive orange glow.

Judy stared at the flames. Her violet eyes reflected the fire, but they were wide, fractured, and entirely uncomprehending. The image of Nick’s face—cold, dead, telling her that predators never change—was burning behind her eyelids, overlaying the actual fire in front of her.

"He... he said..." Judy choked out, her whole body shaking violently against Fangmeyer's chest. She couldn't finish the sentence. She couldn't make it real. She just kept shaking her head, staring into the inferno. "He said..."

"I know, Jude. I know." Fangmeyer held her tighter, resting her chin gently on the top of the rabbit's ears. "I've got you."

The sirens grew louder, drowning out the storm, as the fire consumed everything she had left.

---

Forty-five minutes later, the harbor district looked like a war zone. 

The rain had reduced to a steady, miserable drizzle, but the red and blue strobes of a dozen ZPD cruisers painted the wet asphalt in frantic, overlapping colors. Fire trucks from the Zootopia Fire Department blocked the main access road, their heavy hoses snaking across the docks to drown the smoldering skeleton of Warehouse 4. The air was thick with the bitter, chemical stench of extinguished fire and melted plastic.

Judy sat on the lowered tailgate of a paramedic ambulance. A foil thermal blanket was draped over her small shoulders, but she wasn't shivering from the cold. She was simply staring at the flashing lights, her eyes hollow, disconnected from the chaos around her. Fangmeyer stood rigidly beside her, arms crossed, a silent barrier between Judy and the rest of the world.

Twenty yards away, Chief Bogo was a mountain of authority in the center of the storm.

"I want a two-block perimeter established immediately," Bogo barked, pointing a massive hoof at a pair of rhino officers. "Nobody gets in or out without my authorization. Get Hazmat down here to test the water runoff—God knows what was in those drums. And keep those reporters back behind the barricades, or so help me, I will start locking them up for obstruction!"

He dismissed them with a wave and turned. His broad shoulders slumped just a fraction of an inch as his gaze fell on the ambulance. 

He walked over slowly, his heavy boots splashing in the puddles. Fangmeyer stiffened, but Bogo held up a hand.

"Stand down, Officer," Bogo said quietly. He looked down at the rabbit. "Hopps."

Judy didn't look up immediately. When she finally raised her head, her face was smeared with soot, her fur matted and singed at the tips. 

"Fangmeyer gave me the preliminary sit-rep," Bogo said, his deep voice uncharacteristically gentle. "She told me what you saw. The keys. The handoff." He paused. "Are you injured?"

"No," Judy said. Her voice was flat, mechanical. "I'm fine."

Bogo nodded slowly. He looked past her, toward the smoking crater where the warehouse used to be. Forensics teams in white hazmat suits were already picking their way through the fringes of the wreckage. 

"They found something near the blast epicenter," Bogo said softly. He didn't look at her as he said it. "A piece of a ZPD badge. The serial number matched. Given the heat of that fire, Hopps... there won't be much else to find."

Judy didn't flinch. She just tightened her grip on the thermal blanket. She already knew Nick was dead. Having the physical proof didn't make the hole in her chest any larger; it was already bottomless.

Bogo finally looked down at her, his expression tightening into something resembling grim sympathy. 

"Listen to me carefully, Hopps. What happened tonight was a tragedy. But tomorrow, it becomes a political nightmare. You were his partner. You were supposed to be on a train to a training seminar out of state, yet here you are, at the scene of his... betrayal." 

Judy's ears pinned back. "I was tracking him, Chief. I knew something was wrong."

"I believe you," Bogo interrupted firmly. "But Internal Affairs won't. They're going to tear your life apart looking for a reason why you didn't call this in sooner. They're going to ask if you were covering for him. They're going to ask why you were alone. It's going to get ugly, and it's completely out of my hands."

He reached out and briefly, heavily, placed a hoof on her shoulder. 

"Go home, Judy. Get some sleep. Tomorrow, you're going to need your strength." He looked up at the tiger. "Fangmeyer. Take her home. Don't leave her alone tonight."

"Yes, Chief," Fangmeyer said instantly. 

Bogo gave Judy one last look, a mixture of pity and dread, and turned to walk back to the chaos of the crime scene.

"Chief," Judy said.

The word was quiet, barely carrying over the sound of the rain and the distant sirens, but it stopped the massive buffalo in his tracks. He turned back slowly.

Judy was staring at her own paws, curled tightly in her lap. Her voice was steady now, but completely empty.

"I had him in my sights," she whispered to the wet asphalt. "Before the explosion. My finger was on the trigger. I could have stopped it. I could have shot him." 

She finally looked up, meeting Bogo's eyes. The violet in hers was dull and fractured. 

"But I couldn't do it."

Bogo stared at her for a long moment. He didn't offer empty comfort. He didn't tell her it was going to be alright. He just looked at an officer whose heart had broken her own protocol.

"I know, Hopps," Bogo said quietly. "I know."

He turned and walked away into the flashing lights, leaving the rabbit sitting in the ruins of a life that had just been burned to the ground.

---

Forty-eight hours later, Internal Affairs was painted in clinical white.

There were no windows. The hum of the ventilation system was the only sound, save for the rhythmic, irritating *tap-tap-tap* of a metal pen against the metal table.

"Let's review the timeline again, Detective Hopps," Agent Ronson said. He was a badger, wearing a cheap suit that smelled faintly of stale coffee. He didn't look up from the file.

"We've reviewed it three times," Judy said. Her voice was raw. She hadn't slept since the docks. She was still wearing the same uniform pants, though she had changed her shirt. "I followed him from the Civic Hub. I observed the exchange. I confronted him. The warehouse exploded."

"Yes. You confronted him," the second agent said. Agent Vance. A wolf with a scar over his lip and a voice like grinding stones. "Without calling for backup."

"I called Officer Fangmeyer."

"You called a friend," Vance corrected, leaning across the table. "You didn't call dispatch. You didn't request a tactical unit. You didn't even use your radio."

"I needed to know what I was looking at before I—"

"What you were looking at?" Ronson interrupted, finally looking up. His eyes were perfectly flat. "You were looking at your partner handing over stolen municipal encryption keys to an unidentified criminal operative. What was there to misunderstand, Detective?"

Judy clenched her jaw. Her paws were resting on the table, shackled together by invisible exhaustion. "I wanted to be sure."

Vance scoffed, pushing off the table. He began pacing behind her chair. "She wanted to be sure, Ronson. Isn't that interesting? Let's talk about the Z-Academy seminar. You were supposed to be on a train out of the city that afternoon. A highly coveted spot. But you didn't go."

"I felt sick," Judy lied automatically.

"You felt sick," Vance repeated, stopping right behind her ears. "Or did you stay behind to provide overwatch for your partner's little side-hustle?"

Judy turned around so fast her chair scraped violently against the floor. "Excuse me?"

"You heard me," Vance snapped back, stepping into her space. "Three years you two rode together. Three years of closed-door meetings, off-the-books informants, bending the rules. You expect us to believe he planned a city-wide infrastructural compromise and you had *no idea*?"

"He lied to me!" Judy shouted, the anger finally cracking through the numb shell of grief. She slammed her paws on the metal table, startling Ronson. "He lied to everyone!"

"Then why didn't you shoot him?" Ronson asked quietly.

The room went dead silent.

"Forensics found your tranquilizer pistol in the mud, Detective," Ronson continued, sliding a photograph across the table. It showed her weapon, tagged and bagged. "The safety was off. There was a round in the chamber. The firing mechanism was in perfect working order. You had a clear line of sight, and a hostile suspect with stolen city assets. But you didn't fire."

Judy stared at the photo. Her throat closed up.

"Did your gun jam, Detective Hopps?" Vance asked, his voice dripping with venom. "Or were you just waiting for him to get clear before you fired a warning shot into the dirt to make it look good?"

"I didn't..." Judy stammered, the fury suddenly abandoning her. "I was..."

"You were protecting him," Vance stated as a fact.

"No! I was trying to stop him! He sold this city!"

Ronson sighed heavily and closed the file. He exchanged a look with Vance.

"We have the forensic report from the blast site," Ronson said, his tone shifting from accusatory to coldly administrative. "Dental records and partial DNA from the biological remains recovered near the epicenter. Detective Nicholas Wilde is officially declared deceased."

Judy stopped breathing. Hearing it from Bogo was one thing. Hearing it formalized, stamped, and filed in an administrative office made it final. 

"Given the evidence of your personal involvement, your failure to report a crime in progress, and your refusal to follow ZPD engagement protocols," Ronson continued, pulling a piece of paper from his jacket, "you are hereby stripped of your badge and your firearm, pending a full departmental inquiry. You are suspended without pay, effective immediately."

Vance stepped forward, holding out his hand. 

"Badge and gun, Hopps."

Judy looked at the hand. Then she looked at the white wall. 

The badger's pen tapped against the table. *Tap-tap-tap*. A dry metronome to the end of her old life.

Everything she had built. Everything she had loved. Gone.

Judy reached slowly for her belt.
