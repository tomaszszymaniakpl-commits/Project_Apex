# CHAPTER 17B — The Breaking Point (Part 2)

*Midpoint — The Trap (Judy POV). This chapter steps back in time, then catches up and continues.*

The tactical van hit a pothole hard enough to rattle Judy's teeth.

Red emergency wash strobed through the windshield in pulses—reflections off buildings, flares from stalled intersections, the city's nervous system misfiring in plain sight. Somewhere to their left, a hydrant had blown; water sheeted across three lanes and turned the asphalt into a mirror for spinning lights.

Wolford drove like he hated the road. His paws were locked at ten and two, knuckles pale under fur. The engine growled, low and constant, a mechanical promise that they were still moving when half of Zootopia had decided to stop.

In the back, McHorn checked the breach shotgun again—unnecessary, compulsive, the kind of repetition mammals used when thinking too much could kill them.

Leni sat with her rifle cased, muzzle down, harness buckles clicking softly as the van swayed. Her eyes were forward, not frightened—focused in a way that made the cramped space feel smaller.

Judy didn't let herself look at the red dispatch tablet mounted dead in its cradle. Networks were lying today. Paper didn't.

She unfolded a printed sector map across her knees—SyncPoint marked in pencil, Canal approach routes scratched in the margins, a circle around the loading bay where Wolford had argued for a clean vehicle egress.

"Okay." Judy blew out a breath she hadn't realized she'd been holding. "Guys—listen. I know how this looks."

Three heads turned. Wolford's eyes stayed on the road; his ears swiveled back anyway.

"We're not traffic control tonight. We're not rescue. We're not doing Bogo's all-hands thing." Judy tapped the pencil mark once, quick, like touching wood. "SyncPoint. Canal—that's where the pattern points if they're really moving on that hardware. It's a strong bet. It's not a guarantee. We could roll up and get nothing but alarms and locked doors, and we still have to be right anyway. So we don't spend the ride deciding how it ends. We watch. We move on what we see."

McHorn grunted agreement.

"On the ground it's us." Judy nodded toward Wolford and McHorn. "No movie stuff—no kicking doors for the trailer. We stack smart, we split them if we can, we don't give them one big target."

She looked at Leni.

"I need you on the roof when we're set—not because I want a shooting gallery." Judy's voice tightened, then steadied. "I need eyes. Windows, movement, a second team, anything that doesn't show on my angle. Call it out clean."

She held Leni's gaze.

"You don't fire unless I say fire. Not a warning shot, not a 'maybe'—me. I know that's… a lot of trust. I'm asking anyway."

Leni's ears flicked once. "Intel until you call it. Got it."

"Thank you," Judy said—too fast, too honest, and she didn't take it back.

Wolford's jaw tightened. "And if they come out shooting first?"

"Then we don't stand there and admire the view," Judy said. "But we don't start it on a guess. We start it on what we actually see."

The van slewed around a stalled bus, horn blaring uselessly behind them. The sound died against the windshield like it had been swallowed.

The radio crackled.

Not the encrypted channel.

The main band—open, busy, full of voices trying to sound like control and failing.

Then a different voice cut in—smooth, urgent, the SWAT-commander cadence Judy had heard in too many briefings to find reassuring.

"Hopps—Delgato. You copy?"

Judy's stomach dropped anyway.

Wolford's eyes flicked to her in the passenger seat. One second of warning.

Judy lifted the handset. Her paw almost shook. She steadied it. "Copy. Go ahead."

"Good." A beat of performative relief—like they were old partners, which they weren't. "I'm looking at a manifest pull. Heavy kit. No action packet. Nobody filed you. So either you're ghosts in my district, or you give me a location and a reason before I have to explain you to mammals who *really* don't like surprises."

Judy's mouth went dry.

She could name the building. She could ask for a medic cordon. The exhausted, honest part of her wanted *someone* in the chain to know where they were.

But the wrong ears had been ahead of them too often—ZIA showing up clean and fast, like someone had a tap on their work. If Delgato's request echoed onto the wrong console, SyncPoint wouldn't get help. It would get a jurisdictional knife fight while the real problem walked out the back.

"We're working Sector Four," she said, keeping it deliberately thin. "Grid's a mess—mammals stuck, accidents. Agencies are stretched."

Silence.

Delgato came back, still pleasant, the patience wearing thin at the edges. "Hopps—that's not a traffic vest. That's long guns. If you're Canal-side in a tactical loadout, I can get lanes cleared. I can get a bus staged—but I need a *real* address. Not a mood."

Judy stared at the windshield. Steam rose from a manhole cover ahead like the street was exhaling.

They didn't like each other. They'd never pretended otherwise. That made the false warmth in his voice easier to hear for what it was.

"I'm not putting coordinates on an open band," she said.

"Then give me a callback," Delgato replied—quick, reasonable, a little too easy. "Private. Your pick. I don't care how you dress it up. I need to know where my district's heavy gear is when the city is coming apart."

Judy let the silence hang a half-beat too long.

She wasn't giving him SyncPoint.

Not on the air. Not to him.

"I'm sorry," she said—automatic, the kind of sorry that meant *no.* "Can't."

"Can't—" Delgato started, and the pleasant mask slipped—just an edge, frustration punching through.

Judy rolled the volume down until his voice thinned, then clicked the radio off mid-word.

The cab went quiet except for engine and tires.

McHorn exhaled through his nose. "That sucked."

"Yeah," Judy whispered.

Leni didn't say anything. Her eyes stayed on Judy a beat longer than necessary—steady, unhappy, and still in.

Wolford drove faster anyway.

Through the gap between buildings, the Canal District opened up—gray water, black iron, old concrete chewing at the sky.

Judy folded the map along its creases and slid it into her vest.

"Five minutes," she said. "Gear check. Then we go dark on the open band."

Nobody argued.

The van took the next turn like it was trying to outrun the city itself.

***

Wolford killed the headlights a block early and let momentum do the rest—brake, roll, the big tires swallowing sound against wet asphalt.

He tucked them into the blind pocket behind a derelict textile mill: rust, broken windows, a loading dock that hadn't seen honest freight in years. The SyncPoint block squatted beyond a sagging fence line—brutal concrete, no glamour, canal reek threading through the wind.

Judy stepped out into air that tasted like oil and old water.

Her bare paws met cold asphalt with a soft padfall. No slamming doors. No jokes. The glitch was still screaming somewhere uptown; down here, the Canal District sounded like a held breath.

"Leni," Judy murmured into her encrypted mic. "You're going up."

Leni didn't answer with words. She just shouldered her case, scanned the mill's fire escape, and climbed—fast, quiet, the kind of ascent that only looked easy because she'd done it until her body stopped arguing.

Judy watched until Leni's silhouette merged with the roofline.

Then Judy moved.

No breach. No trailer kick. No fantasy of kicking in SyncPoint's ribs like a movie poster.

They owned the *outside*.

Wolford peeled wide, hugging shadow along the alley that could feed a getaway vehicle toward the nearest bridge on-ramp. McHorn planted at a choke point where a sedan couldn't slip past without showing paint and noise—bulk used like architecture.

Judy took the south sightline herself: loading lane, sealed bays, the dark seam along the building's flank where a service spine had been carved for maintenance routes the brochures never advertised.

If the package came out, it would have to come out *somewhere*.

Her scope caught windows—black glass, nothing friendly. A weather door at the far end, salt-crusted, barely visible unless you knew to look.

*Good,* Judy thought. *Be predictable.*

Her earpiece clicked.

*"On station,"* Leni breathed—so soft it could have been wind. *"I have the flank wall and the service span. Ramp line in view. Over."*

Judy lifted two fingers off her grip—useless gesture, habit—and clicked her mic once. Acknowledged.

They'd kept the shorthand stupid on purpose.

*Clear* meant: no hostile movement in your slice.

*Movement* meant: eyes on something alive.

*Hold* meant: nobody did anything stupid.

Judy wasn't sure which one would break first.

She pressed her shoulder into cold brick and scanned again. Window. Shadow. Steam rising off the canal in thin ribbons.

Nothing moved.

That was the point—and also the problem.

They didn't know what was happening inside. They didn't need to—not yet.

They needed to own the moment the building *coughed* its thieves into open air.

Judy exhaled through her nose, slow, counting without counting.

*"Ground team,"* she whispered into the channel. *"Check."*

Wolford clicked twice.

McHorn clicked once—deep, certain.

Judy stared at SyncPoint and waited for the world to make a mistake.

***

The building betrayed itself in pieces—never a confession, always a symptom.

A thin shiver ran through the weather door's seam: pressure change, like something large had stopped pretending to be asleep. Then a dull *thump*—not an explosion, not Hollywood—just metal remembering it could move.

Judy's scope never left the line.

Steam huffed out under the door in a white cough. Emergency strip-light from inside painted the vapor the color of infected tissue. For a heartbeat, the darkness had depth.

Leni's voice came in dry and fast. *"Movement. Service seam. I don't have interior—just silhouette churn."*

*Hold,* Judy whispered into the channel.

Not because she wasn't ready.

Because readiness wasn't the same thing as permission.

She forced her finger to stay alongside the trigger guard, not inside it. Her mind ran the ROE like a checklist she could bite down on: *see it, name it, then act.*

Another beat. A flash of blue-white light inside—reflection off something being cut, maybe—then gone, swallowed by concrete.

Then the door moved.

It opened wide enough for wind to steal the steam, and the world outside SyncPoint suddenly had *shape*.

Four figures.

Judy didn't know their names yet. She knew geometry.

Lead mass—reptile—low, aggressive, shoulders built for pushing through things that refused to move. Behind him, a fox she would have recognized in her sleep if she'd let herself: lean, ears pinned, eyes scanning like a cop even while he was stealing.

A white fox beside him—tactical weave, pale fur catching the canal light like a flare.

And a fourth—smaller, faster, harness glinting with tech that didn't belong in a dead building.

Between them: a case on heavy wheels, wrong height for luggage, wrong sound for anything innocent. It rolled with the obedient weight of something expensive enough to kill for.

They weren't running yet.

They were *transitioning*—from inside to outside, from plan to exposure—moving along a narrow steel spine Judy hadn't fully appreciated until she saw it used: a catwalk bolted to the flank of SyncPoint like an afterthought, grated, wet, rimmed with a pipe rail that had once been painted safety-yellow.

The canal breathed beneath it. The city screamed somewhere far away.

Up on the mill roof, Leni's scope would have had the perfect obscene angle: four bodies, one package, one line of flight.

Judy's mouth went dry anyway.

*"Wolford,"* she murmured. *"They're not coming out to shake hands. They're coming out to run."*

Wolford's voice came back clipped. *"Alley feeds the on-ramp. If they try wheels, they have to show me the nose of the vehicle."*

*"McHorn."*

*"Choke's still mine,"* McHorn rumbled. *"They try to punch through, they get a wall."*

Judy's eyes tracked the catwalk's end—where metal met loading concrete, where the thieves would have to turn from tight air into a world with corners.

Her brain snapped the map shut into a single clean line.

*They won't go back inside. They won't vanish upward—too exposed too long. They'll want speed and cover. They'll want the route that looks like maintenance and smells like escape.*

The catwalk didn't just connect places. It *aimed*.

*"Leni,"* Judy said softly. *"If they keep that line, they're feeding themselves into our jaws."*

*"Copy,"* Leni breathed. *"I have eyes on the span. I have eyes on the package. I have—"* a pause, the kind that meant her world had narrowed to a single terrible window—*"four targets, clean separation on the open span. Repeat: four. Package visible."*

Judy's heart hammered.

And still—still—she said it.

*"Hold."*

Not *fire.*

Not *now.*

Not while her blood screamed for something simpler than procedure.

Wolford clicked once—*acknowledged, unhappy*.

McHorn clicked once—*heavy patience*.

Leni didn't argue. That was worse. That was trust.

Judy swallowed the taste of copper and kept her eyes on the steel line where four mammals and a city's future were about to become *reachable*.

They had their trap.

They just hadn't closed it yet.

***

Precinct One still thought it could pretend to be a building.

Phones screamed. Dispatch boards rebooted and filled with red like a fever rash. Officers ran past each other in the emergency wash, voices stacking into a single wall of noise.

Benjamin Clawhauser's paws flew across his keyboard until the keys blurred. His headset chirped and chirped and chirped. His throat was dry. His uniform stuck to him in places he didn't want to think about.

He was doing his best.

He was also drowning.

A shadow fell across the desk—broad, tactical, smelling faintly of coffee and stress sweat.

Ben looked up.

Delgato looked like a mammal who'd been awake long enough to forget what sleep meant. His eyes were red at the rims. His smile was gentle in a way that made Ben's stomach twist anyway—not because it was fake, but because it was *kind*.

"Ben," Delgato said quietly—no performance, no crowd. "I need a minute."

Ben's ears flicked. His paws hovered over the keyboard. "I—I'm kind of underwater here—"

"I know." Delgato leaned in, voice low enough to slide under dispatch noise. "Hopps went dark on me. Off manifest. Heavy kit. Long guns. I'm trying to square the circle before someone upstairs decides we're all improvising."

Ben's mouth opened.

Closed.

Opened again.

*You do not tell anyone that location.*

Judy's whisper was still burned into his skull.

"I don't—" Ben heard himself say, too fast, the words tumbling out in the wrong order. "I don't know what you're—I mean, dispatch is crazy right now, I haven't—if she called I didn't—"

Delgato didn't interrupt. He didn't have to.

His gaze stayed on Ben's face—patient, sad, a little too understanding—and Ben felt the lie die before it even finished leaving his mouth.

"Okay," Delgato said softly, like he was letting Ben save face anyway. "I'm not trying to jam her badge in a door. I'm trying to keep mammals alive. The city's coming apart. If Hopps is Canal-side with that kit and something goes wrong, I can't stage medics. I can't clear lanes. I can't keep other units from walking into a friendly-fire triangle because nobody knows who's supposed to be where."

Ben swallowed. "She… she didn't want specifics on the air."

"I get that." Delgato's paw rested on the desk—not grabbing, not trapping. Just *there*. Solid. "But if I don't know where she is, I can't help her when she needs it. And Ben—" Delgato's voice cracked just enough to be human. "I'm scared for her. For all of them."

Ben's eyes stung.

He wasn't built for this.

He was built for kindness, for believing the best, for wanting everyone to get home.

Delgato leaned a fraction closer. "If you know something—anything—I'm not asking you to broadcast it. I'm asking you to let *me* be the one who makes sure help exists. Quietly. Cleanly."

Ben looked at the phones ringing.

He looked at the red icons.

He looked at Delgato's face—open, tired, *worried*—and felt something inside him fold.

*She'll understand,* Ben told himself, desperate. *She'll understand I was trying to protect her.*

"SyncPoint," Ben whispered, the word tasting like betrayal and hope at the same time. "Canal District. Research facility. That's—that's where they went."

Delgato closed his eyes for half a second—relief so real it looked like prayer.

"Thank you," he said.

He squeezed Ben's shoulder once, firm, brotherly.

Then he turned and walked away into the red-lit chaos—fast, purposeful, already talking into his shoulder mic before Ben could ask what he was going to do.

Ben stared after him.

For a moment, he felt lighter.

Then the phones rang again—and the lighter feeling curdled into something he couldn't name.

***

Back at SyncPoint, the wind off the canal didn't care about precincts or kindness.

Judy's scope filled with white.

Not snow. Not paperwork. A living flare—tactical weave, pale fur, the white fox walking the grating like it was a runway, one paw in front of the other, steady as insult.

The case rolled beside her. The reptile led. The other fox—lean, red, wrong—scanned like a predator doing math.

Judy's checklist tried to speak.

*Target one. Target two. Package.*

The words came out wrong inside her skull.

Something hot and stupid rose in her throat anyway.

*Take her down.*

Not a plan.

A need.

Her heart hammered so hard she tasted metal.

Leni's voice slid into her ear—dry, fast, the kind of calm that only existed when someone was dying to keep it.

*"Hopps—that's the package crew from the board. Positive ID. I have the case. I have Wilde on the span—red fox, no question. That's him."*

No hesitation in it.

No *maybe.*

*"Copy,"* Judy whispered—automatic, useless.

*"Hopps,"* Leni continued. *"I have a clean shot on the vixen. No cross through the package. No one behind her on the span—now."*

Judy's finger twitched.

*"But,"* Leni added, *"she's mid-catwalk. She takes a round up there, she doesn't just drop—she carries. Momentum. If she goes over the rail, that's water. That's steel. That's a kill."*

The canal breathed.

*"So—risk,"* Leni finished. *"High."*

Judy stared at the white fox in glass.

Somewhere behind her, a younger version of herself would have counted. Would have waited for the package to hit concrete. Would have split the group the way procedure promised.

That version was very small now.

Very far away.

*"Take her down,"* Judy heard herself say into the wire—too fast, too loud, the words bitten off like she was trying to tear something out of the world.

Leni's silence lasted half a second too long.

*"Hopps—confirm. That is a live fall risk. If you're asking for—"*

*"I gave you an order,"* Judy said.

Her voice didn't sound like hers.

*"I need you to confirm you're not—"*

The white fox moved.

The smile Judy remembered—sharp, mocking, *alive*—flashed on a muzzle that had no right to exist in her world anymore.

*"Leni."* Judy's paw crushed the mic button. *"Take her down. *Take her down*—now—*now*—"*

The words tore out of her throat—no procedure left, no shape, just the same stupid need hammering itself into sound.

Something in the canal-dark seemed to answer.

A rifle cracked from the mill roof—sound arriving late, the way thunder did.

On the catwalk, the white fox jerked.

For a heartbeat she looked almost surprised.

Then physics did what Judy had pretended not to understand.

The vixen spun.

The rail clipped her harness.

Her paw missed wet steel.

Gravity—stupid, honest, ancient—finished what spite had started.

She vanished.

Not a clean fall.

A tumble.

A scream Judy couldn't tell if she'd imagined.

Down into the black weave of pipes and ribbons of steam, where the canal waited for anything the city dropped.

Nick screamed something raw.

The reptile snarled *move*—

and then the night split into motion.

The case's wheels shrieked on wet grating.

The reptile didn't run like prey.

He *drove*—shoulder forward, jaws parted, the kind of forward motion that expected the world to break before he did. The smaller mammal stayed tight to his flank, harness straps flying, one paw on the case handle like it was a weapon in itself.

They hit the end of the catwalk and dropped onto loading concrete hard enough to spark.

McHorn was already there.

Not standing politely.

*Blocking*—mass planted at the choke where the maintenance lane narrowed to a throat the width of one desperate mammal.

The reptile didn't slow.

He tried to *go through*—a shoulder check with all his weight behind it, momentum and ego.

McHorn absorbed it like a wall that enjoyed its job.

Something heavy met something heavier.

The reptile bounced—actually *bounced*—and snarled, furious more than hurt.

Wolford came in from the angle Judy had prayed for all night—not flashy, not cinematic—just fast, low, using the alley's mouth like a vise. His rifle wasn't a toy; it was a line you didn't cross.

"Hands!" Wolford barked. "Off the case! *Now!*"

The smaller mammal's eyes flicked—measuring windows, measuring rail, measuring whether Leni still had sky—

*"Don't,"* Judy said.

She wasn't whispering anymore.

She was on the move herself, sidearm up, bare paws hammering concrete, closing the third face of the triangle until there wasn't a clean escape vector left—only bodies and barrels and the stupid bravery of mammals who thought they could still *negotiate* physics.

The reptile's gaze snapped to her.

Recognition lit behind cruelty.

"Rabbit," he hissed.

"On your knees," Judy said.

He laughed—short, ugly.

"You don't have enough."

He shifted his weight.

Not surrender.

*Feint*—like he might still try to bull through Wolford, might still drag the case into shadow, might still turn this into a story where he walked away.

McHorn didn't give him the room.

He drove in—close, brutal, professional—one massive paw stripping leverage, another finding bone and harness strap, forcing angles mammals weren't meant to hold.

The smaller mammal tried to break—

Judy was there.

Not a fair fight.

Not meant to be.

She didn't try to wrestle the reptile.

She *owned* the smaller one's space—muzzle, line of fire, the threat of a trigger break if a paw reached for anything smarter than air.

The smaller mammal froze halfway into motion, teeth bared, breathing hard.

"Don't," Judy repeated—quiet enough to be worse.

Wolford slammed the case sideways with his paw, pinning it to the ground like he was killing a snake.

The reptile roared—rage, not pain—and bucked anyway.

For a heartbeat Judy believed him.

Believed he might actually tear free.

Then McHorn's weight *settled*—full, final, the kind of pressure that turned struggle into theater.

He still didn't beg.

He spat curses instead—low, vicious, the sound of someone who would rather die on his feet than admit the field had beaten him.

The smaller mammal hissed something in a language Judy didn't catch.

Wolford answered with ZPD plain English. "Shut it. Hands behind your head. *Now.*"

Zip-ties bit.

Judy's ears were ringing.

She swept the loading lane anyway—case, reptile, harness, two prisoners—

Where was—

Her stomach dropped.

Nick was gone.

"Wilde," Judy snapped, spinning. "Where's *Wilde*?"

Wolford swore.

McHorn lifted his head—one second late, like he'd only just realized the count was wrong.

*"Leni,"* Judy barked into the mic. *"Nick—where is he?"*

Leni's voice came back steady and awful.

*"Nick jumped,"* she said. *"Followed the vixen down. He's trying to haul her out."*

Judy was already running.

"Judy—wait—" Wolford shouted behind her.

She didn't slow.

"Dammit," Wolford snarled—too far back, too late.

Brick blurred past her shoulder.

The service seam screamed open.

Her paws hit slick concrete.

*Nick.*

The name arrived like a knife.

She didn't know if she hated him more for being alive—

—or for making her remember she still had a heart to break.

She didn't care.

She ran into the dark after him anyway.

***

The maintenance spine wasn't built for drama.

It was built for drainage, for pumps, for mammals who fixed things when tourists weren't looking—narrow, slick, lit by dying strips that made every shadow look like a verdict.

Water breathed somewhere below.

Steam hissed through joints.

Judy's paws found purchase where Nick's had slipped—she heard it before she saw him: the scrape of metal, a grunt, the wet drag of something heavy that wasn't cargo.

She rounded a bend and the world narrowed to two colors.

Red fur.

White fur.

Nick had the white fox half-lifted, half-crushed against his chest—one arm under her shoulders, the other trying to brace a harness strap that had become a noose for bad luck. Her muzzle lolled. Her eyes were slits. Blood darkened pale fur in a ribbon Judy didn't want to name.

Nick's ears snapped toward Judy anyway.

Cop ears.

Always listening for the wrong kind of silence.

"Judy—" His voice broke clean through the noise in her head. "Judy, you have to—"

She didn't let him finish.

Couldn't.

If he spoke, something inside her would try to *listen*—and listening felt like treason now.

"Hands," Judy said. The word came out flat. "Show me your hands. *Now.*"

Nick's gaze flicked down—to the vixen, to the drop of water threading past his paws, to Judy's sidearm like it was the only honest thing left in the world.

"Please," he said—too fast, too human. "Just—listen for one second—"

"No."

She moved.

Not brave.

Not clean.

Just *fast*—the way training made you when you stopped being a mammal and became a vector.

Nick tried to shift the weight—

and that was the mistake.

You couldn't pivot like a fighter with a body in your arms. You couldn't feint. You couldn't be clever when gravity wanted you stupid.

Judy drove into his space, low, shoulder-first, the kind of hit that wasn't meant to hurt forever—just meant to *unbalance*.

Nick hit the grate.

The white fox slipped.

He reached—

Judy didn't give him the second grab.

She was on him before thought, knee pinning harness webbing, one paw wrenching his wrist behind his back in a lock that was all ZPD academy and none of her heart. Her cuffs snapped out like teeth.

Nick snarled—pain, fury, something rawer underneath that sounded almost like begging.

"Judy—"

Metal clicked.

"Stop talking," Judy said into his ear.

Her voice shook.

She hated that it shook.

"You don't get to—" Nick's muzzle scraped concrete. "You don't get to do this blind—"

"Blind?" The word came out like a laugh that wasn't a laugh. "I see *fine*, Nick."

"Then you're wrong—" he gasped. "You're wrong about—"

There were a thousand ways he could have finished that sentence.

He didn't choose a single one that would have saved him.

Not because he was stupid.

Because the truth wasn't *safe* on his tongue—not with microphones that might exist, not with ears that might be listening, not with the world still balanced on a lie big enough to swallow a city.

Judy heard the gap anyway.

Heard him swallow the rest like a blade.

"Shut up," she whispered. "Shut up, *traitor*."

Nick went still.

Not surrender.

Something worse.

Like she'd cut a wire and watched the light die anyway.

Behind her—heavy pawfalls.

Heavy.

Fast.

Wolford skidded to a stop in the steam, rifle still up, chest heaving. His eyes took in Nick on the ground, Judy on top, the white fox crumpled at the edge of the grate like discarded hope.

"Status," Wolford snapped.

"Wilde's cuffed," Judy said—too loud, too bright. "Vixen's down. She's breathing—I think—"

Wolford was already moving.

He dropped beside the white fox with the ugly efficiency of someone who'd learned first aid because the job demanded it, not because it made him gentle. Fingers at throat. A hiss through teeth.

"Pulse—weak," Wolford muttered. "We need her out of this wet *now*."

Nick's head turned—just a fraction—like instinct tried to follow even when his body couldn't.

Judy shoved him down again without meaning to.

"Don't," she said.

Nick's eyes met hers.

Green.

Hurt.

*Hers.*

Judy looked away first.

"On your feet," she told him.

She hauled him up by the cuffs until his legs remembered how to be legs.

Wolford lifted the white fox with a grunt—careful, brutal, the way you moved a casualty when gentle wasn't on the menu.

They climbed back toward the loading lane in a grim little procession: Wolford ahead with the body, Judy behind with the prisoner, steam licking their ankles like the canal wanted something back.

When the night air hit, Judy tasted iron.

McHorn's voice rose somewhere to her left—short, satisfied, the sound of mammals still on the ground where they'd been told to be.

The case sat pinned.

The reptile knelt.

The smaller mammal knelt.

And Judy walked Nick Wilde out into the open like a trophy she didn't want to win.

***

*"Leni—status,"* Judy murmured into her mic, and the loading yard rose up around her answer whether she wanted it or not.

Steam off the canal. The MSC case grounded and wrong in the way expensive things looked when they finally stopped pretending to be legal. McHorn on the reptile. Wolford on the smaller mammal. Nick cuffed beside her—bloodless outside, ruined somewhere deeper. The white fox on an emergency blanket strip, harness cut, breath shallow, alive by inches.

Judy's eyes swept the roofline anyway.

Habit.

Survival.

Leni's voice came back dry and immediate. *"Clear. Overwatch green. Nothing on the roofline."*

Judy exhaled once—sharp, involuntary.

*"Copy,"* she said.

"Package is static," Wolford said quietly. "Nobody touches it but chain of custody."

McHorn didn't brag.

He just breathed like a mammal who finally had permission to believe the night could end.

Judy lifted her encrypted handset.

*"Clawhauser,"* she said into Precinct One's secure desk line—the one they'd agreed on before the world went wrong. *"You copy?"*

Static.

Then a familiar voice—too high, too human, too *relieved*.

*"Judy? Judy—oh thank goodness—"* Clawhauser sounded like he'd been crying and pretending he hadn't. *"I copy, I copy, the board is insane up here, but I copy—"*

*"I'm secure,"* Judy cut in—gentle, firm. *"Listen. SyncPoint. Canal District. We have the hardware. We have hostiles in custody."*

She paused only long enough to make it official.

For the record.

Couldn't.

***

On the mill roof, the wind tried to peel paint off rust and failed.

Leni lay prone behind the old HVAC spine, rifle packed, cheek still ghosted with scope-ring sweat. Her ears were forward—alert, professional, *alive*.

Her thumb rested near the transmit key.

She'd heard every word through the same wire—every careful name, every careful request for help.

Something unclenched behind her ribs anyway—small, stupid, human.

*We might actually get home,* she thought.

Then—

Footsteps.

Not wind.

Not a pigeon.

A mammal who knew how to walk without announcing itself until it was already too close.

Leni's head turned.

Her paw was already moving—

A figure stepped out of the stairhead shadow—tactical fabric, wrong calm, shoulders squared like the city below wasn't bleeding. Face half in darkness. A smile that didn't reach the eyes.

Leni's mouth opened.

"You?" she started. "What are you doing up—"

A hiss—sharp, fast, wrong—

metal—

air—

Leni's world cut to white noise.

***

Judy forced her voice back into procedure.

"You don't get to talk," she told the kneeling line—loud enough to be law, quiet enough to shake. "You don't get to pretend this was a heist. You turned the city into a weapon tonight. Mammals are hurt. Mammals are dying in hospitals because of your glitch. Traffic. Power. Fear."

Her throat tightened.

She didn't let it show.

"That's not politics," she said. "That's terror."

Vesper's ears didn't move.

Her eyes did—cold, evaluating, like she was watching a lab animal learn pain.

"Terror," he repeated softly—testing the word like a blade on skin. "Little rabbit… you have no idea what you just—"

"Save it," Judy snapped.

She was lying.

She wanted him to keep talking.

Nick stayed silent.

No quip.

No defense.

No performance.

Just green eyes fixed on some middle distance that wasn't the roof and wasn't her.

Like silence was the only safe country left.

Drakov's smile wasn't a smile.

It was a crack in his muzzle.

"You think fear is a charge sheet," he said—soft, almost thoughtful. "You think you can *name* what we took from you. But you don't even know what the city is yet. You're not holding justice, rabbit. You're holding a leash—and someone's always on the other end of it."

Vesper didn't look at Judy.

She looked at the case.

Like the night had always been about that cylinder and everyone else was noise.

"Keep talking," Judy said. Her voice didn't shake.

It should have.

It didn't.

Because if she stopped talking, she'd hear something worse than words—her own heart trying to pretend it still believed in—

Something stung McHorn's neck—small, fast, wrong.

He clawed at it—

then folded.

McHorn hit the ground so hard the sound wasn't one sound.

It was metal.

It was meat.

It was *wrong.*

Judy's sentence died in her throat.

"McHorn—" Wolford spun—instinct, partnership, the stupid bravery of mammals who still thought *closer* meant *safer.*

A shape moved in the blind space behind him—tactical fabric, silent steps, a silhouette that had never been part of their yard because it had never been part of *their* plan.

Wolford's rifle was half-lifted.

Too late.

An arm locked across his chest.

A forearm drove up under his chin.

A full nelson—clean, vicious, practiced—snapped Wolford's world down to breath and panic.

Judy saw the face in the same heartbeat Wolford did.

SWAT cut.

Commander's shoulders.

Eyes she'd only ever seen across briefing tables and bad radio days—never here, never *like this.*

Delgato.

Her mind refused for half a second.

"I'm sorry," he said—to Wolford, to the air, to a version of reality that required apology like it required oxygen. "Nobody was supposed to get hurt."

Delgato's forearm tightened.

Wolford's airway stuttered.

Judy's sidearm came up without her deciding.

"If you fire," Delgato said quietly, "I break his neck. You know I can."

Wolford's paw clawed at empty air. "Don't—Hopps—*don't you dare put it down—*"

Judy's finger trembled against the trigger guard.

Not fear.

Choice.

The kind that turned your stomach into a stone.

Judy heard herself make a sound—small, animal—then silence.

She set the pistol down.

The metal kissed asphalt like a verdict.

Hadn't expected a uniformed solution to step out of nowhere and hand them the night on a plate.

Drakov's gaze slid down like he was measuring a servant.

Delgato's smile didn't reach his eyes. "I'm not the one on my knees."

*No.*
