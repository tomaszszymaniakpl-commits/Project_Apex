# CHAPTER 2: Fox in a Rabbit Hole

The world outside the train station had turned impossibly green.
For the last hour, the steel and glass of Zootopia had bled away, replaced first by the industrial gray of the outskirts, and then by this—endless, rolling waves of emerald hills, broken only by the occasional oak tree or a farmhouse that looked like it had grown out of the earth itself.
Judy Hopps pressed her forehead against the cool glass, watching a tractor navigate a field of carrots that stretched to the horizon. Her nose twitched. Even through the train's filtration system, she could smell it: rich soil, sun-warmed grass, and the complete absence of smog.
Home.
She glanced at the seat beside her.
Nick Wilde looked like he was being transported to a maximum-security prison, not a harvest festival.
He was sitting perfectly still, his posture rigid. His tail, usually draped casually over his leg or flicking with amusement, was tucked tight beneath the seat. He was wearing a casual flannel shirt—Judy's suggestion, to blend in—but he wore it like a bomb disposal suit.
"You're doing it again," Judy said softly.
Nick didn't turn his head. His emerald eyes were fixed on a herd of sheep grazing in the distance.
"Doing what, Carrots?"
"The 'I'm a predator surrounded by prey and I'm afraid I might accidentally sneeze and scare everyone in a ten-meter radius."
Nick finally looked at her. The corner of his mouth twitched, attempting a smirk that didn't quite reach his eyes. They were lighter than usual—a pale, ozone green. Nervousness.
"I'm just admiring the scenery," he deflected, smoothing a wrinkle on his knee. "So much... open space. Where do you people hide? It's unnerving. In the city, if someone wants to mug you, you at least have an alleyway. Here, they'd have to chase you across a meadow for twenty minutes."
"Nobody is going to mug you, Nick. It's Bunnyburrow."
"Right. The mean streets of the Tri-Burrows. I've heard stories about the aggressive vegetable peddlers."
The train whistle blew, a cheerful, rustic sound that made Nick's ears flatten against his skull. The brakes hissed.
"We're here," Judy beamed, grabbing her bag. She nudged his shoulder. "Relax. They're going to love you."
Nick stood up, adjusting his sunglasses. "Or they're going to mount my head above the fireplace. 'Here lies Nicholas Wilde. He underestimated the blueberry pie.'"

The platform was a sea of ears.
Stepping off the train felt like diving into a moving carpet of fur. Hundreds of rabbits were swarming—reuniting with families, chasing kits, hauling crates of produce. The noise was a high-pitched cacophony of squeaks, chatter, and laughter.
And in the middle of it all, Nick was a beacon of red rust.
He walked close to Judy, his movements tight and controlled. He kept his paws visible at all times, shoulders hunched slightly to minimize his height. He was trying to take up less space than he had.
"Judy!"
The shout cut through the noise. Judy's ears swiveled.
"Dad! Mom!"
Stu and Bonnie Hopps stood near the ticket booth. Stu was clutching a hat in his paws, looking like he was bracing for impact. Bonnie was smiling, but her eyes were sharp, scanning the crowd until they landed on the fox.
Judy dropped her bag and ran, colliding with her parents in a messy, three-way hug.
"Oh, look at you!" Bonnie squeezed tight. "You look thin. Are you eating? I bet you're just living on coffee and adrenaline."
"I'm fine, Mom," Judy laughed, pulling back. She turned, waving Nick forward. He approached slowly, stopping a respectful three feet away.
"Mom, Dad... you remember Nick."
The silence stretched for a heartbeat. Stu stared at Nick. Nick stared at Stu.
Then, Nick slid his sunglasses down his nose and flashed a winning, if slightly brittle, smile. He extended a paw.
"Mr. Hopps. Mrs. Hopps. Thank you for having me. I promise not to eat all the pie."
Stu stared at the clawed paw. He blinked. Then, taking a visible breath, he reached out and shook it. His grip was firm—maybe a little too firm.
"Stu," he said, his voice jumping an octave before he cleared his throat. "Just Stu. None of that formal stuff. We're... we're happy you could come, Nick."
"Nicholas," Bonnie corrected, stepping forward. She didn't hesitate. She bypassed the handshake and patted Nick's arm. "Ignore him, he's just nervous. And look at those eyes."
Nick froze, caught off guard. "I... beg your pardon?"
"Green," Bonnie observed, tilting her head. "Like fresh clover. Judy mentioned they were green, but she didn't say they were that green." She winked at her daughter.
Judy felt the heat rush to the tips of her ears. Nick, for his part, looked like he'd been hit with a stun gun, but recovered with a chuckle.
"Well, thank you, B—Mrs. Hopps. I get them from my mother."
"Bonnie, dear. Come on, the truck is this way. We have to beat the traffic before the tractor parade starts."

Dinner at the Hopps farm wasn't a meal; it was a logistical miracle.
The main house had been expanded over generations, a sprawling warren of rooms, tunnels, and wooden add-ons, but the kitchen remained the heart. A massive oak table dominated the room, currently seating about forty rabbits. The rest were eating in the living room, the hallway, and probably a few in the rafters.
The noise was deafening. The smell was incredible—roasted root vegetables, fresh bread, blackberry cobbler.
Nick sat between Judy and her younger sister, Janet.
Judy watched him over the rim of her carrot juice. She had expected him to withdraw, to be overwhelmed by the chaos.
Instead, he was holding court.
"...so there we were," Nick was saying, gesturing with a forkful of salad. "Suspended over the Mystic Springs canal. And Judy says to me, 'Nick, do you trust me?' And I said, 'With my life, Officer Hopps, but maybe not with the grappling hook.'"
A dozen teenage rabbits leaned in, eyes wide. Janet laughed—a bright, musical sound that made Nick grin.
"You made that up," Janet teased, bumping his shoulder with hers. "Judy's the top of her class. She doesn't miss with grappling hooks."
"Oh, she didn't miss," Nick countered smoothly, turning his full attention to Janet. "She hit the target perfectly. The problem was what she hit. Turns out, gargoyles aren't always structural."
Janet roared with laughter, her paw resting casually on Nick's arm.
Something cold dropped in Judy's stomach.
She poked at her roasted parsnip. It wasn't fair, and she knew it. Janet was her sister. Nick was her partner. He was doing exactly what she wanted him to do—getting along.
But it was how he was doing it.
With Janet, he was easy. His shoulders were relaxed. He was charming, funny, light. He wasn't guarding his words. He wasn't watching for threats.
Why is he never that easy with me? Judy thought, the bitterness of the thought surprising her. With me, it's always layers. Jokes hiding worries. Banter hiding fear. Why can't we just... be like that?
She looked down at her plate. She suddenly felt very small in the crowded room.
"Judy?"
Bonnie's voice cut through her internal monologue. Her mother was standing at the counter with a tray of empty pie tins.
"Come help me in the kitchen for a second, sweetie? The boys will never figure out the dishwasher."
Judy nodded, grateful for the escape. She slid off the bench. Nick either didn't notice, or pretended not to. He was too busy showing Janet a card trick, making a spoon disappear and reappear behind her ear.

The kitchen annex was quieter, smelling of cinnamon and warm yeast. Judy started stacking dirty plates, scrubbing harder than necessary.
Bonnie leaned against the counter, wiping her paws on her apron. She watched Judy attack a stubborn spot on a casserole dish.
"He fits in better than I thought," Bonnie said softly.
"He's adaptable," Judy muttered, not looking up. "He's a hustler. It's what he does. He charms people."
"He charms some people," Bonnie corrected. "Your father is still watching him like he expects him to steal the silverware. But Janet? She's smitten."
Judy's sponge stopped moving. "Janet is friendly."
"Janet is flirting," Bonnie said bluntly. "And Nicholas is being polite."
Judy finally turned to look at her mother. "He seems pretty happy about it to me."
Bonnie sighed, the sound of a mother who has seen this drama play out in two hundred different variations. She walked over and took the sponge from Judy's hand, setting it down. She took Judy's wet paws in her own.
"Judy. Look at me."
Judy looked up. Bonnie's eyes were warm, intelligent, and uncomfortably perceptive.
"I told Janet to back off," Bonnie said simply.
Judy's sponge stopped. "You did what?"
"She asked if Nicholas was available. I said no."
"Mom!" Judy felt heat rush to her ears. "You can't just— we're not— he's not my—"
"Then I lied," Bonnie said, unmoved. "And you can go tell your sister he's fair game." She paused. "Go on."
Judy didn't move. Her paws gripped the edge of the sink.
"That's what I thought," Bonnie said softly.
Judy opened her mouth to argue—to cite regulations, to claim they were just partners. But the words died in her throat. She looked at her mother's knowing eyes and felt the defiance drain out of her ears.
She slumped against the sink, looking defeated.
"Fine," she whispered, her voice trembling. "But... it doesn't matter."
"Doesn't matter?"
"I don't even know if he feels the same way," Judy admitted, the insecurity rushing out now that the dam had broken. She stared down at the soapy water. "And why would he? Look at us, Mom. He's... him. He's sophisticated, he's clever. And I'm just a farm girl from Bunnyburrow. What could a fox like him possibly want with a rabbit like me?"
Bonnie smiled—a sad, affectionate curve of her lips. She turned Judy gently toward the doorway leading to the living room.
"Oh, my silly, brave girl. You really are blind, aren't you? Look at him."
Judy looked.
In the living room, the chaos was at its peak. Janet was laughing loudly, touching Nick's arm. Stu was looming nearby with a photo album. The noise was deafening.
But then, as if feeling the weight of their gaze, Nick stopped.
He looked up. He didn't look at Janet. He didn't look at the threats or the exits.
He looked right at Judy.
And just like that, the performance dropped. His ears perked up. His shoulders dropped an inch. The hard public green softened into something private.
"He's terrified, Judy," Bonnie murmured in her ear. "He's a fox in a burrow of three hundred rabbits. He's putting on a show because that's how he survives. But when he looks at you..."
Bonnie squeezed Judy's paws tight.
"...he looks at you like the room gets quieter when you're in it."
Judy felt her breath catch in her throat. She watched Nick offer her a tiny, barely visible smile—a real one, just for her—before turning back to charm her father.
"Okay," Judy whispered, a tear pricking the corner of her eye. "Okay."

The sun began to dip below the rolling hills, painting the sky purple and burnt orange.
The festival grounds were a riot of sensory overload. Strings of Edison bulbs zigzagged overhead like captured fireflies, casting a warm, amber glow over the crowd. The air was thick with the scent of roasted corn, spun sugar, and the earthy, sweet perfume of thousands of rabbits celebrating the harvest.
Nick Wilde stood in front of the "Ring-a-Carrot" booth, staring at a plastic hoop with the intensity of a bomb disposal expert.
"It's physics, Carrots," he muttered, adjusting his sunglasses, which were reflecting the carnival lights. "Simple aerodynamics. These rings are weighted incorrectly. It's a scam. A hustle. And frankly, I respect the artistry of it."
"You missed six times, Nick," Judy pointed out, leaning against the wooden railing. She was holding a bag of kettle corn and grinning. "Just admit it. You have no aim."
"I have impeccable aim. The wind shear in this valley is unpredictable."
He threw the seventh ring. It bounced off the wooden peg with a mocking clack and rolled into the dirt.
The teenager running the booth—a lanky buck with braces—snickered.
Nick sighed, smoothing down his flannel shirt. "Fine. You try, Officer Hopps. Show me how it's done."
Judy handed him the popcorn. She picked up a ring. She didn't calculate wind shear. She didn't check the aerodynamics. She just flicked her wrist.
Thwip.
The ring landed perfectly around the center peg.
"Beginner's luck," Nick deadpanned.
Thwip. Second ring. Same peg.
Thwip. Third ring.
The teenager's jaw dropped. He reached under the counter and pulled out the top prize—a plush carrot the size of a small toddler.
Judy accepted it with a theatrical bow, then shoved it into Nick's chest.
"For you," she said, her violet eyes dancing with mischief. "So you have something to hug on the train ride home."
Nick looked at the giant orange plush. Then he looked at her. The corner of his mouth curled up, and for a second, the mask slipped.
"You're insufferable," he said softly. But he tucked the plush carrot under his arm like it was a treasure.
The hours that followed blurred into a warm, hazy montage of gold and amber. As the sun finally surrendered to the horizon, the frantic energy of the afternoon softened. The loud cries of the booth-runners were replaced by the rhythmic chirping of crickets and the low, collective hum of thousands of rabbits winding down their day.
They wandered the grounds together, Nick's tail occasionally brushing against Judy's shoulder as the paths grew crowded with families heading toward the glow of the main farm.
When the evening chill began to settle over the valley, the Hopps family barn stood as a beacon of light against the darkening fields. It wasn't just a structure anymore; it was the heart of the Burrow, radiating a heat that promised something more intimate than the carnival's chaos.
The massive wooden doors were thrown open to the cool night air. Inside, the high rafters were draped with miles of twinkle lights and garlands of dried flowers. The floor, usually covered in straw, had been swept clean, though the scent of hay and old oak still lingered—a warm, rustic perfume that felt like a hug.
A band was playing on a raised platform in the corner—a lively fiddle, a rhythmic banjo, and a double bass that thumped in time with Judy's heart.
"Come on," Judy said, grabbing Nick's free paw (the other was still clutching the giant carrot).
"Whoa, easy there," Nick planted his feet. "Where are we going?"
"Dance floor."
"Absolutely not," Nick shook his head, his ears flattening. "I don't dance, Carrots. Foxes don't line dance. It's against the laws of nature. We lurk. We lean against walls looking mysterious. We do not do-si-do."
"We're not line dancing," Judy said, pulling harder. "The music is changing."
As if on cue, the frantic fiddle melody slowed. The banjo dropped out, replaced by a weeping, soulful violin melody. The rhythm shifted from a stomp to a sway. A waltz.
The crowd on the floor thinned out. Couples moved closer together.
"Judy," Nick warned, but there was no heat in his voice.
"One dance," she pleaded, stopping and turning to face him. She used the weapon she knew he couldn't fight—she looked up at him with wide, expectant eyes. "Please? For the harvest?"
Nick sighed, a long, dramatic exhale that ruffled his whiskers. He set the giant plush carrot on a bale of hay.
"One dance," he grumbled. "But if you step on my paws, I'm arresting you for assault."
He walked her onto the floor.
It was awkward for the first three seconds. The size difference was always the elephant (or rhino) in the room. But Nick solved it with a grace that surprised her. He dropped to one knee for a fleeting second to adjust his stance, then simply leaned down, offering his paws at her level.
Judy placed her paws in his. They were warm. Rougher than hers, with the faint, tactile scratch of claws she knew would never hurt her.
She stepped in. He took the lead.
And the world fell away.
They moved in a slow circle, in a slow orbit around each other. The music swelled—a haunting, nostalgic melody that sounded like memories of a time they never had. The golden light from the rafters caught the dust motes swirling around them, turning the air into a shimmering mist.
Someone holds me safe and warm...
The thought drifted through Judy's mind, unbidden.
She looked up. Nick was looking down.
He wasn't scanning the room anymore. He wasn't checking the exits. He wasn't performing for her parents or charming her sister.
His eyes were locked on hers.
The hard, defensive ozone-green was gone. In the soft amber light of the barn, his irises were the color of deep moss, of hidden forests, of something ancient and safe.
"You're staring," he whispered. His voice was a low rumble in his chest, vibrating against her paws.
"You're dancing," she whispered back.
"I'm improvising under duress."
"You're doing great."
He spun her gently. The barn blurred into streaks of gold and brown. For a moment, they weren't a fox and a rabbit. They weren't partners or cops. They were just two souls, tethered together in the center of a spinning world.
Judy felt a warmth spread through her chest, expanding until it made her fingertips tingle. Bonnie's words echoed in her ears: He looks at you like the room gets quieter when you're in it.
She tightened her grip on his paws.
"Nick?"
"Hmm?"
"Thank you."
He frowned slightly, his brow furrowing. "For what? The terrible aim? The risk of social humiliation?"
"For coming," she said, her voice trembling slightly. "For trying. For being here."
Nick stopped spinning. They were swaying now, barely moving, just rocking in place to the dying notes of the violin. He lowered his head until his muzzle was inches from hers. The playful smirk vanished, replaced by a raw, terrifying vulnerability.
"There's nowhere else I'd rather be, Carrots," he breathed.
The music faded to a single, sustained note.
The air between them crackled. It tightened, charged, and hard to breathe through. The sounds of the festival outside—the laughter, the distant chatter—sounded like they were underwater.
Judy stopped breathing.
Nick leaned in.
Slowly. Careful. Giving her a thousand chances to back away.
She didn't back away. She went up on her tiptoes.
She could smell him—cedar wood, coffee, and that sharp, distinct musk that was just Nick. She saw the individual flecks of gold in his green eyes. She saw her own reflection there, wide-eyed and waiting.
His nose brushed hers. A soft, wet touch that sent a jolt of lightning down her spine.
His eyes fluttered half-closed. His grip on her paws shifted, his thumbs brushing over her knuckles.
This is it, Judy thought, her pulse kicked prey-quick against her ribs. This is real.
Their lips were a breath apart.
BOOM.
The explosion shook the floorboards.
White light flooded the barn, bleaching the color out of the world.
Judy jumped, her instincts screaming danger, and she shoved Nick back on reflex. He stumbled, his ears snapping flat, teeth bared in a reflex snarl before his brain caught up.
CRACK-BOOM.
Another explosion. Then a cascade of whistles and pops.
"Oh! Look!" a voice shouted from the doorway. "The fireworks are starting!"
The spell shattered like a dropped mirror.
The music started up again—a fast, jarring reel. The crowd surged toward the big doors to watch the show.
Nick and Judy stood frozen in the middle of the empty dance floor, chest-heaving, staring at each other with wide, shocked eyes. The distance between them, which had been millimeters a second ago, now felt engineered and deliberate.
Nick blinked rapidly. The vulnerability vanished behind a wall of sudden, awkward panic. He cleared his throat, adjusting his collar.
"Right," he said, his voice cracking. "Fireworks. Loud. Very... festive."
Judy lowered her heels to the floor. Her face was burning. She wrapped her arms around herself, trying to hold onto the warmth that was rapidly fading.
"Yeah," she squeaked. "The... the finale."
"We should probably..." Nick gestured vaguely toward the door. "Go watch? Or... find the pie? I think I promised your mother I'd eat the pie."
He was rambling. He never rambled.
Judy let out a shaky breath. She stepped forward and reached out, grabbing his paw again. It was trembling.
"Nick," she said softly.
He stopped, looking down at her. The fear was back in his eyes—fear that he'd ruined it, fear that he'd gone too far.
Judy squeezed his paw. She didn't say I love you. Not yet. The words were too big, too heavy for a barn floor.
Instead, she smiled. It was a soft, tired, promising smile.
"Let's go watch the fireworks," she said.
Nick searched her face. He saw the promise there. He saw that she wasn't running away.
His shoulders relaxed. He turned his paw over, threading his digits between hers.
"Lead the way, Officer Hopps."
They walked to the barn doors together, hand in paw.
Outside, the sky was exploding in flowers of red, gold, and green. The colors reflected in their eyes—Nick's emerald, Judy's violet—mixing together in the dark.
They stood close. Their shoulders touched.
They had tomorrow. At least, that was the story she chose to believe.
