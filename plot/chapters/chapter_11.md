# Chapter 11: Ghosts of the Street

The alley behind the Palm Hotel in Sahara Square smelled like rotting fruit and exhaust fumes. At eight in the morning, the heat was already baking the asphalt, turning the narrow corridor into an oven. 

Judy wore a faded grey hoodie and dark jeans, her badge left behind in her apartment. Wolford stood ten yards back, leaning against a rusted dumpster in a plain black t-shirt, pretending to check his phone. He was a solid, unmoving shadow in the morning glare.

Finnick’s custom van was parked next to the hotel’s loading dock. The fennec fox was sitting on the rear bumper, counting a thick stack of bills with practiced, rhythmic flicks of his thumbs. 

Judy stepped out of the shadow of the fire escape.

Finnick’s ears twitched before he even looked up. He didn't jump. He just stopped counting, slid the money into his oversized jacket pocket, and let out a long, raspy sigh.

"I don't do parking tickets, Hopps," he said, his deep voice scraping like sandpaper. "And I don't do charity. So unless you're buying a counterfeit health inspector permit, you're blocking my sun."

"I'm not here for a permit, Finnick," Judy said, stopping a few feet away. She kept her voice low, conversational. "And I'm not here on the clock."

Finnick’s eyes flicked past her, landing on Wolford for a fraction of a second before snapping back to her. "Right. Just a casual morning stroll with a two-hundred-pound wolf. Very subtle."

"I need a name."

"I sell popsicles and fake IDs. I don't sell names."

Judy reached into her hoodie pocket and pulled out an envelope. She tossed it onto the bumper next to him. It landed with a heavy, solid thud. Five hundred dollars. Her own money.

Finnick looked at the envelope. He didn't touch it.

"Someone is moving heavy infrastructure hardware through the districts," Judy said, watching his face. "Relays, pressure boards, high-end utility tech. They're using shell contractors. And they’re being led by a white arctic fox. Female. A ghost."

Finnick’s hand, which had been resting casually on his knee, slowly curled into a fist. The movement was tiny, but Judy caught it. 

"I don't know any ghosts," he said. His voice had lost its usual cynical bounce. It was flat. Dead.

"Finnick, listen to me—"

"No, you listen." Finnick slid off the bumper. He didn't grab the envelope. He didn't even look at it again. He just turned and hit the button to roll down the van’s rear shutter. "I'm closed."

Judy stepped forward, putting her paw on the metal shutter before it could close. "Nick is alive."

The words hung in the hot, stagnant air. 

Finnick froze. For a long, agonizing second, he didn't move. His back was to her. When he finally turned around, there was no surprise in his eyes. No relief. Just a cold, hard exhaustion.

"I know," Finnick said quietly.

Judy’s breath hitched. "You knew? And you didn't tell me?"

Alive meant hope for one part of her and a target for the rest. She buried both before Finnick could read either one.

"Tell you what, Hopps? That the fox I hustled with for ten years is running with a crew that makes the local syndicates look like a boy scout troop?" Finnick stepped closer, his voice dropping to a harsh whisper. "You think this is a game? You think this is some street hustle you can badge your way out of?"

"I'm not wearing a badge today."

"That doesn't make you invisible. It just makes you a target." Finnick looked past her again, scanning the rooftops and the mouth of the alley. His ears were pinned flat against his skull. "You're asking the wrong questions about the wrong people."

"I need to find them, Finnick. I need to find *him*."

"You find them, you die. Simple math." Finnick reached out, grabbed the envelope of cash, and shoved it hard into Judy’s chest. "Keep your money. I want no part of this."

Judy didn't take the envelope. It fell to the asphalt between them. "They hit a sub-station yesterday. They're planning something bigger. The street always knows who's moving the freight."

Finnick grabbed the edge of the shutter, his knuckles white. He looked at her, and for the first time since she had met him, Judy saw genuine, unfiltered fear in the fennec’s eyes.

"On the street, you ask a question about a white ghost exactly once," Finnick said, his voice barely audible over the hum of the city traffic. "You ask it a second time... and nobody ever picks up your calls again. Because you're already dead."

He yanked the shutter down. It slammed shut with a metallic crash that echoed off the brick walls. 

"Go home, Hopps," Finnick said through the metal grating. "Before you get that wolf killed, too."

The engine roared to life. The van’s tires squealed against the pavement as Finnick threw it into gear, tearing out of the alley and disappearing into the morning traffic.

Judy stood alone in the heat, the envelope of cash lying in the dirt at her feet. 

Wolford pushed off the dumpster and walked over. He looked down at the money, then up at the empty mouth of the alley.

"Well," Wolford muttered, his voice grim. "That went well."

Judy slowly bent down and picked up the envelope. The paper was already grimy. 

"He didn't take the money," she said softly.

"I noticed."

"Finnick never walks away from money." Judy looked at Wolford, her jaw tightening. "They're terrified, James. Whoever these people are... they own the street."

Wolford nodded slowly. "So what's the play? We don't have a name."

"We don't need a name from the street," Judy said, turning toward the exit of the alley. "We need a paper trail. Tell Fangmeyer to start pulling the utility subcontract logs. If they're moving heavy hardware, they're leaving a digital footprint."

"And us?"

"We find the people who don't scare as easily as Finnick."

By two in the afternoon, the city felt like it was breathing through a hot, wet towel. 

In an unmarked sedan parked near the edge of the Canal District, McHorn shifted his massive bulk against the driver's seat. The suspension groaned in protest. He was wearing a civilian button-down that was already plastered to his fur with sweat, the air conditioning struggling and failing to keep up with the greenhouse effect of the windshield.

"White cab, flatbed trailer," Fangmeyer said from the passenger seat. Her voice was a low, steady hum. She didn't lower the binoculars. "License plate Tango-Seven-Niner-Bravo. Matches the manifest for the Rainforest District routing."

McHorn grunted, tapping the plate number into a ruggedized tablet resting on his knee. "Registered to Apex Logistics. Clean DOT record. Taxes paid. Drivers have no priors."

"Too clean," Fangmeyer murmured. A drop of sweat traced a path down her striped cheek, but she didn't blink. "They've moved four tons of industrial cabling in the last three hours, and every single piece of paperwork is flawless. Nobody on the docks is this perfect unless they're hiding something."

"We can't pull them over for having good handwriting, Nadine."

"I know." Fangmeyer finally lowered the binoculars, rubbing the bridge of her nose. The faint smell of hot vinyl and frustrated predator filled the cabin. "Keep logging. They have to make a mistake eventually."

Across the city, in the rusted husk of an abandoned industrial park in Sahara Square, Judy and Wolford were finding exactly nothing.

The office of 'Meridian Sub-Contracting' was a single-story concrete block at the end of a dead-end road. The door hadn't been locked; it had been left hanging open, swaying slightly in the dry wind. 

Judy stood in the center of the empty room. There were no desks. No chairs. No filing cabinets. The air smelled of stale dust and the sharp, chemical tang of industrial bleach. 

"Scrubbed," Wolford said, his boots crunching softly on the bare concrete. He ran a claw along the edge of the window sill. "Not just moved out. Sanitized. They even wiped down the light switches."

Judy’s ears swiveled, catching the faint hum of distant highway traffic. She walked back outside, her eyes scanning the cracked pavement of the loading bay. 

"James. Look at this."

Wolford stepped out, squinting against the glare. Judy was pointing to a set of tire tracks pressed into a patch of dried mud near the loading ramp.

"Dual-axle," Wolford noted, crouching down. He sniffed the air near the tracks. "Heavy load. The suspension was bottomed out when they pulled away. Fresh, too. Maybe twenty-four hours."

"They were here," Judy said, her voice tight. "They used this place as a staging ground, and the second we hit the sub-station yesterday, they burned it and vanished."

Before Wolford could answer, Judy's burner phone vibrated in her pocket. 

She pulled it out. A single encrypted text message from Leni: *Get to a secure line. Now.*

Ten minutes later, Judy and Wolford were sitting in the sweltering cab of Wolford's personal truck, the windows rolled up, the engine off. Judy held the phone to her ear.

"Talk to me, Leni."

*“I’ve been digging through the telemetry logs from Sub-Station 12,”* Leni’s voice crackled through the speaker, underscored by the heavy, rhythmic hum of cooling fans in her garage. *“The physical breach was clean, but the digital side... Judy, it’s a masterpiece.”*

"Give it to me in English."

*“They didn't just smash the firewall. They picked the lock from the inside out. It’s a handshake bypass. A Cerberus ghost-loop variant.”* Leni paused, and Judy could hear the clatter of a keyboard. *“It tricks the system into thinking the diagnostic override is coming from the central dispatch server. By the time the local security realizes it’s a breach, the intruders already have root access and the cameras are blind.”*

Judy frowned, exchanging a look with Wolford. "Okay. They have good hackers. We knew that."

*“You're missing the point,”* Leni said, her voice dropping an octave. *“I ran that specific code signature against the municipal incident database for the last three months. The 'random' outages we’ve been seeing? The water pressure drop in Tundratown two weeks ago? The rolling blackout in the Canal District last month?”*

Judy’s grip on the phone tightened. "Same signature."

*“Identical,”* Leni confirmed. *“Down to the millisecond timing. It’s the exact same ghost-loop. Three different incidents, three different utility sectors. All using the same digital fingerprint.”*

The heat inside the truck suddenly felt very heavy. 

"They aren't just stealing hardware," Judy said softly, the realization clicking into place like a loaded magazine. 

*“No,”* Leni said. *“They’re mapping the grid. They’re testing response times. They’re figuring out exactly how long it takes for the city to notice when a primary artery gets cut.”*

Wolford leaned his head back against the headrest, staring at the ceiling of the cab. "A stress test."

"Leni, send the correlation data to Fangmeyer," Judy ordered, her mind already racing ahead. "If they're testing the grid, they need a central node to plug all this stolen hardware into. A blind spot."

*“I'm on it. But Judy... whoever wrote this code isn't street talent. This is state-level architecture.”*

"I know." Judy ended the call. 

She looked out the windshield at the empty, sun-baked industrial park. The ghost of a white fox seemed to linger in the heat shimmer above the asphalt. 

"They aren't hiding from us," Judy said, her voice cold. "They're getting ready to turn the lights out."

The sun was finally beginning to dip behind the jagged skyline of Savanna Central, casting long, bruised shadows across the pavement. 

In a narrow service alley behind a strip of discount electronics stores, Duke Weaselton was having a very good day. He stood at the open trunk of a battered sedan, admiring a neat stack of high-end commercial tablets. They still had the warehouse inventory tags on them.

"I'm telling you, genuine article," Duke said, rubbing his paws together. He grinned at the empty air, practicing his pitch. "Fell right off the back of a truck. And by fell, I mean I used bolt cutters, but who's keeping track?"

"I am."

Duke jumped nearly a foot in the air. He spun around, slamming the trunk shut with a panicked yelp. 

Judy Hopps stepped out of the shadow of a dumpster. She wasn't in uniform, but the way she stood—balanced, centered, eyes locked on him—screamed cop louder than any badge. 

Duke’s eyes darted left toward the mouth of the alley. 

Wolford stepped into the light, blocking the exit. The wolf crossed his massive arms over his chest and offered a slow, toothy smile. 

Duke’s shoulders slumped. He let out a long, theatrical groan. "Oh, come on. Flopsy the Copsy? Really? I've been clean for months! I'm a legitimate businessman now. I sell... uh... imported glass."

"You're selling stolen commercial tablets, Duke," Judy said, walking slowly toward him. "Grand theft, possession of stolen property, and violation of parole. That’s a five-year stretch, minimum."

Duke scoffed, though his tail gave a nervous twitch. "You got nothing. You didn't see me take 'em. Prove it."

"I don't have to prove anything," Judy said. She stopped three feet away from him. Her voice was completely devoid of its usual bright cadence. It was flat and cold. "I can just arrest you right now. Or..."

Duke’s ears perked up slightly. "Or?"

"Or you tell me something I want to know, and Wolford and I turn around and walk out of this alley. We never saw you. We never saw the trunk."

Duke relaxed, a greasy smile spreading across his muzzle. He leaned against the bumper of the sedan. "Well, why didn't you say so? You want street gossip? I got gossip. You know the Wooly brothers? They're moving counterfeit designer bags out of—"

"I don't care about the Wooly brothers," Judy cut him off. "I want to know about heavy infrastructure hardware. Relays. Pressure boards. Someone is moving serious tonnage through the districts using shell contractors. And they're being led by a white arctic fox."

The greasy smile vanished from Duke’s face so fast it looked like it had been wiped off with a rag. 

"Nope," Duke said instantly. He stood up straight, his hands raised in surrender. "No. No way. Arrest me. Put the cuffs on. I'll take the five years."

Wolford raised an eyebrow, genuinely surprised. 

Judy didn't blink. "Duke—"

"I said arrest me!" Duke’s voice cracked, pitching up an octave. "I ain't talking about ghosts, Hopps. I like breathing. Breathing is my favorite hobby."

Judy took a step closer, invading his personal space. "You think prison is going to keep you safe? Tell me what you know, Duke."

"I don't know nothing!"

"Fine." Judy pulled her phone from her pocket. "I won't arrest you. I'll just make a quick call to Tundratown. I hear Mr. Big is still looking for the guy who’s been skimming off his electronics shipments. I'm sure Kevin and Raymond would love to have a little chat with you. Maybe show you a new ice fishing hole."

Duke froze. His eyes went wide, darting frantically between Judy’s phone and her face. He was trapped between two predators, and he knew it. 

"You wouldn't," he whispered.

"Try me," Judy said, her thumb hovering over the screen. 

Duke swallowed hard. His paws were actually shaking. He looked around the alley, his eyes wide with a paranoia that bordered on madness. When he finally spoke, his voice was barely a breath.

"They don't have faces," Duke whispered, leaning in so close Judy could smell the stale coffee on his breath. "The street doesn't know who they are. But they pay in pure, untraceable cash. They buy up empty warehouses, hire independent rigs, and then... they just disappear."

"What's the name of the shell company?" Judy demanded.

"Hopps, I swear to God—"

"The name, Duke."

Duke squeezed his eyes shut. "Blackwood. Blackwood Freight."

Judy committed the name to memory. "Who runs it? Where are they based?"

"I don't know!" Duke opened his eyes, taking a stumbling step backward. The mere act of saying the name seemed to have broken something inside him. "That's all I know! And if they find out I told you, they won't break my legs like Mr. Big. They'll just erase me. I'll be a smudge on the pavement."

"Duke, calm down—"

"No! The deal is done!" Duke spun around, grabbed the handle of his trunk, and then stopped. He looked at the thousands of dollars worth of stolen electronics. He looked at the shadows at the end of the alley. 

He let go of the handle. 

"Keep it," Duke choked out. "Keep the car. Keep the merchandise. I'm leaving the city."

Before Judy could say another word, the weasel bolted. He scrambled over a chain-link fence at the back of the alley with frantic, desperate energy, dropping down the other side and vanishing into the maze of the district. 

Judy stared at the empty fence. 

Wolford walked up beside her, looking down at the abandoned car full of high-end tech. Duke Weaselton, a mammal who would normally sell his own grandmother for a quick buck, had just walked away from the biggest score of his month.

"He left the merchandise," Wolford said quietly. The big wolf sounded deeply unsettled. 

"I know," Judy said. She looked down at her own paws. They felt cold despite the lingering heat of the day. "Call Fangmeyer. Tell her to run every database we have for Blackwood Freight."

The air inside the Zootopia Intelligence Agency's regional headquarters was filtered, chilled, and completely devoid of scent. It was a sterile environment designed to keep minds sharp and emotions dull.

Jack Savage sat behind his desk in an office that looked exactly like every other office on the floor: glass walls, brushed steel surfaces, and precisely zero personal items. He was reading a decrypted field report on a secure terminal, his striped ears perfectly still.

The glass door slid open with a soft hiss. 

Agent Moss stepped inside. The moose wore a charcoal suit that fit his massive frame perfectly. He moved with a quiet, deliberate heaviness, holding a slim manila folder in one hand.

"Working late, Savage?" Moss asked, his voice a deep, resonant rumble. 

"The data doesn't sleep, Moss," Jack replied, not looking away from his screen. He tapped a key, minimizing the report before Moss could get a clear angle on it. "What do you need?"

Moss took a seat in the chair opposite the desk, crossing his legs. He looked perfectly relaxed, but his dark eyes were sharp, cataloging every detail of the room. 

"Just checking in on the primary objective," Moss said. "The Director is asking for a status update on the infrastructure anomalies. Have we established a firm operational footprint for the target?"

Jack folded his hands on the desk. He knew exactly what Moss was doing. "The investigation is progressing within expected parameters. We're currently analyzing logistical patterns across multiple districts to establish a predictive model."

"Ah. 'Predictive models.' 'Logistical patterns.'" Moss smiled, showing just a hint of white teeth. "That's a very elegant way of saying you don't have a location yet."

"It means we are building a comprehensive threat assessment rather than chasing shadows," Jack said smoothly. "When we move, we will have a complete picture of the network."

"Of course." Moss nodded slowly, as if agreeing with a student. He placed the manila folder on the brushed steel desk and slid it across the surface with one heavy hoof. "Which brings me to this."

Jack looked at the folder but didn't touch it. "What is it?"

"A formal request for compliance and inter-agency coordination," Moss said. His tone remained perfectly pleasant. "The Director feels that given the escalating nature of these... 'anomalies,' we need to ensure all our assets are fully synchronized. The request asks for a complete, unredacted list of your current field sources, your active informant network, and a detailed timeline of your operational movements over the last seventy-two hours."

Jack’s expression didn't change. His heart rate didn't elevate. He simply looked at the folder, then back up at the panther. 

"You know I can't provide that, Moss."

"Can't?" Moss tilted his head. "Or won't?"

"Both," Jack said. "My operational mandate falls under a Level Four compartmentalization directive. Releasing source identities or raw timelines outside of my immediate clearance chain violates core security protocols."

"Even to your own agency?"

"Especially within the agency," Jack corrected him softly. "Compartmentalization exists to prevent systemic contamination. If a source is compromised, the damage remains isolated. You know the doctrine."

Moss let out a soft, understanding sigh. He stood up, smoothing the front of his suit jacket. 

"I do know the doctrine, Jack. But you have to admit... from an administrative perspective, it creates a rather unfortunate optic."

Moss adjusted his cufflinks, voice still mild. "And let's not ignore the obvious: you pushed hard to lead this case from day one."

He tilted his head. "Most agents avoid infrastructure work unless they have to."

A faint smile. "So why were you so eager, Jack?"

Jack finally leaned back in his chair. "Meaning?"

"Meaning," Moss said, walking toward the glass door, "that while heavy infrastructure is being systematically dismantled across the city, the lead agent on the case is actively refusing to share his intelligence with his own team. He's hiding his files behind protocol." 

Moss paused at the door, looking back over his shoulder. The smile was gone. 

"To a less understanding mammal, Jack, it might look like you're protecting someone."

The door hissed open, and Moss stepped out into the brightly lit corridor, leaving the folder sitting on the desk like a live grenade.

Jack sat in the silence of his office. He didn't touch the folder. He didn't need to read it. He knew exactly what it was. 

It wasn't a request for information. It was a trap. 

Moss had just laid the first brick in a paper trail. When the time came, that folder would be the documented proof that Jack Savage had gone rogue, withheld critical intelligence, and obstructed a federal investigation. 

Jack reached out and slowly closed the cover of his laptop. The sterile air of the office suddenly felt very thin. 

The hunt had changed. He wasn't just chasing ghosts anymore. 

He was becoming one.

Late Wednesday evening, the air in Leni's garage was thick with ozone and stale pizza. The Strike Force was gathered around the central workbench, illuminated by the harsh glare of a single overhead halogen.

"Blackwood Freight," Fangmeyer said, tapping a claw against a printed ledger. "Duke wasn't lying. They're a ghost company. Incorporated three weeks ago in Sahara Square. Paid cash for a short-term lease on a commercial warehouse in the Meadowlands."

"And the trucks?" Judy asked, leaning against the edge of the bench with her arms crossed.

"Two heavy-duty flatbeds registered to the same LLC," Leni said from her terminal, not looking away from the scrolling code. "They've been making runs between the docks and the Meadowlands for the last forty-eight hours. Tiny footprint, but it's there."

McHorn crossed his massive arms. "So we have a location. Do we call it in? Get a warrant?"

"On what grounds?" Wolford asked quietly. "The word of a weasel who ran out of town without giving a sworn statement? A judge would laugh us out of chambers."

"If we go through official channels, ZIA gets flagged," Judy said, voice hard. "Savage swoops in, takes the location, and we're back to zero. Worse, if the white fox has ears inside the department, a warrant request gives them a twelve-hour head start to burn the place down."

Silence settled over the garage.

"You want to hit it off-book," Fangmeyer said. It wasn't a question.

"Soft approach," Judy replied, meeting the tiger's gaze. "We observe tonight. If we see the trucks, if we see the hardware, we find a way inside. Quietly. First light."

Wolford raised an eyebrow. "Breaking and entering without a warrant. That's a felony, Hopps."

"I know what it is, James."

Fangmeyer studied Judy for a long moment, her tail flicking once. "Wilde would be proud of you right now."

Judy didn't flinch, though the name still felt like a physical blow. "Nick would've picked the lock two hours ago. Get some sleep. We move at dawn."

She made it sound tactical. Not personal. Not like a promise she hadn't admitted out loud.

***

The warehouse sat at the dead end of a crumbling industrial spur in the Meadowlands, surrounded by overgrown weeds and rusted chain-link fencing.

It was 5:30 AM. Morning mist hung heavy over damp earth.

Strike Force moved in absolute silence. They had spent the last two hours observing from a drainage ditch a hundred yards away. No guards outside. No visible cameras. Just a single dim bulb over a side entrance.

Judy pressed her back against corrugated metal near the door. Wolford stacked behind her with mechanical picks. Across the loading bay, McHorn and Fangmeyer held the main roll-up door, waiting for the signal.

Judy gave Wolford a nod.

The wolf worked the cheap deadbolt in under ten seconds. A soft click. He checked for tripwires and swung the door open.

Judy slipped inside, weapon drawn, flashlight off.

She heard it immediately.

Breathing. Slow, heavy, rhythmic, from a small office near the back wall.

She tapped Wolford's shoulder and pointed. They crossed the floor like shadows, took opposite sides of the office door, and Judy pushed it open with her flashlight snapping on.

"ZPD! Don't move!"

The beam hit a battered cot. A middle-aged badger in a stained undershirt bolted upright, paws over his eyes, screaming.

"Don't shoot! I'm just the night watchman! I don't know nothing!"

Wolford stepped in, weapon low but ready. "Hands where we can see them. Who else is in the building?"

"Nobody! Just me! I swear!"

Judy swept the office. Half-empty coffee cups. Zip-ties. A city utility grid map on a folding table. Thick black industrial cable coiled on the floor.

Fangmeyer's voice crackled through the earpiece. "Building clear. Main floor empty. No trucks. No hardware."

Judy lowered her gun, jaw tight. "Who hired you?"

"Some guy online. Five hundred cash to sit here three nights and guard the cables." The badger pointed at the coils. "Said they were moving heavy equipment in tomorrow."

"They're gone," Wolford muttered at the table. "Looks like they bugged out fast. Left routing schematics."

Fangmeyer entered, rifle slung, pointing at red circles on the map. "This is a goldmine, Judy. We finally got ahead of them."

Judy stared at the table. Then closed her eyes and filtered scent.

Dust. Damp concrete. Rust.

No predator musk. No white-fox scent. No residual body heat from a working crew.

She opened her eyes, looked at the map, then at the cable. She knelt and lifted one coil.

Too light.

"Knife."

Wolford handed her a combat knife with a frown. Judy slit the thick rubber casing.

"Judy, what are you doing? That's evidence."

"No, it's not."

She peeled the sheath back.

No copper. No fiber. Hollow plastic tubing made to look like relay cable.

Fangmeyer stepped closer. "What?"

Judy grabbed the map and flipped it. The back was pristine white, untouched by the grime coating the table.

"They didn't bug out," she whispered, throwing the map back down. "They were never operating out of here."

Wolford's ears pinned back. "A decoy."

"A stage set," Judy corrected, bitter. She looked at the badger and saw a prop with a pulse. "They knew Duke would talk. Knew we'd pull the Blackwood lease. They built a set, hired a babysitter, and waited to see if we'd take the bait."

"Why?" McHorn asked from the doorway. "Just to waste our time?"

"To test response time," Fangmeyer said, eyes widening. "Like the utility hits. They wanted to see how fast an off-book ZPD unit can mobilize without dispatch."

Judy tightened her grip on the hollow cable until her knuckles ached.

They hadn't caught the ghost off guard.

The ghost was playing with them.

They were dancing to the white fox's tune.

"Pack it up," Judy said, voice tight with fury. "We're done here."
