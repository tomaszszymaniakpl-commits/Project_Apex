# CHAPTER 3: Static on the Line

The rhythm of the train was a lullaby. Clack-whoosh. Clack-whoosh.

Judy didn't know when she had fallen asleep, but she knew exactly why she felt safe. Warmth radiated against her left side, solid and steady. Her head was resting on something soft—flannel over fur—and the scent of woodsmoke and dryer lint filled her nose every time she inhaled.

It was the kind of sleep that felt heavy, the deep recharge after forty-eight hours of adrenaline and family chaos. Outside the window, the countryside was a blur of shadows, but inside the private compartment, the world was small. Just two seats. Just them.

Nick's breathing was slow, deep. His head was tilted back against the seat, but at some point in the last hour, gravity had won, and his cheek was pressed against the top of her ears. His paw, usually so guarded, lay open on the armrest between them, inches from hers.

Judy shifted slightly, her nose twitching. She let her eyes drift half-open. The cabin lights were dimmed to a soft blue. She looked at his paw. Then, slowly, bravely, she moved her own. Her fingers brushed against his knuckles. He didn't pull away. In his sleep, he turned his paw over, his claws retracted, creating a space for her.

She smiled, closing her eyes again. We have time, she thought. We finally have time.

BZZZZT.

The sound was harsh, violent against the hard plastic of the table.

It wasn't a ringtone. It was the vibration of a phone set to silent, rattling against the folding tray, thin and insistent.

Nick jerked awake. The warmth vanished instantly from Judy's side as he sat bolt upright, his body going rigid.

"Wha—?" He blinked, disoriented, his ears swiveling wildly toward the sound.

Judy sat up, rubbing her eyes. "Phone," she mumbled, her heart jumping a little at the sudden movement. "Yours."

Nick stared at the device face-down on the table. It buzzed again. BZZZZT.

He wasn't reaching for it. He was looking at it with a strange, sharp confusion. His pupils were blown wide, swallowing the green iris. He looked like he had just heard bad news twice.

"Who's calling you at three in the morning?" Judy asked, reaching for her water bottle.

Nick flipped the phone over.

The screen lit up his face in a ghostly pale blue. Judy watched him. She expected a groan, a complaint about telemarketers or a wrong number.

Instead, she saw his ears flatten.

It happened in a microsecond. One moment, he was the sleepy, warm partner she had almost kissed in a barn. The next, he was stone. His scent spiked—sharp, acrid ozone cutting through the woodsmoke. Shock—raw and electric.

He read the message. Once. Twice.

"Nick?" Judy asked, her voice clearer now.

He snapped the screen off. He shoved the phone into his pocket, his movements jerky and too fast. Then he stood up, moving to the other side of the compartment, putting three feet of distance between them.

"Wrong number," he said.

His voice was dry. Brittle.

"A wrong number sent a text?" Judy frowned, her ears drooping slightly. The loss of his warmth was a physical ache. "You looked... startled."

Nick turned to the window, staring at his own reflection in the dark glass. He smoothed down his flannel shirt, but his paws were trembling. Just a little. His thumb found the tarnished pocket watch in his vest pocket, rubbing the metal edge obsessively. *Click-click-click.*

"It's nothing, Carrots. Just spam. Some bot selling nonsense." He forced a chuckle, but it sounded hollow, like a coin dropped in an empty well. "Go back to sleep. We've got another hour."

He didn't sit back down. He stayed by the window, his tail tucked tight against his leg, his silhouette rigid against the passing lights of the outer districts. The only sound was the rhythm of the train, and the frantic, quiet clicking of the watch in his pocket.

Judy lay back down, pulling her jacket tighter around herself. The compartment suddenly felt very cold. She watched him for a long time, waiting for him to relax, to make a joke, to come back.

He didn't.

---

The rain in Zootopia didn't wash things clean; it just made everything gray.

It had been raining for three days straight. A relentless, cold drizzle that turned the fur damp and made the city smell of wet concrete and burnt fuel.

Judy sat in the passenger seat of the cruiser, watching the windshield wipers fight a losing battle. Swish-clack. Swish-clack.

"Snarlbucks run?" Nick asked from the driver's seat.

Judy looked over. Nick was driving with one paw on the wheel, his eyes fixed on the road. He looked tired. Not the good kind of tired—the 'up all night solving a case' tired. This was different: dark circles under his eyes, dull fur, no spark.

"Please," Judy said. "Double shot for me. I think the mold in the precinct is finally getting to my brain."

"Coming right up. Skim latte, extra foam, dash of cinnamon. I know the drill."

He parked the cruiser along the curb, popped the collar of his raincoat, and stepped out into the downpour.

"Be right back. Don't arrest anyone while I'm gone."

It was a joke. It should have felt like one. But he didn't smile when he said it.

Judy watched him jog toward the coffee shop. The neon green mermaid logo of Snarlbucks glowed through the mist. The shop had huge plate-glass windows, currently streaked with rain.

She leaned back, closing her eyes for a second. The last three days had been weird. Nick was there, but absent. He missed cues, dropped conversations, deflected every direct question with a joke.

She opened her eyes and looked toward the shop.

The line inside was short. Just a beaver at the counter and a jaguar waiting for a pickup. Nick wasn't in line.

He was standing in the corner, near the condiment station. He wasn't getting sugar.

He was on the phone.

Judy sat up straighter. Through the rain-blurred glass, she couldn't see his expression clearly, but she could read the body language. He was hunched over, shielding the phone with his body, facing the wall. His free paw was moving—chopping the air. Arguing. Pleading?

He dragged a paw down his face, ears pinned back. He looked like an animal caught in a trap, gnawing at its own leg.

The barista called out something. Nick jumped, startled. He quickly ended the call, shoving the phone into his raincoat, and grabbed the tray of drinks.

Judy sank back into her seat just as the cruiser door opened. A gust of wet wind and the smell of coffee filled the cab.

"Absolute circus in there. Took forever," Nick huffed, shaking water from his ears. He handed her the cup.

Judy took the cup. It was hot against her paws.

She looked at the coffee shop. The beaver was gone. The jaguar was gone. It was empty.

She looked at Nick. He was buckling his seatbelt, humming a tuneless melody, deliberately not looking at her. The scent coming off him wasn't just rain and coffee. Underneath the roasted beans, there it was again under the coffee: burnt sugar and fear.

"Pennies?" Judy repeated, her voice neutral. "That's annoying."

"Tell me about it. Civilization is crumbling, Carrots." He put the car in gear. "Ready to go file those reports on the Mystic Springs break-in? I promise not to fall asleep."

"Nick," she started, the question burning on her tongue. Who were you talking to? Why lie about the line?

He turned to her, flashing a smile. It was perfect. The trademark Nick Wilde smirk. But it didn't reach his eyes. His eyes were flat, green glass, reflecting nothing but the dashboard lights.

"Yeah?"

Judy gripped her coffee cup tighter.

"Nothing," she said, taking a sip. "Let's go."

The word formed anyway: *Liar*.

She turned to look out the window as they pulled into traffic. The reflection of the city slid over the glass, distorted and dark. She didn't ask him again. But she didn't stop watching.

---

The fluorescent lights of the bullpen hummed with a low, headache-inducing buzz that seemed to harmonize with the rain beating against the windows.

Nick was balancing a pen on his nose. He had been staring at a witness statement for forty minutes without turning the page.

Judy walked back to her desk, the heavy manila folder from Bogo's office clutching tightly in her paw. She sat down, the chair squeaking in the quiet room.

Nick didn't look up. "Did Bogo finally yell at you for the glitter incident? I told you, technically, it is a hazard."

"No," Judy said, placing the folder on the desk. "He offered me a spot."

The pen wobbled on Nick's nose. He caught it before it fell, finally turning to face her. His eyes were guarded, the green iris dull under the artificial light.

"A spot? What, like a parking spot? Or a stain?"

"The Advanced Tactics & Infiltration Program," Judy said, watching him closely. "In Zoo-York."

Nick blinked. Once. Twice. His ears twitched, swiveling forward. For a second, he looked genuinely blank.

"Zoo-York?" he repeated. "That's... that's across the continent, Carrots. That's the big leagues."

"It's a three-month rotation," Judy continued, leaning forward. "Starting almost immediately. The transfer shuttle leaves day after next."

She waited for the objection. She waited for him to say 'Three months? Who's going to buy my coffee?' or 'You can't leave me alone with Clawhauser, I'll go native.'

Instead, Nick leaned back in his chair. A strange expression crossed his face—quick, hidden, and gone before she could name it.

"Well," he said, a smile plastering itself onto his muzzle a fraction of a second too late. "You have to take it."

Something cold settled in Judy's stomach. "I do?"

"Are you kidding?" Nick gestured with the pen, his voice ramping up into his salesman cadence. "It's Advanced Tactics. That's the golden ticket to Lieutenant. Plus, think of the networking. You'll be rubbing shoulders with the feds. You'd be crazy to say no."

"It's three months, Nick," Judy said quietly. "We've never been apart for more than a weekend."

Nick waved a paw dismissively, though his claws were digging slightly into the fabric of his armrest. "In the grand scheme of our illustrious careers, that's a coffee break. Besides, I can hold down the fort here. I'm a big boy. I might even finish my paperwork on time without you hovering."

He was selling it. He was selling her leaving with the same slick charm he used to sell pawpsicles.

"I don't know," Judy murmured, looking down at the file. "It feels... sudden. I told Bogo I'd think about it."

"Don't overthink it, Fluff. Opportunities like this don't knock twice. Especially not for bunnies from the Burrows." He offered her a wink, but his scent betrayed him. It wasn't the warm musk of support. It was sharp, metallic—cold and metallic at the back of her throat. He was tense. Coiled tight.

Judy forced a smile. "Maybe you're right. I guess I could use the resume boost."

"That's the spirit," Nick said, turning back to his paperwork a little too quickly.

Judy bit her lip. She needed to know. She needed to test the wall he had built since the train ride.

"Hey," she said, keeping her voice casual. "I can't make a decision like this on an empty stomach. And a new season of 'The Crowned Antler' dropped last night. How about you come over? We order Thai, binge the first three episodes, and you can convince me why I should abandon you for ninety days."

It was their ritual. New season, Thai food, Nick complaining about historical inaccuracies in the costumes. He never missed it.

Nick froze. His pen stopped moving on the paper.

He didn't look at her. He looked at the clock on the wall.

"Ah," he said. The syllable hung in the air, heavy and awkward. "I can't tonight, Carrots. Previous engagement."

"Engagement?" Judy frowned. "With who? You said you were broke."

"Errands," he corrected quickly, shifting in his seat. "Boring stuff. Meeting a guy about a... thing. An investment. It's gonna take all night."

"Nick," Judy said, her voice hardening. "You're blowing off 'The Crowned Antler' for an errand?"

He finally looked at her. His eyes were hard, flat surfaces that deflected everything she was trying to send him.

"I'm busy, Judy. Okay? I'm just... busy."

He stood up, grabbing his jacket off the back of the chair.

"I'm gonna go hit the vending machine. I need something sour."

He walked away without waiting for a response. Judy watched him go. He walked with a stiffness in his shoulders she hadn't seen since his hustler days.

Busy was the cover story. Running was the truth.

---

The apartment was quiet. The only sound was the refrigerator compressor humming in the kitchen and the relentless rain tapping against the windowpane.

Judy was not crying.

She was wearing her oversized gym t-shirt and sweatpants, pacing the length of her small living room. Back and forth. Kitchen to window. Window to kitchen.

On her coffee table, a notepad lay open.

FACTS:

*Warehouse: Froze when he found the circuit board. Pocketed it. Lied about it. ("Vintage tech.")*
*Train: Received text. Startled. Visibly shaken.*
*Train: Physical withdrawal. "Wrong number."*
*Coffee Shop: Argument on phone. Lied about the line. "Pennies."*
*Precinct: Enthusiastic about me leaving for 3 months.*
*Precinct: Refused movie night. "Busy."*

She stopped at the window, staring at her reflection. A sad bunny would be eating ice cream right now. A heartbroken bunny would be calling her mom.

Judy Hopps was neither. She was a cop.

"He lied about the line," she whispered to the empty room. "Why lie about a line at a coffee shop?"

She tapped the glass.

If Nick was seeing someone else, he wouldn't push her to go to Zoo-York. He would want her close but distracted. Sending her away for three months was... absolute.

If he was bored with her, he wouldn't have been so warm on the train right before the phone buzzed.

She looked at point number 4 on her list. *Enthusiastic about me leaving.*

"He wants me gone," she murmured. Her eyes narrowed. "Not because he doesn't want me around. But because he doesn't want me *here*."

She replayed the look on his face in the bullpen. That wasn't annoyance. That wasn't indifference. Under the mask, under the salesman grin... his paws had been trembling.

He was scared.

Nick Wilde, the fox who stared down a savage jaguar without blinking, was scared. And whatever he was scared of, he was trying to put three thousand miles between it and Judy.

"You idiot fox," she said, a surge of adrenaline replacing the hurt in her chest. "You think you can protect me by pushing me away?"

She walked over to the table and picked up the notepad. She ripped the page out, crumpled it into a ball, and tossed it perfectly into the recycling bin.

She grabbed her phone. She didn't call Nick. She pulled up the ZPD transfer portal.

She would go to Zoo-York on paper. Nick just had to believe she went for real.

"Okay, Slick," she said, her voice steady and dangerous in the quiet apartment. "You want me to leave? I'll leave. And the second you think I'm gone, I'm going to find out exactly who—or what—has got you so terrified."

She walked to her closet and pulled out a duffel bag. She didn't pack her dress uniform. She packed her dark tactical gear, a voice recorder, and a black hoodie.

The hurt was still there, a dull ache in her chest. But now, it had a purpose.

Partners didn't let partners drown. Even if they had to drag them kicking and screaming to the shore.
