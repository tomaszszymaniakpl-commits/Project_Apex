# Chapter 08: Purgatory

*Three months later.*

The summer heat in Savanna Central was heavy and unforgiving. It baked the asphalt, turning the air above the streets into a shimmering, rippling mirage.

Judy Hopps stood next to a bright yellow parking meter, the sun beating down mercilessly on her dark blue meter maid uniform. She wasn't wearing her kevlar. She wasn't wearing her badge. 

A sleek, unnecessarily large sports car was parked almost two feet into the red loading zone. 

Judy didn't sigh. She didn't roll her eyes. Her movements were entirely mechanical, stripped of any emotion or energy. She pulled the ticket book from her belt, scribbled the citation with a ballpoint pen, tore the slip of paper, and tucked it under the windshield wiper. 

"Hey! Hey, meter maid! What do you think you're doing?!" 

A large hippopotamus in a loud, expensive shirt came jogging out of a nearby café, waving a half-eaten pastry. He stopped next to his car, glaring down at her. "I was gone for two minutes! You can't just slap a ticket on there without a warning!"

In the old days, Judy would have stood her ground, offered a cheerful but firm recitation of the municipal parking code, and sent him on his way. 

Today, she didn't even blink. 

She turned her head slowly, looking up at the furious hippo. Her violet eyes were dull, flat, and completely empty. There was a darkness behind them that did not belong on a sunny street in Savanna Central.

The hippo’s mouth opened to yell again, but the words died in his throat. He looked at the rabbit’s face, swallowed hard, and took a step back. 

"If you wish to contest the citation, sir," Judy said, her voice entirely devoid of inflection, "you may contact the Department of Motor Vehicles between nine and five. Have a pleasant day."

She turned and walked away before he could answer. She didn't look back.

A familiar screech of worn brake pads cut through the street noise. A standard ZPD cruiser pulled up to the curb beside her, the engine idling roughly. The passenger side window rolled down, letting a blast of glorious, arctic air conditioning spill out onto the sidewalk.

Officer Wolford leaned across the center console. The wolf was wearing his patrol uniform, his sunglasses pushed up on his forehead. 

"Get in, Hopps," Wolford grunted. "You're burning the retinas out of my eyes with that vest."

Judy stopped. "I'm on shift, Wolford. I have three more blocks to clear."

"Not anymore. Bogo finally got tired of watching you play parking zombie," Wolford said, kicking the passenger door open from the inside. "Internal Affairs cleared your probationary status an hour ago. You're back on active street patrol. With me."

Judy stared at the open door. The cold air beckoned, but she didn't move. 

"You volunteered to ride with me?" she asked, her voice dropping, laced with bitter disbelief. "You agreed to be partners with the traitor's partner?"

Wolford’s ears flicked. He didn't offer a pitying smile or a gentle reassurance. He just looked at her with the pragmatic, unsentimental gaze of a wolf who had seen worse.

"I volunteered to ride with a good cop," Wolford said flatly. "And frankly, I need someone who actually does the paperwork. Get in the car, Judy."

He paused, then added more quietly, "And no, I don't buy all of it. Not yet."

"Then don't say it out loud," Judy said, eyes still on the open door. "Not until I can prove what this really is."

She hesitated for a fraction of a second longer, then unclipped the orange vest, tossed it into the back seat, and climbed in. The cold air conditioning of the cruiser felt like a shock to her overheated system.

Wolford put the car in drive and pulled out into the midday traffic. 

"So," Judy said quietly, staring out the window at the passing city. "How bad is it out here?"

"Messy," Wolford sighed, tapping his claws against the steering wheel. "Ever since the... harbor incident, the streets have been restless. Everyone thinks the ZPD is distracted, so every two-bit gang is trying to make a land grab. We've had a spike in everything. Break-ins, vandalism, logistical hijacks. It's a logistical nightmare."

Judy kept her eyes on the window. "Just gangs fighting for territory?"

"That's what the brass says," Wolford shrugged. "A lot of weird coincidences, though. Someone hit a power relay in Tundratown last week and didn't steal a dime. Two days later, a crew broke into a Savanna water sub-station, patched into the pressure routing board, and vanished. Then there was that utility transfer hijack in Rainforest. They took relay modules and dispatch-grade routing hardware, left everything expensive behind. Just random chaos, I guess."

Judy frowned slightly, the detective instincts buried under three months of grief twitching faintly in the dark. 

"Random," Judy echoed. "Right. Just coincidences."

Wolford glanced at her out of the corner of his eye. He didn't press the issue. 

Before he could say anything else, the police radio squawked. 

*"10-50, minor traffic collision with altercation. Intersection of Vine and Cypress."*

"Show us responding," Wolford grunted into the radio, flipping the lights on. 

When they arrived, a heavily dented sedan belonging to a wild boar was wedged against the bumper of a delivery truck driven by a very angry rhinoceros. Both mammals were standing in the middle of the intersection, shouting at the top of their lungs, blocking three lanes of midday traffic.

"You cut me off!" the boar squealed, his face red with heat and fury. "I had the right of way!"

"You're a speed bump with legs!" the rhino bellowed back, stepping forward aggressively. "I'll crush your miserable little car into a cube!"

Wolford stepped out of the cruiser, resting a hand casually on his belt. "Alright, break it up. Everyone back to their vehicles."

The rhino turned, glaring down at the wolf. "Stay out of this, dog. He ruined my bumper!"

Wolford's ears pinned back, a low growl starting in his chest, but before he could step forward, Judy was already there. 

She walked directly between the two massive mammals. She didn't look intimidated. She didn't try to de-escalate with a calm, friendly tone like she would have three months ago. She just looked exhausted and entirely out of patience.

"Driver of the truck," Judy said, her voice cutting through the heat like a blade of ice. She pointed a finger at the rhino. "You are in violation of municipal code 4-12, obstructing a public thoroughfare. You will move your vehicle to the shoulder immediately."

The rhino scoffed, looking down at the rabbit. "Or what?"

Judy didn't blink. "Or I will cite you for reckless driving, impound your truck for a mandatory forty-eight-hour hold, and arrest you for threatening a fellow civilian. Do you want to spend the weekend in a holding cell without air conditioning, or do you want to move the truck?"

Her eyes were completely dead. It wasn't a threat; it was a cold, administrative promise. 

The rhino stared at her, the fight draining out of him as he realized this tiny cop meant every word. He muttered a curse under his breath and lumbered back to his cab. Judy turned the same dead-eyed stare on the boar, who immediately scrambled into his dented sedan without a word.

Wolford stood by the cruiser, watching her. He let out a low whistle as she walked back and opened the passenger door.

"Remind me never to ask you for a favor," Wolford said, putting the cruiser back in gear as the intersection cleared. "Anyway, Bogo wants us back at the station. We've got some new suits from downtown in the bullpen."

---

The atmosphere inside Precinct 1 used to feel like a second home to Judy. It was loud, chaotic, smelling of cheap coffee and wet fur, but it was a pack.

Today, walking into the bullpen alongside Wolford, it felt like walking into a freezer.

The ambient noise didn't stop, but it shifted. Conversations dropped in volume as they passed the rows of cubicles. A few officers looked away, suddenly intensely interested in their computer monitors. Others didn't bother hiding their stares.

Judy kept her eyes locked straight ahead, her chin held high, but her ears swiveled involuntarily, catching the fragmented whispers.

*"...can't believe she's back on the floor..."* 
*"...bet she knew..."* 
*"...partner of a traitor..."*

A low, threatening rumble started in Wolford's chest. He slowed his pace, his yellow eyes sweeping the room, daring someone to say it louder. The whispering ceased immediately in his immediate vicinity, but the heavy, suffocating weight of the ostracism remained.

Officer Delgato leaned back in his chair as they passed his desk. The lion didn't say a word, but the smirk on his face was loud enough.

Judy’s hands balled into fists, her steps slowing. Wolford noticed, his own hackles rising slightly.

"Hey."

Fangmeyer stepped out from behind a filing cabinet, smoothly cutting off Delgato's line of sight to Judy. Her massive, striped frame blocked the aisle. She held two steaming mugs of coffee. She handed one to Judy without asking, then leveled a glare over Judy’s head at a group of gossiping officers near the water cooler. They scattered instantly.

"Ignore them, Jude," the tiger said softly, her voice a comforting rumble. "They're just scared. They need someone to blame because it's easier than admitting the department got played."

Judy gripped the warm mug, taking a small measure of comfort from the heat. "It's fine, Fang. I'm used to it."

"You shouldn't have to be," Fangmeyer growled softly.

"We have bigger problems," Wolford interrupted, pointing a claw toward the far end of the room.

Judy followed his gaze. Her stomach plummeted.

Her old desk—the one she had shared with Nick in the center of the detective bullpen—was surrounded by portable privacy screens. Three mammals in perfectly tailored, nondescript gray suits were currently dismantling the workspace. They were packing every file, every notepad, and every personal item into heavy plastic bins marked with federal seals.

Judy didn't think; she reacted. She dropped her coffee mug into the nearest trash can and marched across the room.

"Excuse me," Judy said, her voice sharp enough to cut glass as she bypassed the privacy screen. "What do you think you're doing with my files?"

One of the suits, a tall, severe-looking greyhound, held up a hand to stop her from stepping further. "This is a restricted area, Officer."

"This is my desk," Judy snapped.

"Not anymore."

The voice came from behind the desk. A mammal stepped out from the shadows of the privacy screen, holding a pristine manila folder.

It was a striped rabbit. He wore a sharply tailored suit that looked completely out of place in the messy ZPD precinct. His fur was meticulously groomed, and his eyes—a striking, cold blue—analyzed Judy with the mechanical detachment of a computer processing a flawed algorithm.

"Officer Hopps, I presume," the striped rabbit said. His voice was smooth, cultured, and utterly devoid of warmth. "Agent Jack Savage. Zootopia Intelligence Agency. We are officially assuming jurisdiction over the Nicholas Wilde investigation, as well as all cases previously handled by your unit."

"You can't just come in here and take my case files," Judy said, stepping forward, ignoring the greyhound. "I was there. I'm the primary witness to the explosion."

"You are a compromised asset," Savage corrected her smoothly, the words slipping out like poison on a silver spoon. He didn't raise his voice, but the impact was devastating. "Your involvement in anything related to Wilde ended the moment you failed to pull the trigger in the harbor district."

Judy flinched. Her paws curled into fists. "There's more to this than just—"

"I don't particularly care about your theories, Officer," Savage interrupted, tossing the folder into one of the bins. "The theft of municipal cryptographic keys elevates this to a matter of national security. The ZPD's jurisdiction is officially revoked. You are to surrender any personal notes, keys, or access codes related to your former partner immediately."

"Now, now, Agent Savage, let's not be unnecessarily antagonistic."

Another mammal stepped out from Bogo’s office on the mezzanine above them and began descending the stairs. It was a large moose, dressed in a slightly rumpled suit, moving with a calm, grandfatherly gait.

"Senior Agent Moss," Savage acknowledged, his posture stiffening slightly.

"Officer Hopps has been through a terrible ordeal," Moss said, offering Judy a sympathetic, surprisingly warm smile. He stopped next to Savage. "I apologize for my colleague's... directness. He's under a lot of pressure. We all are."

"I don't want your apologies," Judy said, glaring between the two federal agents. "I want to know what you're doing with my unit's files."

Moss sighed, a heavy, tired sound. "I'm afraid Agent Savage is correct regarding the protocol, Officer. The scope of Wilde's betrayal is too large for a municipal department to handle. There are implications of a wider network. We need to analyze every scrap of data he left behind."

"He's dead," Judy said, the words tasting like ash in her mouth. "Internal Affairs confirmed the DNA. What else is there to analyze?"

"That," Savage said, turning his cold blue eyes back to her, "is classified. And frankly, none of your business. Your Chief has already signed the transfer orders. Go back to writing parking tickets, Hopps. Leave the real work to the professionals."

Savage signaled the greyhound. "Box it up. We're moving it all to ZIA headquarters."

Judy stood there, her blood boiling, watching them pack away the last three years of her life. Wolford stepped up beside her, placing a heavy paw on her shoulder, silently urging her to walk away before she did something that would get her fired permanently.

She let Wolford guide her away, back toward the breakroom. But as she walked, the anger inside her didn't burn out. It crystallized.

Savage hadn't just insulted her. He had inadvertently confirmed her worst suspicion. *Implications of a wider network.*

If the ZIA was here, tearing apart Nick's life looking for connections, then the harbor wasn't the end of it. The weird vandalisms, the tech thefts Wolford mentioned... they were connected. And the ZIA was about to lock all the answers away in a federal vault.

Judy glanced toward the reception desk, where Clawhauser was currently distracted by a box of glazed donuts.

She needed to see those files. And she needed to do it tonight.

---

Judy didn't follow Wolford to the breakroom. The moment the wolf turned the corner, she pivoted on her heel and headed straight for the reception desk.

Benjamin Clawhauser was exactly where she left him, staring miserably at a half-eaten glazed donut in a pink cardboard box. He looked up as she approached, his spots seeming a little less vibrant than usual.

"Hey, Judy," he said softly, offering a weak smile. "Rough day?"

"Ben," Judy said, leaning casually against the high counter. She kept her voice light, friendly, entirely at odds with the adrenaline spiking in her veins. "You wouldn't happen to have a spare one of those, would you? The heat out there is brutal."

Clawhauser’s face immediately brightened. "Oh! Of course! Here, take the one with the sprinkles. You look like you could use the sugar." 

He slid the box toward her. As he did, Judy’s left paw slipped smoothly under the lip of the desk, plugging a small, encrypted USB drive into the secondary port of his terminal. 

"Thanks, Ben. You're a lifesaver." She took a bite, forcing herself to chew. "Hey, while I'm here... Wolford and I were trying to track down a witness from a fender-bender earlier. Could you pull up the shift logs from sector four?"

"Sure thing," Clawhauser said, his fingers flying across the keyboard. "Which day?"

"Actually, could you just print the last week's worth?" Judy asked, pointing toward the heavy duty printer located ten feet away, down the hall. "My terminal is acting up again."

"No problem!" Clawhauser hit a sequence of keys. The printer down the hall whirred to life. The cheetah stood up, his chair squeaking in relief. "I'll go grab them for you."

The moment his back was turned, Judy’s paws flew to his keyboard. She didn't have time to be careful. She opened the department's shared drive, bypassed the classified folders the ZIA was currently emptying, and dove straight into the unclassified, raw patrol logs. 

*Tundratown power relay. Utility transfer hijacking. Water control sub-station breach.*

She highlighted the raw data files and hit transfer. A permission warning flashed for one heartbeat; she killed it and rerouted through the open incident-export panel. The progress bar on her USB drive crawled forward. *Come on, come on.*

Down the hall, Clawhauser was gathering the printed sheets. He turned around.

Judy yanked the drive out a fraction of a second before he faced the desk, slipping it into her uniform pocket. She was casually leaning against the counter, finishing her donut, when he returned.

"Here you go, Jude," he said, handing her the stack of papers. "Hope it helps you find your guy."

"It definitely will," Judy said, giving him a genuine, if tired, smile. "Thanks, Ben."

She walked away before he could ask any questions, but she didn't head for the exit. She headed downstairs. 

The basement level of Precinct 1 housed the evidence lockers and the forensics lab. The air down here was cooler, smelling sharply of bleach and ozone. Through the glass double doors of the lab, she saw a familiar, slender figure hunched over a microscope. 

Leni Scharf was tapping a pen against her chin, her large ocelot ears swiveling independently as she listened to some fast-paced rock music bleeding from her headphones. 

Judy pushed the door open. 

Leni jumped slightly, pulling her headphones down around her neck. "Hopps? What are you doing down here? I thought you were banished to the asphalt."

"I was. I'm back on patrol," Judy said, stepping into the lab and letting the door click shut behind her. She kept her voice low. "How are things in the cave?"

"Crowded," Leni grumbled, gesturing vaguely upward toward the bullpen. "Those ZIA suits have been down here twice already, taking inventory. They're treating my lab like it's a crime scene. Pompous, bureaucratic hacks." She paused, studying Judy's face. The ocelot's eyes narrowed. "You look... different. Less dead inside. What's going on?"

"Nothing," Judy lied, walking casually past a row of evidence boxes. "Just needed to stretch my legs. Hey, whatever happened to that vintage tech we pulled from the Dune Runners raid? The green circuit board?"

Leni frowned, walking over to a secure filing cabinet. "Oh, that piece of junk? The suits haven't flagged it yet because it was tagged before Wilde's official... you know. It's still in the secondary queue." She pulled open a drawer and lifted out a clear plastic evidence bag containing the small, green board. A preliminary analysis report was stapled to the front. 

"Can I see it?" Judy asked. 

Leni hesitated. She looked at the door, then back at Judy. She was an analyst, but she wasn't stupid. She knew exactly what Judy was risking by asking for evidence connected to a ZIA investigation. 

Leni sighed, a small smirk playing on her lips. "I was just about to take my fifteen-minute break. It would be a real shame if someone were to misplace a piece of unclassified evidence while I was getting a coffee."

She placed the bag on the metal counter, right in front of Judy. 

"I won't be long," Judy said softly. 

"Don't get caught," Leni replied, turning her back and walking out of the lab. 

The moment the door closed, Judy snatched the bag and the report. She shoved them deep into the inner pocket of her jacket, right next to the USB drive. 

She finally had the pieces. Now, she just needed to build the puzzle.

---

The heat in Judy’s apartment was stifling. The ancient window AC unit rattled and hummed, pushing around warm, stale air without actually cooling anything. 

Judy sat cross-legged on the hardwood floor of her living room, wearing a loose, faded gray t-shirt. An empty mug of coffee sat near her knee, surrounded by a chaotic sea of printed paper.

She hadn't slept. She didn't think she could, even if she tried.

For the past four hours, she had been systematically breaking down the raw patrol logs she’d stolen from Clawhauser's terminal. She cross-referenced them with the city grid, plotting the locations of every "random" act of vandalism and theft that Wolford had mentioned.

She stood up, her joints popping in the quiet room. She walked over to the blank corkboard hanging above her tiny dining table. She grabbed a handful of pushpins and a spool of cheap red yarn she’d bought at a craft store on the way home.

*Tundratown power relay. Pin.*
*Rainforest District utility transfer hijack. Pin.*
*Savanna Central water control sub-station. Pin.*

Judy stepped back, her violet eyes scanning the board. If you looked at them as isolated incidents, they were just gang violence. But when she connected them with the red yarn, a pattern emerged. They weren't hitting banks. They weren't hitting jewelry stores. They were probing water, power, and dispatch routing. They were touching the nervous system of the city. 

"They're not just smashing things," Judy murmured, wiping a bead of sweat from her forehead. "They're testing response times. They're mapping blind spots. And they're learning where the city can be rerouted without anyone noticing."

She walked back to the coffee table and picked up the plastic evidence bag containing the green circuit board. Beside it lay the preliminary analysis report Leni had let her take.

Through the plastic, Judy noticed something she hadn't seen in the dim light of the warehouse. Scratched deeply into the corner of the green fiberglass was a tiny, stylized logo—a gear with two letters etched inside it: *J.W.* 

It meant nothing to her, just another manufacturer's mark. She set it down and read the highlighted section of Leni's report again. 

*...device is constructed using archaic, decades-old analog components, yet contains a highly sophisticated, localized bypass architecture. Not designed for data theft. Designed to passively mimic an authorized 'handshake' protocol with Cerberus-class municipal firewalls, allowing user to remain undetected in the system indefinitely.*

Judy stared at the words. *Cerberus-class municipal firewalls.*

A ghost from the past drifted into the stifling apartment. A memory of sitting in a cruiser during a stakeout, drinking terrible coffee. Nick had been complaining about the city's new security upgrades.

*"Cerberus is a joke, Carrots," he had drawled, leaning his seat back. "Three layers of authentication? Please. If I wanted to get past Cerberus, I wouldn't try to break the door down. I'd just build a fake ID badge that keeps pinging the system with a 'Hello, I belong here' handshake every three seconds. The system gets bored and stops checking. It's a classic ghost-loop."*

Judy looked down at the green circuit board in her paw. 

*A ghost-loop.*

Nick hadn't been afraid of this circuit board because it was "vintage tech." He had been terrified of it because he recognized the methodology. He recognized the code. 

A cold clarity washed over her, chilling the sweat on the back of her neck. 

Suddenly, everything made a twisted, painful kind of sense. The puzzle pieces clicked together, forming a picture that Judy's grieving heart desperately wanted—and needed—to believe.

Nick wasn't the architect. He was used.

He had recognized the tech in that warehouse three months ago. The people who built it—this network the ZIA was so terrified of—they must have known who he was. They knew he was a former hustler with intimate knowledge of the ZPD's blind spots. 

Judy looked at the photos of the stolen infrastructure keys from the harbor explosion. 

"She used him," Judy whispered. 

The white fox. The vixen from the rain. She must have found Nick. She must have used his past against him—blackmailed him, or manipulated him—forcing him to steal the Chief's keys because only a cop could get that close. And once he handed the keys over at the docks... they didn't need him anymore. 

*They may have blown the warehouse to cover their tracks and silence their only loose end.*

Judy's paws began to shake, but not from grief. The crushing, heavy apathy that had defined her existence for the last three months evaporated, burned away by the white-hot inferno of absolute, righteous fury. 

Nick hadn't betrayed her because he wanted to. He had been a victim of the very predators he used to run with. They had stolen his knowledge, stolen his life, and left him to burn while they used his methods to tear the city apart.

And the ZIA was going to bury it all in a classified file cabinet. 

"Not on my watch," Judy said. Her voice was no longer empty. It was lethal.

She walked up to the corkboard. She grabbed a thick red marker. In the very center of the board, right in the middle of the web of red yarn, she drew a large circle. 

Inside the circle, she wrote two words in bold, heavy letters:

**WHITE FOX**

She didn't have a badge. She didn't have the ZIA's resources. But she had something they didn't. She had a motive. 

She was going to find the fox who killed her partner. And she was going to tear her empire to the ground.
