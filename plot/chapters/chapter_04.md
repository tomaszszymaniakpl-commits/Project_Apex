# CHAPTER 4: Ghosts in the Rain

The morning briefing had been a blur of parking enforcement updates and Bogo grumbling about budget cuts for the coffee machine.
Now, the bullpen was settling into its usual rhythm. Phones rang, keyboards clattered, and the scent of stale donuts fought a losing war against the smell of wet fur drying under the fluorescent lights.
Judy sat at her desk, typing a report. Or rather, pretending to type. Her eyes were fixed on the reflection in her monitor, watching the fox at the adjacent desk.
Nick was leaning back in his chair, tossing a stress ball in the air. Catch. Toss. Catch. He looked bored. He looked normal.
Judy took a deep breath. Showtime.

"You were right, Nick," she said, keeping her voice casual.

The stress ball paused in mid-air. Nick caught it, turning to face her. "Right about what?"

"About Zoo-York," Judy said. She turned her chair, locking eyes with him. "I sent the confirmation email to Bogo this morning. I'm taking the rotation."

For a heartbeat, Nick didn't move.
Then, his shoulders dropped. It was a subtle movement - a release of tension Judy hadn't even realized he was holding. The tight lines around his eyes eased.

"You did?" he asked, his voice lighter than it had been all week. "That's... that's fantastic, Carrots. Seriously."

"I figure three months isn't that long," she lied, watching him closely. "And like you said, the networking opportunities are too good to pass up."

"Exactly." Nick grinned, sitting up straight. The salesman mask was back, but the tension had drained from his shoulders. "You're going to run circles around those feds. They won't know what hit them. When do you leave?"

"Day after tomorrow."

"Perfect. We should-"

"OH. MY. GOSH."

A tray of donuts slammed onto Judy's desk, followed immediately by Benjamin Clawhauser, who looked like he was about to vibrate out of his uniform.

"Did I hear Zoo-York?" the cheetah gasped, his eyes wide. "Judy, you have to - I mean, you simply must - go to Prancy's Pastries. They have bagels the size of your head. Well, my head. Your head is smaller. But still. Bagels."

Nick chuckled, leaning back and snatching a chocolate sprinkle donut from the tray. "Easy, Spots. Let the bunny breathe. She hasn't even packed yet."

"I'm just saying." Clawhauser grabbed a napkin, wiping a crumb from his cheek. "It's so exciting. Although..." His ears drooped slightly. "It's going to be awfully quiet around here without you, Judy. Who's going to keep Nick from selling the office supplies?"

"I resent that," Nick mumbled through a mouthful of donut. "I only lease them."

Judy laughed, but her mind was racing. Nick was happy. Not just supportive—relieved she was leaving. The realization stung, sharp and cold, but she pushed it down. She had a trap to spring.

The heavy oak door to Bogo's office creaked open on the mezzanine above.

"Chief on deck," Clawhauser whispered, hurrying back toward the front corridor on foot.

Nick and Judy immediately buried their noses in their files.

When the coast was clear, Judy looked up.
"Hey, since I'm leaving so soon," she started, keeping her tone light. "Bogo wants the Dune Runners evidence fully cataloged before I go. It's a two-mammal job. I was thinking we could knock it out tonight. Order pizza, stay late?"

She waited. Come on, Nick. Be normal. Say yes.
Nick stopped chewing. He swallowed hard.

"Ah," he said, wiping his paws on a napkin. He didn't look at her. He looked at the clock on the wall. "Tonight? Tonight is... tricky."

"Tricky?"

"Yeah. I've got a... thing." He scratched the back of his neck. "I've got a check-in. Paperwork thing downtown. Can't move it."

Judy stared at him. A check-in. Last minute. With no notice.
It was a lazy lie. Insultingly lazy.

"Oh," Judy said, forcing a smile that felt like it might crack her muzzle. "Right. Paperwork is sacred. Don't worry about it. I can handle the inventory myself. I'm a fast typer."

Nick relaxed instantly. "You're a lifesaver, Fluff. I owe you one."

"Yeah," Judy whispered as he turned back to his computer. "You do."

The ZPD basement smelled of warm electronics, stale recycled air, and spicy corn chips.
It was a different world down here. The hum of servers replaced the chatter of the bullpen, and the lighting was a permanent, soothing blue.
Judy walked past rows of humming server racks until she reached the "nest"—a chaotic corner of desks piled high with monitors, cables, and empty energy drink cans.

Leni Scharf, a petite ocelot with oversized glasses and headphones around her neck, was currently spinning in her chair, tossing popcorn into the air and trying to catch it.

"Missed," Judy noted as a kernel bounced off Leni's nose.

Leni yelped, nearly tipping the chair over. She scrambled to regain her balance, adjusting her glasses.
"Hopps," she breathed, clutching her chest. "Sweet cheese, don't sneak up on a predator like that. My instincts almost made me delete the entire traffic database."

"Sorry, Leni." Judy smiled, leaning against the doorframe. "Working hard, I see?"

"Compiling encryption protocols is 90% waiting and 10% panic." Leni shrugged, grabbing a bag of Flaming Hot Beetle Chips. She offered the bag to Judy. "Want one? They burn your tongue off. It's great."

"I'm good, thanks." Judy walked into the room, looking at the screens. Leni had a talent for multitasking - one screen showed code scrolling like a waterfall, another showed a live feed of a traffic intersection, and a third was paused on a level of Super Animal Crossing.

"So." Leni crunched a chip loudly. "To what do I owe the pleasure? Did you lock yourself out of your email again? Because I told you, 'password123' is not secure."

"Actually," Judy said, her voice dropping a notch. "I need a favor. A specific one."

Leni stopped chewing. She spun her chair around to face Judy, her expression shifting from bored techie to wary gatekeeper. "Define 'specific.'"

"I need you to ping a phone. GPS location. Real-time."

Leni narrowed her eyes behind her thick lenses. "Okay. Do you have a warrant? Or a Bogo-signed authorization form?"

"Not... exactly."

"Then no." Leni turned back to her screens. "I like my job, Hopps. Bogo will mount my head on his wall if I run unauthorized traces. He has a spot picked out and everything."

"It's Nick's phone," Judy said quietly.

Leni froze. She slowly spun back around. Her ears twitched.
"Wilde?" She looked at Judy, really looked at her. She saw the tension in the rabbit's shoulders, the lack of sleep in her eyes. "Why do you need to track your partner? Did he get kidnapped? Is he in trouble?"

"I don't know," Judy admitted. "That's why I need to find him. He's... lying to me, Leni. He says he's at a check-in right now."

Leni glanced at the clock. "At five-fifteen? Who schedules a paperwork check-in at rush hour?"

"Exactly."

Leni sighed, taking off her glasses and rubbing her eyes. "Look, Judy. I get it. Trouble in paradise. But if I ping a fellow officer's phone without cause, that's immediate suspension. I can't do it."

Judy pushed off the doorframe. She walked closer to the desk. She needed to do this. She hated it, but she needed it.

"That's a shame," Judy said, her voice dripping with exaggerated disappointment. "Because James Wolford is upstairs right now."

Leni's head snapped up. "So?"

"So," Judy leaned against the doorframe, straightening her sleeve. "I was just chatting with him. And I was thinking about telling him how much you admire his... what was it you said during the SWAT cross-training? His 'majestic tactical posture'?"

Leni's fur puffed up instantly. She looked like a bottlebrush. "You wouldn't."

"I might," Judy continued, stepping toward the door. "In fact, I think I'll go ask him about his posture right now. OFFICER WOLFORD-"

"SHHH." Leni launched herself out of the chair, scrambling over the desk and clamping a paw over Judy's muzzle. "Are you crazy? Keep your voice down."

Judy gently pulled Leni's paw away, raising an eyebrow. "Do we have a deal?"

Leni glared at her. She looked at the door, then back at Judy. She groaned, a sound of pure defeat.
"You are evil," Leni hissed, retreating to her keyboard. "Tiny, fluffy, and evil. I respect it, but I hate you right now."

"I love you too, Leni."

The ocelot's fingers flew across the keyboard. "Okay, okay... bypassing the privacy shield... handshake with the tower... and... got him."

A map of Zootopia appeared on the main screen. A red dot blinked rhythmically.

"He's not at the check-in," Leni noted dryly.

"No," Judy stared at the screen. "He's in the Rainforest District. Lower canals."

"That's the middle of nowhere," Leni frowned. "What is he doing down there?"

"I'm going to find out," Judy said. She memorized the cross-streets, checked them against the map on her own phone, and turned to the door. "I owe you one, Leni. Seriously."

"You owe me a bag of chips," Leni called after her. "And if Wolford asks, I hate his posture."

Judy didn't answer. She was already running for the elevator.

The climate transition hit Judy like a physical wall.
One moment, she was in the air-conditioned, sterile pod of the SkyTram. The next, the doors slid open at the Rainforest District transfer station, and the air turned into hot soup.
Ninety percent humidity. Thirty degrees Celsius.

Judy stepped onto the wooden platform, her nose twitching as the heavy, wet air coated her lungs. It smelled of forced growth - rotting mulch, blooming jasmine, and the metallic tang of the massive sprinkler systems hissing high above in the canopy.
Artificial rain. It was constant here. A fine, warm mist that clung to everything.

She pulled up the hood of her black civilian jacket, checking her phone. Leni's red dot was pulsing steadily.

Target: Stationary. Sector 4. Canal Level.

"Got you," she whispered.

She didn't take the main elevator. She took the maintenance stairs, winding down the massive, artificial tree trunk that supported the station. The deeper she went, the darker it got. The neon lights of the upper canopy faded, replaced by bioluminescent moss and flickering, moisture-damaged streetlamps.

Her heart was hammering a frantic rhythm against her ribs.
Why are you here, Nick?

She ran through the scenarios in her head.
Gambling debt? He'd mentioned poker once. Maybe he owed the wrong mammals.
An old hustle gone wrong? Maybe someone from his past was threatening to expose him.
A confidential informant? Maybe he was working a case off the books, trying to be a hero, trying to prove he didn't need help.

"You idiot," she muttered, slipping on a patch of wet moss. She caught herself on the railing, her paws damp. "You don't have to do this alone."

She reached the bottom level. The Canal District.
Here, the roar of the water was omnipresent. Waterfalls cascaded from the upper levels, feeding the dark, sluggish canals that wound through the roots of the district. The mist was thicker here, turning the world into a soft-focus dreamscape of deep greens and shadows.

The red dot on her screen merged with her blue dot.
50 meters.

Judy slowed down. She moved off the main boardwalk, stepping carefully onto the service gantry that ran underneath the gondola line. The wood was slick, silent under her paws.
She peered through the curtain of hanging vines.

Ahead, a small wooden bridge arched over a drainage canal. A single, sodium-yellow streetlamp buzzed overhead, casting a cone of sickly light into the gloom.
And there he was.
Nick.

He was pacing. His raincoat was soaked, the dark fabric clinging to his frame. He wasn't looking at his phone. He was scanning the shadows, his ears pinned flat, his tail wrapped tight around his leg.
Judy's chest tightened. She instinctively reached for her belt - not for a weapon, but to reassure herself. She was here. She was going to step out. She was going to say, Nice night for a walk, Slick, and watch the relief wash over his face. They would fix this. Whatever trouble he was in, they would fix it together.
She took a step forward.
Then, she froze.

A boat engine purred in the darkness of the canal.
It wasn't a police boat. It was a sleek, unmarked skiff, drifting silently out of the fog. It bumped gently against the wooden piling of the bridge.
A figure climbed out.

Judy ducked back behind the vines, her breath hitching. The contact. The threat.
The figure was cloaked in a heavy gray poncho, hood pulled low. They moved with a fluid, dangerous grace—no wasted motion. They stepped into the pool of yellow light, standing opposite Nick.

Nick stopped pacing. He stood paralyzed, his whole body rigid.

"Nick," Judy breathed, her muscles coiling, ready to sprint, ready to intervene if the stranger made a move.

The stranger reached up.
Judy braced herself for a weapon. A knife. A gun.
Instead, the figure pulled back the hood.

Judy's world tilted on its axis.
It wasn't a thug. It wasn't a mob boss.
It was a vixen. Arctic.

Even in the dim, yellow light, she was breathtaking. Her fur was a pristine, luminous white that seemed to glow against the grime of the district. Her eyes were striking - ice-blue, sharp, and intense.
She didn't look like a criminal. She looked like something out of a dream. Or a nightmare.

"Who..." Judy's thought trailed off.

The vixen didn't speak. Or maybe she did, and the roar of the waterfalls swallowed it. She took a step toward Nick.
Nick didn't back away. He didn't raise his paws in defense.

The white fox reached out. She peeled off her wet gloves, dropping them to the muddy wood. Then, she raised both paws and cupped Nick's muzzle.

It was a gesture of such profound, desperate intimacy that Judy felt like she had been slapped.

Nick's eyes slipped closed. The tension that had been radiating off him for days - the fear, the distance, the wall - shattered. He leaned into her touch. He slumped forward, resting his forehead against hers, his paws coming up to grip her waist, pulling her closer, as if she were the only thing keeping him upright.

It wasn't the body language of a victim. It wasn't the body language of a debtor.
It was the body language of a drowning man finding air.

Judy stood frozen behind the vines, the damp wood seeping cold into her knees.
The puzzle pieces slammed together in her mind, forming a picture she had refused to see.

The text on the train. A secret message.
The hushed phone calls. Lovers' quarrels.
The "check-in." A rendezvous.
The enthusiasm for Zoo-York. Getting the partner out of the way.

"He wasn't protecting me," Judy whispered. "He was hiding from me."

She watched Nick whisper something to the white fox. She watched the way his thumbs brushed over her arms, the way he looked at her with a mixture of pain and adoration that Judy recognized.
Because she had looked at him that way in the barn.

He hadn't been scared of criminals. He hadn't been in trouble. He had just moved on.
He had found someone who moved in the same weather he did. Someone she could never mirror.

The vixen pulled back slightly, her paws still on his shoulders, looking deep into his eyes. It looked like a private world. Two foxes inside it, and no room for anyone else.

Judy Hopps didn't belong there. She was just a rabbit with a badge and a foolish crush.

A sob rose in her chest, hot and sharp. She clamped both paws over her muzzle to stifle it, tears mixing with the mist on her cheeks.
She backed away. One step. Two.
The wood creaked under her paw.

Down on the bridge, Nick's ears swiveled. He turned his head sharply toward the shadows where she stood.
"Hello?" he called out, his voice sharp.
His voice carried alert, not recognition.

Judy didn't wait. She turned and ran.
She scrambled up the maintenance stairs, slipping on the wet metal, scraping her shins, ignoring the pain. She ran until her lungs burned, until the yellow light and the two foxes were swallowed by the darkness and the roar of the water.

She made it to the station platform before her legs gave out.
She sat on a metal bench, soaked through, shaking. The warm mist of the Rainforest District clung to her fur, but she felt nothing except the cold spreading through her chest. She pressed her paws against her knees and stared at the empty tracks.

The first sob came without warning. It doubled her over, squeezing the air from her lungs. She bit down hard, grinding her teeth together, but it didn't matter. Another followed. And another.
A pangolin waiting for the next tram glanced over, then looked away.

Judy Hopps sat on the bench and cried until the platform lights blurred into streaks of meaningless color. When the tram finally arrived, she boarded without wiping her eyes. She rode home with her reflection staring back at her from the dark glass - a small, soaked rabbit staring at an answer she couldn't survive and couldn't unsee.
