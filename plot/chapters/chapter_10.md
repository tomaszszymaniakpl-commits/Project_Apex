# Chapter 10: The Iron Proof

The air conditioning in Cruiser 27 had surrendered somewhere around noon, leaving the cabin smelling faintly of hot plastic and wet wolf fur.

Judy sat in the passenger seat, staring blankly through the heat distortion shimmering off the asphalt of Sahara Square. The clipboard in her lap felt heavy. Another day, another stack of parking citations. Purgatory with a badge.

"I'm just saying," Wolford grumbled from the driver's seat, wiping sweat from his muzzle with the back of his paw, "if the city can afford new tactical gear for Delgato’s SWAT unit, they can afford some freon for the patrol cars. I feel like a hot dog in a microwave."

Judy offered a thin, mechanical smile. "Roll the window down, James."

"It's hotter outside, Hopps. It’s physics." Wolford reached for a crumpled paper bag between the seats, pulling out a slightly squashed pastry. "Bear claw? I got two from that bakery on Vine. The guy feels sorry for us. Or me, mostly."

"No, thanks. I’m fine."

Wolford paused, holding the pastry halfway to his mouth. He looked at her—not with pity, but with that quiet, steady canine observation that Judy had come to rely on over the last two months. He was the only one who didn’t treat her like she was made of glass. "You didn't sleep again, did you?"

"I slept," Judy lied smoothly. The red strings on her apartment wall flashed in her mind.

Before Wolford could press the issue, the radio spat static. 

*“Dispatch to any available unit in Sector 4. We have a silent alarm trip and a 10-31 in progress at Savanna Central Water Control Sub-Station 12. Utility security reports lost feed. Priority response requested.”*

Wolford paused. The sub-station was just three blocks east. He looked at Judy. The apathy was instantly gone from her eyes, replaced by a sharp, sudden focus.

Wolford grabbed the mic. "Dispatch, Car 27 is on site. Two minutes out." He dropped the radio, threw the pastry onto the dashboard, and slammed the sirens on. "Hold on."

The cruiser tore through the sun-baked streets, taking a sharp left into the industrial district. When they pulled into the service yard of Sub-Station 12, the dust had barely settled. It was dead quiet. No getaway vehicles, no sirens, no shouting. Just the low mechanical hum of municipal pumps cycling behind reinforced concrete walls.

They drew their weapons and approached the control annex. The heavy steel service doors hadn't been blown up—they had been systematically bypassed. The electronic lock panels were cleanly melted, precise and professional.

"Clear," Wolford muttered, sweeping the entrance.

Inside, the control hall was dim and eerily silent. Judy’s ears swiveled, catching a muffled thumping sound behind a bank of pipe manifolds. They moved in, flashlights cutting through the gloom.

They found the private security guards—a rhino and two rams. They weren't dead. They weren't even bleeding. They were zip-tied to a structural pillar, blindfolded, and gagged with industrial tape. 

Wolford holstered his weapon and pulled out his combat knife, quickly cutting the rhino free. "ZPD. You're safe. Who hit you?"

The rhino ripped his blindfold off, gasping for air. "I don't know! They came out of nowhere. No faces, tactical gear, night vision. They didn't even yell. Just dropped us in seconds."

Judy swept her flashlight over the control racks and open service cabinets. "What did they hit?"

"Flow-control relay modules from the primary manifold cabinet," the ram choked out, rubbing his wrists. "And they patched something into the pressure routing board in Control Room B. They were in and out in under six minutes. No shouting, no panic. They were ghosts."

Judy stepped away from the guards, walking toward Control Room B. Wolford followed her a moment later.

"Relay hardware," Wolford whispered, his voice low. "Hopps, look at the security cameras."

Judy looked up. Every single lens in the room had been painted over with a quick, single burst of aerosol foam. The local telemetry junction box on the wall was open, its wires neatly clipped. No collateral damage. No wasted movement.

"This wasn't a street gang," Wolford said, his brow furrowing. "This is military-grade. The timeline alone... to isolate a utility node, strip relay modules, patch a board, and vanish before we got here? They knew the response windows perfectly."

It was too clean. Judy’s nose twitched. Criminals always left something. A scuff mark, a dropped casing, a footprint. She dropped to one knee, her flashlight sweeping the concrete floor near the maintenance trench beside the pressure manifold.

Shadows stretched long against the floor. And there, wedged deep in a structural crack in the concrete, almost invisible unless you were exactly her height and looking for it—was a small piece of reinforced paper.

Judy pulled it free. It was half-burned, but not entirely. A black-market utility transfer tag. It had a partial barcode, a faded contractor logo, and a fragment of printed text: *RLY-MNF // SC-12*.

It was completely out of place. This crew was too precise to drop a transfer tag. It looked almost as if someone had deliberately shoved it into the crack, shielding it from the quick-burn cleanup.

"Hopps!" Wolford called out from the entrance. 

Judy quickly slipped the tag into her pocket just as the screech of tires echoed through the yard. Three black SUVs slammed to a halt outside the bay doors. The doors opened in unison, and a dozen mammals in dark suits and body armor poured out, forming a perimeter.

Jack Savage stepped out of the lead vehicle. He wasn't wearing his suit jacket today, just a tactical vest over a white shirt. His cold, striped ears were pinned back.

"Stand down, officers," Jack announced, his voice cutting through the warehouse like ice. "This scene is now under the jurisdiction of the Zootopia Intelligence Agency."

Wolford stepped forward, bristling. "We just got here. We haven't even processed the vics yet."

"And you won't," Jack replied smoothly, not even looking at the wolf. His eyes locked onto Judy. "I thought you were confined to traffic enforcement, Officer Hopps. I suggest you return to it before I have to make a phone call to your Chief."

Judy stared at him. The sheer arrogance of the rabbit made her blood boil, but she kept her face completely neutral. She felt the weight of the tag in her pocket. 

"Let's go, James," Judy said quietly. "We're done here."

Wolford looked like he wanted to argue, but he nodded. As they walked back to the sweltering cruiser, Judy didn't look back at Savage. The air conditioning was still broken, but for the first time in weeks, Judy didn't feel the heat. 

She had a thread.

---

By the time Judy and Wolford got back to Precinct One, the sun had shifted from punishing white to molten orange, baking the front steps and turning every metal surface into a branding iron.

The bullpen smelled like printer toner, stale coffee, and wet uniforms.

Wolford dropped into the chair beside her desk with a grunt and started typing his incident report with two claws at a time, like he was trying to punch the keyboard into confession.

"Flow-control relays, pressure board patch, cameras foamed, telemetry clipped," he muttered, eyes on his screen. "You know what I like about this city? Absolutely nothing."

Judy was already halfway through her own report, line by line, clean and procedural.

No adjectives.  
No theories.  
No anger.

*Sub-Station 12.*  
*Utility breach.*  
*Unknown suspects.*

Wolford leaned back and rubbed his face. "You gonna tell me what you pocketed?"

Judy didn't look up. "Evidence I didn't have time to log before ZIA took the floor."

"Mm." He studied her profile for a second. "You trust me enough to tell me what it is?"

She stopped typing for just a beat.

"Not yet."

He nodded once, strangely unoffended. "Fair."

A voice called from the duty desk.

"Judy?"

Clawhauser stood near the main terminal, headset crooked over one ear, holding a clipboard to his chest.

"Chief Bogo wants you in his office. Now."

Judy's jaw tightened.

Of course.  
Savage had complained already.

She stood, capped her pen, and slid her report into the pending tray.

"If I'm not back in ten, tell Wolford to water my plant."

"You don't have a plant," Wolford said.

"Exactly."

She crossed the bullpen without looking left or right and climbed the short hall to Bogo's office. She knocked once, then stepped inside.

"Close the door," Bogo said.

Judy closed it.

Then Bogo stood, walked past her, and pulled the blinds down himself. One by one.

Judy folded her arms. "If Savage called you, tell him to file it with dispatch. We did our job."

"It's not Savage."

"Then why are the blinds down, Chief?"

"Because what I'm about to say never leaves this room."

She stopped.

"I didn't call you up here to discipline you, Hopps," Bogo said. "I called you because before I show you what I need to show you, I have to tell you something I should've told a long time ago."

Judy's ears angled forward despite herself.

Bogo exhaled through his nose, then lowered himself into the chair as if it suddenly weighed twice as much.

"Twenty years ago, I was a sergeant. Rain coming down so hard it sounded like nails on the cruiser roof. I knocked on a door past midnight, and a vixen opened it while I stood there to tell her her husband had been murdered."

He stared at the blinds for a moment.

"I've seen grief in this job. I've seen rage, denial, shock. What I saw in her face that night was worse. It was the exact second someone understands the world is never going to be what it was ten minutes ago."

His jaw tightened.

"I told her we'd find who did it. I said it with a badge on my chest and certainty in my voice. I believed it when I said it."

A beat.

"I pushed that case until the paper bled. Then the machine started grinding it down. Requests delayed. Leads reassigned. Resources pulled."

His jaw flexed.

"We didn't get justice. The case was buried. Deprioritized. Wrong species. Wrong politics. Wrong timing. Pick your excuse."

"I signed off on silence. I wore that silence for twenty years."

He looked back at Judy.

"I let the memo stand when the case was moved out of active rotation. Told myself it was temporary. Told myself I'd bring it back when the pressure eased."

Another beat.

"It never did."

His voice dropped.

"That husband was John Wilde."

Silence.

"And the night I looked Marian Wilde in the eye and gave her my word... was the night I started teaching this city how to break it."

Judy stared at him, confused. "Chief... why are you telling me this now?"

Bogo reached into a sealed folder on his desk.

"Because I needed you to hear the truth from me before you saw this."

He slid a single photo across the desk.

Judy didn't move at first. Then she stepped forward and picked it up.

Grainy telephoto. Industrial yard. Night timestamp in the corner from three days earlier.

Nick.

Alive.

No smoke. No fire. No grave.

Alive, standing shoulder to shoulder with a white arctic fox, a caracal, and a massive reptile.

Judy's paw tightened so hard the photo bent at the corners.

There was no drag mark, no gun to his head, no panic in his stance. Nick was there by choice.

"ZIA surveillance captured that frame seventy-two hours ago," Bogo said. "I've had it since then."

Judy kept staring.

"Vesper Nyx. High-value assassin. Infrastructure sabotage specialist."

A beat.

"Ragnar Drakov. War-crime profile. Terror support. Regime-change contractor."

"Multiple jurisdictions. Bodies in every file."

He pointed to the white fox. "Unidentified."

Judy still said nothing.

"Within twenty-four hours, Wilde gets a formal national warrant," Bogo said. "After that, media dump. This photo goes public. I couldn't stop it, and I couldn't keep this from you forever. I didn't want you learning it from a television crawl."

He leaned forward.

"Once that warrant activates, all four names move to the Black Warrant List."

The words sat in the room like a loaded weapon.

"Rules of engagement change," Bogo continued. "No tranq-first doctrine. No verbal warning requirement under active-threat criteria. Authorized units shoot to neutralize."

He didn't blink.

"And with Drakov in that group... they probably will."

Judy set the photo down very carefully.

No expression. No visible reaction.

Only stillness.

"I'm telling you because I know you," Bogo said quietly. "You're going after him no matter what I say."

Judy finally spoke, voice low and sharp. "You had seventy-two hours."

"Yes."

"You let me keep thinking he was dead."

"I had no legal lane for classified ZIA intel."

"You built legal lanes when they protected the badge."

"Hopps—"

"No." She stepped closer, jaw tight. "If Wilde had a reason to burn this city, you handed it to him with both hands."

Bogo absorbed it like a blow.

He looked older than he had an hour ago.

"Bring him in alive," he said.

Judy went still again.

"It's the only debt I can still pay that doesn't come in a body bag."

For a second she looked like she might throw the photo in his face.

Instead she gave one short, humorless laugh.

"Now you care."

She stepped back from the desk. "You don't get to ask me for mercy after twenty years of procedure."

"Judy—"

"Save it, Chief."

She turned and yanked the door open hard enough for it to hit the stopper, then walked out without looking back.

The door slammed.

Silence swallowed the office.

A shadow moved in the far corner near the filing cabinets.

Lt. Nadine Fangmeyer stepped into the light, arms folded, expression set in stone.

Bogo didn't look surprised.

"Nadine."

"Chief."

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Bogo rubbed a hand over his face.

"How far gone is she?"

Fangmeyer's voice stayed level. "Worse than yesterday. Not yet unrecoverable."

Bogo stared at the closed door.

"Stay on her."

Fangmeyer nodded once. "I already am."

"Don't let her hunt alone."

"I won't."

"Whatever she's about to do," Bogo said quietly, "make sure she lives long enough to finish it."

Fangmeyer met his eyes.

"Yes, Chief."

Nadine didn't move.

"Chief... there's something else."

Bogo looked up. "What?"

"The DNA report from the harbor. Someone tampered with it."

His face hardened. "You understand what that means."

Nadine held his gaze.

"Yeah."

---

The door to Bogo's office slammed so hard the glass in the bullpen windows rattled.

Heads turned.

Judy didn't care.

She moved like a live wire through the room, ears pinned, jaw locked, eyes fixed on the equipment wall. She snatched her tactical vest off the rack, shoved one arm through, then yanked open a drawer for her spare sidearm mags. Not procedure. Not protocol. Pure momentum.

Clawhauser looked up from the duty console, startled. "Judy? You okay?"

She didn't answer.

Wolford stood halfway from his chair. "Hopps-"

"Don't," she snapped, not even looking at him.

She clipped her radio on, grabbed her keys, and headed for the garage access door at the end of the corridor.

Fast. Purposeful. Wrong.

A broad striped paw hit the wall panel before she reached it. The security door refused to open.

Judy spun.

Fangmeyer stood between her and the exit, one forearm braced across the frame. Not aggressive. Not theatrical. Just immovable.

"Move."

"No."

"Move, Lieutenant Fangmeyer."

"Not while you look like this."

Judy took one step forward, voice dropping to a blade. "He's on Black Warrant in less than a day. You know what that means."

"I do."

"Then get out of my way."

Fangmeyer didn't blink. "In this state, you're not going to catch terrorists. You're going to hand them gifts."

Judy laughed once, sharp and ugly. "A lecture? Right now?"

"A mirror," Fangmeyer said. "You don't want justice tonight. You want impact."

"Damn right I do."

"You want revenge."

Judy's breath hitched for half a second. Barely visible. Then her mouth hardened again.

"Every minute we stand here is another minute someone else puts a round in his chest."

"Or yours," Fangmeyer said. "You kick down a door alone tonight, one of two things happens. You kill someone you can't unkill, or you die in a hallway and become tomorrow's briefing slide."

Judy's claws tightened around her keys so hard the metal bit her palm.

"Either you hunt like an amateur, and end up in the morgue," Fangmeyer said quietly.

A beat.

"Or you command like a cop."

Silence stretched between them. Fluorescents hummed overhead. Somewhere in the bullpen, a phone rang, got picked up, and died.

Judy looked away first.

When she spoke, the fury was still there, but cracked down the middle with cold honesty.

"If I leave right now," she said, voice low, "I'm going to do something I can't take back."

Fangmeyer nodded once. "Good. That's the first smart thing you've said in five minutes."

Judy let out a breath that sounded like it hurt.

"Mini reset," Fangmeyer said. "Ten minutes. Air. Facts. Then we move."

Judy wiped a hand across her face. "We?"

"I'm not letting you go alone," Fangmeyer said.

"Where?"

"My place first," Fangmeyer said. "Trust me."

---

They did not leave right away.

There was still over an hour left on shift, and optics mattered. Judy finished the bare minimum paperwork with hands that would not stop shaking. Fangmeyer kept her visible, busy, and out of armories. Wolford pretended not to watch and watched anyway.

At end-of-shift, they moved.

Not through the main hall. Through records storage, down old maintenance stairs, past a disused locker corridor where the cameras had been broken for years and never replaced.

At the rear service lot, Fangmeyer keyed open a side gate and cut across to a detached utility building at the edge of the block.

She knocked twice. Pause. Once.

The roll-up door rattled and climbed.

Judy stopped dead.

Inside, under hard white workshop lights, was not Fangmeyer's apartment.

It was Leni's garage.

Tool walls. Welding bench. Half-disassembled signal repeater on a stand. Three city maps pinned over a scarred corkboard. And parked center bay like a loaded argument: Leni's black, low-profile pursuit motorcycle, bodywork stripped for speed and silent handling.

Wolford was already there, sleeves rolled up, laying out compact breaching charges and mechanical entry tools on a cloth mat with surgical neatness.

McHorn stood near the far wall, arms folded across his chest, solid as poured cement.

Leni looked up from a laptop bristling with cable adapters. "Took you long enough."

Judy stared at all of them, stunned clean through her anger.

"What is this?"

Fangmeyer stepped in beside her. "What it should've been from the start."

Wolford spoke without looking up. "Before you ask, yes, I stole evidence-grade putty from the training locker. No, I'm not sorry."

McHorn gave Judy one short nod. "You going to stand there all night, or are we doing this?"

Judy swallowed hard. "Why?"

Leni snorted softly. "Because if you run solo, you get dead."

Wolford added, "And because I'm not letting federal suits decide how this story ends."

McHorn's jaw flexed. "This is our case too. Wilde was one of us."

The words landed heavy.

Judy looked from one face to the next, like she didn't quite trust what she was seeing.

Fangmeyer walked to the board and tapped a blank section with a marker. "No badges in this room. No paperwork. No hero runs."

"Start from the top," Leni said. "What do you have that we don't?"

Judy reached into her pocket, pulled out the half-burned transfer tag, and laid it on the workbench.

RLY-MNF // SC-12.

"Sub-Station breach was not random," Judy said. "Pressure board patch, relay pull, clean exit. Same signature as the other utility hits."

She tapped the code fragment.

"This ties to Rainforest utility subcontract chains. And Bogo just confirmed Nick is alive, active, and within twenty-four hours of full national warrant status. Black Warrant."

No one spoke.

"I'm done waiting for ZIA to end this with a bullet and call it procedure," she said. "We find the white fox first. We find the network around her. Then we find him."

Fangmeyer nodded once. "Tasking."

Judy's command voice clicked back online, still edged with steel but finally under control.

"Wolford, with me. Tomorrow first light we work the street. No names, no uniforms, no heroics."

Wolford nodded. "Understood."

"Fangmeyer, McHorn. Run parallel lead checks during shift. Utility access logs, subcontract movement, quiet pressure on informants and old contacts."

McHorn: "Done."

Fangmeyer: "We'll keep it clean."

"Leni, digital only for now," Judy said. "Map links between water, power, and dispatch routing. Stay passive tonight. Map networks, watch for bleed, no active interception yet."

Leni gave a thin smile. "I can try. No promises on speed."

"Clawhauser stays inside Precinct One, routine-facing," Fangmeyer added. "He tracks warrant traffic, evidence pulls, and any unusual IA/ZIA requests."

"Can he do that without burning himself?" Judy asked.

Leni nodded. "If he stays boring, yes. If he gets clever, no."

Judy stood very still for a beat, then drew one long breath and let it out.

"Rules," she said.

No one interrupted.

"From this point on, we move as a unit. No solo runs. No ego plays. We gather, verify, move, extract. We are off-book and we have zero jurisdiction, so we stay invisible and we do not collide with ZIA unless we choose to."

She looked at Fangmeyer, then at the black warrant photo burned into the back of her skull.

"Primary objective is live capture. No one in this room gives ZIA an excuse to close this with body bags."

Wolford tilted his head. "And if possible becomes impossible?"

Judy's eyes hardened. "Then we survive long enough to make sure they don't win."

She picked up the marker and wrote one word across the top of the board in heavy block letters:

HUNT.

Then she underlined it once.

"No more ghosts," she said.

Her gaze swept the room, landing on each of them.

"We hunt as a pack."
