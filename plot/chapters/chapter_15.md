# Chapter 15: The Missing Piece

The garage was quiet. The usual hum of Leni’s servers seemed muted, swallowed by the heavy, oppressive atmosphere that had settled over the Strike Force. 

It was 3:00 AM. 

Judy stood in front of the corkboard. She had stripped off her blood-stained tactical vest and was wearing a plain grey t-shirt. She wasn't pacing. She wasn't shaking. She stood perfectly still, her violet eyes locked on the chaotic web of red string, photos, and evidence tags. 

The fury that had consumed her on the street outside her apartment had burned down to a cold, hard ember. 

"Talk to me, Leni," Judy said, her voice steady and completely devoid of emotion. "Who knew where we were going to be tonight?"

Leni typed a rapid sequence into her terminal, bringing up a complex, color-coded matrix on the main screen. 

"I built a query-to-incident correlation model," Leni explained, her voice tight with exhaustion. "I tracked every time someone accessed our duty logs, our radio frequencies, or our vehicle GPS trackers, and cross-referenced it with the timeline of the attacks."

Wolford leaned against the workbench, his arms crossed, his yellow eyes fixed on the screen. "And?"

"And we have a leak," Leni said flatly. "A bad one. Before the hit on Sub-Station 12, someone pulled the patrol routes for Sector Four. Before we hit the decoy warehouse, someone queried the Blackwood Freight lease records. And tonight..." 

Leni swallowed hard, looking away from the screen for a second. "...tonight, someone pinged the GPS on Nadine's personal vehicle exactly twelve minutes before the shot was fired."

McHorn let out a low, dangerous rumble from the back of the garage. "Who?"

"I don't have a name," Leni said, bringing up two distinct data clusters. "The queries were routed through heavily encrypted proxy servers. But I narrowed down the access vectors. It's coming from two places. One is a high-level clearance terminal inside Precinct One. The other is an inter-agency cross-access node."

"ZIA," Wolford growled. "Savage."

"Or someone in our own house," McHorn countered heavily. 

Judy didn't turn around. She kept her eyes on the board. "It doesn't matter who it is."

Wolford frowned. "Judy, we have a mole feeding our locations to a sniper. It matters."

"It matters for the trial," Judy said, her tone slicing through the room like a scalpel. "Right now, it's just an operational hazard. We have a leak. We assume everything we say on the radio or type into a precinct terminal goes straight to them. We operate around it."

She finally turned to face them. Her posture was rigid, her expression unreadable. The rabbit who had cried in the street was gone. The commander of the Strike Force had taken her place. 

"Let's do triage," Judy ordered, walking over to the workbench and spreading out the physical evidence they had managed to collect. "What do we actually know, and what are we guessing?"

She tapped the half-burned transfer tag. "Evidence one. They're moving relay hardware."

She pointed to Leni's screen. "Evidence two. The Cerberus ghost-loop. They have a digital skeleton key to the city's infrastructure."

She moved to the ledger. "Evidence three. Blackwood Freight. They have the logistics to move heavy tonnage without leaving a paper trail."

Finally, she picked up the two scraps Wolford had pulled from the fire at the Annex: the burned map fragment and the shipping sticker. 

"Evidence four and five," Judy said, laying them flat on the table. "A partial location code, *SYN-RND-C4*, and a serial number for a piece of hardware they cared enough to evacuate before ZIA breached the building."

She looked at the team. 

"They have the tools, they have the transport, and they have the code. But we are missing the center of the board," Judy said, her eyes narrowing. "Why? What are they building?"

"We don't know," Wolford admitted quietly. "But forensics might give us a thread. I dropped the dart and the balistics data with a tech I trust. He owes me. He said he'd run it off the books."

"Call him," Judy said. "Wake him up if you have to. We don't sleep until we have the missing piece."

***

Wolford stepped away from the workbench, holding his burner phone to his ear. He listened intently for two full minutes, his brow furrowing deeper with every passing second. He offered a low grunt of thanks and ended the call. 

"That was forensics," Wolford said, turning back to the group. "The tech ran the dart and the sweep from the roof."

Judy didn't move. "Give it to me."

"It wasn't a standard rifle," Wolford reported, his voice grim. "Pneumatic firing mechanism. Silent, high-velocity, completely custom. No brass casings, no gunpowder residue. The shooter was a professional."

"We knew that," McHorn said, crossing his arms. "Did they leave anything behind?"

"No DNA, no hair, no fibers," Wolford said. "But the tech found something else near the chimney stack where the shooter set up. Microscopic traces of a specialized industrial lubricant. It's a high-viscosity polymer used almost exclusively for maintaining heavy-duty, high-pressure utility valves."

Leni looked up from her terminal. "The kind of valves you'd find in a municipal water sub-station."

"Exactly," Wolford nodded. "The shooter didn't just buy that lube at a hardware store. They've been handling the stolen infrastructure equipment recently enough to track it onto the roof."

Judy looked at the half-burned transfer tag pinned to the board. *RLY-MNF // SC-12.* "The same crew that hit the sub-station took the shot at Nadine. What about the dart itself?"

Wolford’s expression darkened. "The tech managed to isolate a microscopic sample of the fluid from the needle tip. It's a synthetic neuro-inhibitor. Highly unstable, incredibly complex. He said it's lab-grade. You can't cook this stuff up in a bathtub."

"Military?" McHorn asked. 

"Or corporate," Leni suggested, her fingers flying across her keyboard. "That kind of chemical synthesis requires a sterile environment and millions of dollars in equipment."

Judy stared at the board, the pieces finally snapping together in her mind. The cold logic of it was terrifying. 

"They have access to our secure police logs," Judy said, her voice a low, steady hum. "They use military-grade chemical weapons. They have the logistics to move tons of hardware without a trace, and they have a digital skeleton key that can bypass the city's central dispatch."

She turned to face her team, her violet eyes hard as flint. 

"This isn't a street gang," Judy stated. "This isn't even a syndicate. We're looking at a state-level operation. A private army."

She walked back to the workbench and picked up the shipping sticker Wolford had sliced off the empty crate in the burning Annex. 

"They have everything they need," Judy said, holding the sticker up. "Except for whatever was in this box. They risked exposure to evacuate it. It's the only thing that matters to them."

She dropped the sticker onto Leni’s keyboard. 

"Leni. Run that serial number," Judy ordered. "I don't care how deep it's buried. Find out what they took."

***

Judy stared at the corkboard. 

She looked at the stolen relay hardware. She looked at the Cerberus ghost-loop pattern. She looked at the Blackwood Freight logistics routes, and finally, at the sniper's bullet that had nearly killed Nadine. 

The pieces were all there. They had been staring her in the face for days. 

"They're not terrorists," Judy whispered, the realization washing over her like a bucket of ice water. 

Wolford looked up. "What?"

"Terrorists want to break things," Judy said, stepping closer to the board, her eyes tracking the red strings connecting the different utility sectors. "They want to cause panic. They blow up a sub-station and claim responsibility. But these people... they're stealing hardware, not destroying it. They're testing response times, not causing permanent blackouts. They're mapping the grid."

She turned to face the team, the final piece of the puzzle locking into place. 

"They don't want to destroy the city's infrastructure," Judy said, her voice echoing in the quiet garage. "They want to take control of it."

McHorn let out a low whistle. Wolford just stared at her. 

"Is that even possible?" Judy asked, turning to Leni. "Can someone just hack the whole city?"

Leni shook her head immediately. "No. Zootopia's grid is deliberately decentralized. The water pumps in Canal District run on a completely different architecture than the power grid in Sahara Square. To take control of the whole city, they'd have to physically breach fifty different master nodes simultaneously. It's a logistical nightmare."

"But they have the logistics," Wolford pointed out. "Blackwood Freight."

"Even with the trucks, the software wouldn't talk to each other," Leni insisted, rubbing her tired eyes. "You'd need a central hub. Something that could force all those different systems to synchronize."

Silence fell over the garage. 

Leni froze, her paws hovering over her keyboard. Her brow furrowed deeply. 

"Wait," Leni murmured, staring blankly at her screen. "When I was at the academy... I read a paper in the *Zootopia Engineering Quarterly*. It was an old theoretical model. Someone proposed building exactly that. A central hub to unify the city's infrastructure to prevent cascading blackouts."

"Find it," Judy ordered. 

Leni’s fingers flew across the keys, diving into the university's digital archives. "The city council killed the idea because it created a single point of failure. If someone took the hub, they took the city. They called it... Project Genesis."

She hit a final key. A scanned, yellowing PDF document popped up on the main monitor. 

The title read: *Project Genesis: The Master Synchronization Core.*

Leni started to read the abstract, but her voice died in her throat. She stared at the screen, her eyes widening in absolute shock. 

"Leni?" Judy asked, stepping closer. "What is it?"

Leni didn't speak. She just reached out and turned the monitor so Judy could see it. 

Judy’s eyes scanned the title, dropping to the byline just below it. 

*Lead Systems Engineer: Dr. John Wilde.*

The air rushed out of Judy’s lungs. The name hit her like a physical blow. 

*John Wilde.* Nick's father. 

"Sweet cheese and crackers," Wolford breathed, reading the name over her shoulder. 

"Run a background check on him," Judy ordered, her voice suddenly tight. "Now."

Leni pulled up the public records. "Dr. John Wilde. Employed by SyncPoint Research, a private municipal contractor. He headed the Genesis division twenty years ago. The project was abruptly canceled due to 'unspecified security concerns'."

"And what happened to Dr. Wilde?" McHorn asked quietly. 

"He died," Judy answered before Leni could read it. She remembered the photo Bogo had shown her. She remembered the Chief's confession about the buried case. "Three months after the project was canceled. A hit-and-run. The driver was never found."

She looked back at the board, the entire conspiracy suddenly snapping into a horrifying, personal focus. 

"They killed him," Judy whispered. "They killed John Wilde because he built something that gave absolute power over the city. And Bogo buried the case because the people who ordered the hit were too powerful to touch."

"And now they're back," Wolford realized, his yellow eyes darkening. 

"If he built a prototype of this Master Synchronization Core," Judy said, her mind racing, "they wouldn't have destroyed it. They would have kept it. Archived it."

"Leni," Judy snapped, pointing at the burned map fragment on the table. "The code. *SYN-RND-C4*. Check SyncPoint Research's property records."

Leni typed furiously. "SyncPoint went bankrupt fifteen years ago, but their parent company still owns their real estate. They have a decommissioned Research and Development facility on the edge of the Canal District. Building designation: C4."

Judy looked at the team. The exhaustion was gone, replaced by a terrifying clarity. 

"They aren't stealing random hardware," Judy said, her voice dropping to a cold, hard edge. "They're gathering the pieces to plug the Core back in. They're going to the Canal District to get the key to the city. The key Nick's father built."

McHorn crossed his massive arms. "So we hit the R&D facility. Before they can move it."

"Yes," Judy said, her violet eyes locking onto the map. "We find out exactly when they're making their move. And we burn them to the ground."

***

Director Sterling, Head of the Zootopia Intelligence Agency, did not like being interrupted before his morning coffee. 

The honey badger sat behind a massive mahogany desk on the top floor of the ZIA headquarters, his thick, scarred arms crossed over his chest. He glared at Agent Moss, who was standing perfectly still on the other side of the desk, holding a thick manila folder. 

"This had better be important, Moss," Sterling growled, his voice like grinding stones. "I have a briefing with the Mayor in twenty minutes, and half the city's infrastructure is currently bleeding hardware."

"It's about the infrastructure case, Director," Moss said smoothly. He stepped forward and placed the folder on the desk. "Specifically, it's about the lead investigator."

Sterling frowned, looking at the name printed on the tab. *SAVAGE, J.*

"Jack?" Sterling let out a short, dismissive snort. "Savage is the best operative in this building. He's closed more high-profile cases than you've read about. What is this?"

"It's a pattern of obstruction, sir," Moss said, his tone perfectly respectful, masking the venom underneath. "I've been conducting a routine compliance audit on his recent operations. The data is deeply concerning."

Sterling opened the folder, his eyes scanning the top sheet. He didn't look happy, but he looked more annoyed with Moss than with Jack. "Summarize."

"Agent Savage has completely locked down his informant network. He refuses to log his field sources. He actively blocked the ZPD from sharing their initial findings on the harbor explosion," Moss listed off, ticking the points on his claws. "Furthermore, the financial audits show massive discrepancies. He's been burning through black-budget discretionary funds for years, funneling money to 'assets' that don't exist on any agency registry."

Sterling frowned, flipping to the financial summary. "Jack runs deep-cover assets. That requires off-book funding."

"It requires oversight," Moss corrected gently. "And yesterday, during the raid on the Cliffside Power Annex, he engaged in a completely unnecessary jurisdictional argument with local police outside the building."

"Tactical friction happens," Sterling muttered, flipping a page. "Jack is protective of his cases. You know that."

"It gave the targets exactly three minutes to escape out the back," Moss countered softly. "A highly organized criminal syndicate, cornered in a warehouse, and they slip away without a single shot fired by ZIA personnel. Because Savage was busy yelling at a local detective."

Sterling stopped reading. He looked up at the moose. "You're reaching, Moss."

"I'm looking at the timeline, sir," Moss said, his dark eyes locking onto the Director's. "The evidence suggests Agent Savage isn't investigating this syndicate. He's protecting them. He's cleaning their tracks. He's a mole."

Sterling stared at the file for a long, heavy moment. He closed it with a sharp *smack*. "I'm not going to ruin my best agent's career over a compliance audit and a bad tactical call. But I will ask him to explain himself. Let's go."

Two floors down, Sterling and Moss walked down the quiet, sterile corridor of the senior operations wing. Two heavily armed ZIA tactical agents trailed a discreet distance behind them—standard protocol when a Director moved through the building, but their presence suddenly felt heavier. 

Sterling didn't knock. He hit the override code on the keypad, and the glass door to Jack's office hissed open. 

Jack Savage was standing in front of his open wall safe. He was shoving several unmarked drives into a black tactical duffel bag. 

Sterling stopped in the doorway. The annoyance on his face vanished, replaced by a cold, hard shock. 

Jack slowly zipped the duffel bag closed and turned around. He didn't look surprised. He didn't look guilty. He just looked at them with cold, flat eyes. 

"Going somewhere, Jack?" Sterling asked, his voice dropping to a dangerous rumble. 

"Field work, Director," Jack said smoothly, picking up the bag. "The situation is fluid."

"It's about to become static," Sterling said, stepping into the room. He kept his tone measured, giving Jack the benefit of the doubt. "Agent Moss has raised some concerns regarding your handling of the infrastructure thefts. I need you to walk me through your tactical decisions yesterday."

Jack looked at Sterling, then at Moss. "I secured the perimeter and prevented local law enforcement from contaminating a federal crime scene. Standard procedure."

"And while you were securing the perimeter, the targets evacuated," Moss interjected, stepping up beside the Director. "You gave them a three-minute head start, Savage."

"I don't answer to you, Moss," Jack said coldly. 

"You answer to me," Sterling said, his patience fraying. "Let's talk about the harbor explosion. Why did you block the ZPD from sharing their initial forensics with the federal database?"

"The local forensics were compromised," Jack replied without missing a beat. "I was maintaining the integrity of the evidence chain."

"And the financial discrepancies?" Moss pressed, his voice silky. "Years of black-budget funds funneled to unregistered assets. Who are you paying, Savage?"

"I am paying the people who get results," Jack said, his eyes narrowing slightly. "Assets who would be dead in a week if I put their names on a server you have access to."

"Jack, tell me what's going on," Sterling demanded, his voice rising. "Give me your source logs and your unredacted operational timeline. Right now. Let's clear this up."

Jack stood perfectly still. "I can't do that, sir. Operational security. Revealing those sources would compromise an ongoing infiltration."

"I am the Director of this agency," Sterling growled. "You don't have operational security from me. Give me the files, Jack. Tell me you aren't working for them."

"I'm working for the city," Jack replied. 

Moss offered a microscopic, triumphant smile. "He's stalling, Director. He's burned."

Sterling stared at Jack, the last shred of trust evaporating in the face of Jack's blatant refusal. The honey badger let out a heavy, disappointed sigh. 

"Hand over your badge and your weapon, Jack," Sterling ordered, his voice echoing with finality. "You're suspended pending a full treason tribunal."

"You're making a mistake, sir."

"Take him," Sterling ordered the tactical agents. 

Jack slowly raised his paws in the air, stepping away from the desk. "Alright. Easy."

Moss stepped forward, pulling a pair of heavy-duty cuffs from his belt. "Turn around, Savage. Hands behind your back."

Jack turned slowly. Moss reached out to grab his wrist. 

Jack moved faster than the eye could track. 

He didn't hit Moss with his fists. He dropped his center of gravity, pivoting sharply, and drove his elbow directly into the moose's solar plexus. The impact forced the air from Moss's lungs with a wet gasp. Before Moss could fold, Jack grabbed the heavy tactical duffel bag off the desk and swung it in a brutal arc, smashing the solid mass directly into the side of Moss's neck. 

The moose collapsed like a felled tree. 

"Gun!" one of the tactical agents shouted, drawing his weapon. 

Jack didn't try to fight them. He kicked his heavy mahogany desk, sending it sliding across the polished floor on its casters, slamming into the knees of the two agents and knocking them off balance. 

Sterling roared, lunging forward, but Jack was already moving. He vaulted over the overturned desk, slipped past the stumbling agents, and sprinted out the door into the main corridor. 

Seconds later, the harsh, pulsing wail of the ZIA lockdown alarm echoed through the building. Red emergency lights bathed the office in a bloody glow. 

Moss groaned, pushing himself up onto his hands and knees, rubbing his bruised neck. He looked up at Director Sterling, who was staring at the empty doorway in absolute shock. 

"I told you, sir," Moss rasped, a dark smile twisting his muzzle. "He's running back to his handlers."
