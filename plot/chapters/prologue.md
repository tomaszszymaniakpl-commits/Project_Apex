# PROLOGUE: The Promise

## P.1

The storm hammered against the roof like a fist, drowning out the world. Inside the small house, the air was warm and smelled faintly of chamomile and drying fur.

Then the lights came.

They slashed through the front curtains—violent strobes of red and blue that turned the hallway into a wound. The vixen froze in the kitchen doorway, a towel in her paws. Her pulse kicked hard against her ribs.

She crossed to the door, unlocked it, and opened the wood to the wind.

The storm rushed in.

Standing on the small porch was a mountain of a mammal. A Cape Buffalo, young but massive, his cape uniform soaked through, water cascading off the brim of his cap. He filled the entire frame, blocking out the wind, blocking out the street.

The vixen looked up at him. Rain dripped from his muzzle. She smelled the ozone of the storm and the sharp, metallic scent of fear clinging to him.

"Where is he?" she whispered. She already knew. She was asking anyway, because the alternative was a sound she wasn't ready to make.

The buffalo opened his mouth. Nothing came out. He closed it again and looked down at his hooves.

The sound came anyway. It tore out of her—a single, broken sob that she tried to catch with both paws, pressing them hard against her muzzle. It wasn't a scream. It was worse than a scream. It was the sound of something fundamental snapping inside her.

Behind her, the stairs creaked.

Two kits stood halfway down the stairs, bathed in the flashing police lights. The older one, a red fox boy of perhaps ten, stood with his ears flattened against his skull. His emerald eyes were wide and unblinking, fixed on the buffalo. He wasn't looking at his mother. He was looking at the uniform.

Clinging to his paw was a much smaller kit. She rubbed sleep from her eyes, looking between the giant at the door and her mother, confused by the sound Mama was making. She squeezed her brother's paw, not understanding.

The boy's grip tightened. The light in his green eyes didn't flicker; it went still.

The buffalo saw them. His stoic expression fractured. He looked at the vixen, then down at the mud on the porch. Slowly, agonizingly, he sank to one knee. The wood of the porch groaned under his weight. He lowered his massive head until he was eye-level with the trembling vixen.

"Mrs. Wilde..." His voice was a deep rumble, cracking with something that wasn't rain. "I am so sorry."

"Don't," she breathed. "Don't be sorry. Find who did this."

The buffalo held her gaze. The rain hammered his back. He leaned in, and when he spoke, it was with a fierce, desperate intensity—not a promise from an officer, but from a mammal who meant every word.

"We will find who did this, Mrs. Wilde. You have my word."

He held her gaze for a second longer, then stood up, the floorboards complaining under his hooves. He pulled his cap down tight and turned back into the drowning night without looking back.

The door swung shut, clicking softly, locking the storm out and the silence in.

## P.2

The buck checked his watch. He tapped a polished hoof against the steering wheel, staring through the rain-streaked windshield.

"Nineteen minutes," he muttered to the dashboard. "For a notification."

The passenger door was yanked open. The roar of the storm flooded the cabin for a split second before the door slammed shut, cutting it off again.

The buffalo dropped into the passenger seat, the suspension groaning in protest. He brought wet fur, rain, and grief into the sterile bubble of the cruiser. Water dripped from his cape, staining the upholstery dark. He stared straight ahead, chest heaving, gaze hollow.

The buck put the car in gear.

"Well?" the buck said, his voice flat. "Done?"

"She saw it in my face," the buffalo whispered. His hooves shook in his lap. "We need to call in Forensics. Lock down the perimeter. If we start canvassing now—"

"Canvassing." The buck let the word hang, as if tasting something sour. "In THIS neighborhood. At two in the morning."

"There could be witnesses—"

"There are no witnesses, rookie. There are never witnesses. Not here." The buck adjusted his mirror, checking his antlers out of habit. "You know what this district gives us? Silence. Every time."

The buffalo turned to him slowly. "A mammal is dead."

"A fox is dead," the buck corrected. He wasn't angry. He wasn't loud. He was simply empty. "In the labyrinth. No witnesses. No leads. No one downtown is going to authorize overtime for this."

"He had a family. Two kids."

"And we have a city to run." The buck pulled the gearshift. "File the report. Write it clean. And don't let the rain get to you, rookie. It makes you sentimental."

"Drive," the buffalo said.

The buck drove.

In the side mirror, the small house with the grieving widow and the two orphans shrank into the distance, swallowed by the relentless, uncaring rain.

## P.3

Three months later, the rain was gone, but the weather inside the house had not changed. In its place was a flat, suffocating gray afternoon that pressed against the windows of the kitchen.

The house was quiet. The only sound was the tinny, cheerful hold music coming from the telephone receiver pressed against the vixen's ear. It played a synthetic melody that sounded like a mockery in the empty room.

The red kit stood in the hallway shadow. He was leaner now, his ribs showing slightly through his fur. His ears were swivelled forward, twitching at every sound.

At the table, the vixen twisted the phone cord around her paw, tighter and tighter. She looked diminished, as if the last three months had eroded her from the inside out.

The music stopped. A voice crackled on the line. Dry. Bureaucratic.

"The case has been... reprioritized."

The vixen flinched. "Reprioritized?"

"We'll be in touch if anything comes up, ma'am."

"He was murdered," she whispered.

Click.

The vixen lowered the receiver to the cradle. She rested her forehead on the kitchen table and let out a long, shuddering breath.

The boy watched her. A cold weight settled in his chest, heavy and permanent.

His gaze shifted to the other chair at the table. The smaller kit was there. She was humming quietly to herself, oblivious to the phone call, her tongue poking out between her teeth in concentration. She was coloring.

She pushed the paper across the table toward the empty spot where their father used to sit.

It was a crayon drawing. A fox in a blue uniform. A gold star on his chest. A hero.

The boy looked at the drawing. Then he looked at his mother, broken and small in the gray light.

The emerald fire in his eyes went out.

He walked into the kitchen. He didn't go to his mother. He walked past her to the little one.

He put a paw on her shoulder.

She looked up, beaming, holding up the drawing. "For Daddy," she chirped. "When he comes back."

The boy looked at the drawing. He looked at the lie.

"Yeah," he said, his voice flat and steady. "For Daddy."

He didn't correct her. He would never correct her. If the world was going to be this cold, he would be the fire that kept her warm, even if he had to burn everything else down to do it.
